## Introduction – The current financial reporting environment (paras 1.1-1.13)

<a id="s7-chap_01_navPoint_2"></a>
### Financial reporting today

 

<a id="s7-Para_1.1"></a>**1.1** Recent years have seen major changes in financial reporting worldwide. International financial reporting standards (IFRS® standards) or a local variant thereof are increasingly becoming mandatory throughout the world. The International Accounting Standards Board (the Board) has been strengthening relationships with securities regulators, standard-setters and others around the world in order to encourage consistent application of existing IFRS standards and to deepen co-operation over the future development of IFRS standards.

[FAQ 1.1.1 – How many countries use IFRS or IFRS for small and medium-sized entities (SMEs)?](07.md#s10-FAQ_1.1.1)

 

<a id="s7-chap_01_navPoint_3"></a>
### Global harmonisation – IFRS and US GAAP

 

<a id="s7-Para_1.2"></a>**1.2** The Board promotes global harmonisation. After an era of convergence, including joint working on the revenue and leasing standards, a mandatory change to IFRS standards is not expected in the US for the foreseeable future, although other methods of incorporating IFRS standards are supported.

<a id="s7-Para_1.3"></a>**1.3** The US Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB) and the Board have shifted attention to their individual agendas. The Board is moving to a more multi-lateral approach, working with standard-setters around the world (including the FASB), using the Accounting Standards Advisory Forum (ASAF).

<a id="s7-Para_1.4"></a>**1.4**The development of the IFRS for SMEs® standard by the Board continues to be adopted widely across the world. This Manual is for users of full IFRS standards and does not cover the IFRS for SMEs standard in detail.

 

<a id="s7-chap_01_navPoint_4"></a>
### The international financial reporting structure

 

<a id="s7-Para_1.5"></a>**1.5** The website of the Board sets out the current organisational structure in place for the development of IFRS standards and describes the duties of the various bodies involved in international standard-setting, governance and oversight and public accountability. These are the IFRS® Foundation (including the Board and the IFRS® Interpretations Committee), the IFRS® Foundation Trustees, the IFRS® Advisory Council, the Accounting Standards Advisory Forum (ASAF) and the IFRS® Foundation Monitoring Board.

[www.ifrs.org].

 

<a id="s7-chap_01_navPoint_5"></a>
### The IASB

 

<a id="s7-Para_1.6"></a>**1.6** The stated objective of the Board is “to develop, in the public interest, a single set of high quality, understandable, enforceable and globally accepted financial reporting standards based upon clearly articulated principles”. Further information on who the Board are and what they do, including the process for international accounting standard-setting and their future work plan, can be found on the Board's website.

[www.ifrs.org]

<a id="s7-Para_1.7"></a>**1.7** The Board publishes both IFRS standards and the IFRS for SMEs standard.

<a id="s7-Para_1.8"></a>**1.8** IFRS standards are designed for general purpose financial statements and other financial reporting of all profit-oriented entities. They can also be useful for non-profit-oriented entities. General purpose financial statements give information about financial performance, position and cash flow that is useful for making economic decisions by a range of users, including shareholders, creditors, employees and the general public.

<a id="s7-Para_1.9"></a>**1.9** IFRS standards apply from the date specified in the standard, and transition provisions should be applied when appropriate. The general policy of the Board is to apply new standards retrospectively, to all relevant transactions, whether they took place before or after the issue of the standard. This might have an effect on previously negotiated contracts or agreements; it is up to those who negotiate such contracts and agreements to make appropriate provisions for future changes. In some specific circumstances, the Board can specify that new standards are adopted prospectively. This is considered on a case-by-case basis.

<a id="s7-Para_1.10"></a>**1.10**The IFRS for SMEs standard is a separate standard that can be applied to the general purpose financial statements and other financial reporting of small and medium-sized entities, which are defined as entities that (1) do not have public accountability and (2) publish general purpose financial statements for external users.

<a id="s7-Para_1.11"></a>**1.11** Key differences between IFRS standards and the IFRS for SMEs standard are:

- The omission of certain standards not relevant to SMEs (such as earnings per share, segment reporting and interim reporting).

- The simplification of measurement requirements in some areas.

- Reductions in disclosure requirements.

- Simplification of language.

[www.ifrs.org: About the IFRS for SMEs Standard].

 

<a id="s7-chap_01_navPoint_6"></a>
### The IFRS Interpretations Committee

<a id="s7-Para_1.12"></a>**1.12** The IFRS Interpretations Committee (IFRS IC, formerly the International Financial Reporting Issues Committee or ‘IFRIC’) addresses emerging issues that require attention before an international standard can be amended or issued. It is a committee of the IASB whose duties include the interpretation of IFRS standards, when conflicting or unsatisfactory interpretations have emerged, and the giving of timely guidance on issues that have not yet been addressed by IFRS standards. It does not issue stand-alone standards. Interpretations are, however, authoritative under IAS 1.

[www.ifrs.org].

<a id="s7-chap_01_navPoint_7"></a>
### Local adoption of IFRS

<a id="s7-Para_1.13"></a>**1.13** The Board’s standards apply automatically in some jurisdictions; but in many jurisdictions they need to go through an endorsement process before they are brought into effect.

## The IFRS accounting framework – concepts and principles (paras 1.14-1.63)

 

<a id="s8-Para_1.14"></a>**1.14** The concepts underlying IFRS accounting standards are set out in the Board’s Conceptual Framework for Financial Reporting issued in March 2018 (the Framework). Changes reflected in the March 2018 Framework address areas that caused problems in practice or that needed updating to reflect concepts developed by the Board in other projects. These include:

- updating the definition of assets, liabilities, income and expenses, including removal of the probability threshold;

- adding criteria for derecognition of assets and liabilities;

- updating the discussion of measurement for measurement bases;

- providing information regarding management’s stewardship;

- re-introducing the concept of prudence;

- introducing new sections on financial statements and the reporting entity, and on presentation and disclosure; and

- updating fundamental concepts (including unit of account, going concern and capital maintenance).

 

<a id="s8-chap_01_navPoint_10"></a>
### Framework for setting accounting standards

 

<a id="s8-Para_1.15"></a>**1.15** The main sections of the Boards’s Framework are:

- Status and purpose of the Conceptual Framework.

- The objective of general purpose financial reporting.

- Qualitative characteristics of useful financial information.

- Financial statements and the reporting entity.

- The elements of financial statements.

- Recognition and derecognition.

- Measurement.

- Presentation and disclosure.

- Concepts of capital and capital maintenance.

- Appendix – defined terms.

[Framework Table of Contents].

 

<a id="s8-chap_01_navPoint_11"></a>
### Status and purpose of Framework

 

<a id="s8-Para_1.16"></a>**1.16** The Framework reflects the concepts that underlie the preparation and presentation of financial statements for external users. Its purpose is to:

- Assist the Board in the development of future international accounting standards based on consistent principles.

- Assist preparers of financial statements in applying international accounting standards when no standard applies to a particular transaction or event, or when a standard allows a choice of accounting policy.

- Assist all parties in understanding and interpreting the standards.

[Framework SP 1.1].

<a id="s8-Para_1.17"></a>**1.17** The Framework is not a standard, and it does not override any standard.

[Framework SP 1.2].

<a id="s8-Para_1.18"></a>**1.18** The Framework deals with general purpose financial reporting that is useful to a range of users. It identifies the primary users as existing and potential investors, lenders, and other creditors who must rely on general purpose financial reports to obtain information. Other parties, such as employees, suppliers, customers, governments and their agencies, regulators and the public, might find general purpose financial reports useful. However, general purpose financial reports are not primarily directed to these parties.

[Framework SP 1.5]. [Framework SP 1.10].

 

<a id="s8-chap_01_navPoint_13"></a>
### The objective, usefulness and limitations of general purpose financial reporting

 

<a id="s8-Para_1.19"></a>**1.19** The Board identifies the objective of general purpose financial reporting as to “provide financial information about the reporting entity that is useful to existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors in making decisions relating to providing resources to the entity. Those decisions involve decisions about (a) buying, selling or holding equity and debt financial instruments, (b) providing or settling loans and other forms of credit, or (c) exercising rights to vote on, or otherwise influence, management’s actions that affect the use of the entity’s economic resources”.

[Framework SP 1.2].

<a id="s8-Para_1.20"></a>**1.20** Decisions about buying, selling or holding equity and debt financial instruments, providing or settling loans and other forms of credit by existing and potential investors, and exercising rights to vote on or otherwise influence management’s actions are dependent on the returns expected to be generated by those instruments (namely, dividends, principal and interest repayments and market price increases). Expectations about returns are dependent on an assessment of the amount, timing and uncertainty of future net cash inflows and stewardship of the entity’s economic resources.

[Framework para 1.3].

<a id="s8-Para_1.21"></a>**1.21** To make these decisions, users need information about the economic resources of the entity, claims against it, changes in the resources and claims, and how efficiently and effectively management and the governing board have used the entity’s resources.

[Framework para 1.4].

[FAQ 1.21.1 – What information is needed to assess the prospects for future net cash inflows, and why?](07.md#s10-FAQ_1.21.1)

<a id="s8-Para_1.22"></a>**1.22** The Framework identifies the primary users of general purpose financial reports (to whom the general purpose financial reports are directed) as those existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors who cannot require entities to provide information directly to them and, therefore, are obliged to rely on the general purpose financial reports for their information needs.

[Framework para 1.5].

<a id="s8-Para_1.23"></a>**1.23** General purpose financial reports are not designed to reflect the entity’s value. They provide information to assist existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors to estimate the entity’s value.

[Framework para 1.7].

<a id="s8-Para_1.24"></a>**1.24** The Framework establishes the concepts that underlie the estimates, judgements and models on which financial reports are based.

[Framework para 1.11].

 

<a id="s8-chap_01_navPoint_14"></a>
### Accrual accounting

 

<a id="s8-Para_1.25"></a>**1.25** Accrual accounting shows the effects of transactions, events and circumstances on the economic resources and claims in the period in which those effects occur, regardless of when the resulting cash receipts and payments take place.

[Framework para 1.17].

<a id="s8-Para_1.26"></a>**1.26** It is possible that income and expenses might be simultaneously recognised if that recognition arises from the changes in assets and liabilities, such as when the sale of goods for cash results in recognition of both income (from the recognition of one asset – the cash) and an expense (from the derecognition of another asset – the goods sold). However, this ‘matching’ of costs with income is not an objective of the Framework. The Framework does not allow recognition in the balance sheet of items that do not meet the definition of an asset, a liability or equity.

[Framework para 5.5].

 

<a id="s8-chap_01_navPoint_15"></a>
### Stewardship

 

<a id="s8-Para_1.27"></a>**1.27** Information about an entity’s economic resources, and claims to those resources, is useful to investors, creditors and other users to assist them in assessing management’s stewardship of the entity’s economic resources.

[Framework para 1.13].

<a id="s8-Para_1.28"></a>**1.28** To assess stewardship, useful information assists financial statement users in distinguishing between changes in the entity’s economic resources and claims resulting from financial performance versus changes resulting from other events such as issuing debt or equity. The information about past financial performance and stewardship can be helpful in predicting future returns.

[Framework para 1.15]. [Framework para 1.16].

<a id="s8-Para_1.29"></a>**1.29** In judging stewardship, financial statement users also consider how efficiently and effectively management discharged its responsibilities over the entity’s resources. Assessments of stewardship also require useful information to predict future cash flows and use of economic resources, in relation to how management is anticipated to contribute to achieving those results.

[Framework para 1.22]. [Framework para 3.2].

 

<a id="s8-chap_01_navPoint_16"></a>
### Qualitative characteristics of useful financial information

<a id="s8-Para_1.30"></a>**1.30** For financial information to be useful, it must be relevant and it must faithfully represent what it purports to represent. The usefulness of financial information is enhanced if it is comparable, verifiable, timely and understandable. Relevance and faithful representation are considered to be the fundamental qualitative characteristics of financial information. Neither a faithful representation of an irrelevant phenomenon nor an unfaithful representation of a relevant phenomenon helps users to make good decisions.

[Framework para 2.4]. [Framework para 2.5]. [Framework para 2.21].

<a id="s8-Para_1.31"></a>**1.31** Relevance suggests the ability to influence users’ economic decisions by helping or confirming the evaluation of events of the past, present or future. Financial information is considered to be capable of making a difference to users’ decisions if it has predictive value, confirmatory value or both.

[Framework para 2.6]. [Framework para 2.7].

<a id="s8-Para_1.32"></a>**1.32** Materiality is a subsidiary concept of relevance. See [paragraph 1.33](07.md#s8-Para_1.33).

[Framework para 2.11].

<a id="s8-Para_1.33"></a>**1.33**The concept of substance over form is embedded within the concept of representational faithfulness. The Framework recognises that, while the substance of an economic phenomenon and its legal form might be the same, there are many circumstances in which substance and legal form are not the same. In those instances, “providing information only about the legal form would not faithfully represent the economic phenomenon”.

[Framework para 2.12].

<a id="s8-Para_1.34"></a>**1.34** To be useful, information must not only present relevant effects of transactions and other events and conditions, but it must also faithfully represent the effects of transactions and other events and conditions that it purports to represent. To be a perfectly faithful representation, three characteristics would need to be present: (1) completeness, (2) neutrality, and (3) freedom from error. The Board’s objective is to maximise those qualities to the extent possible.

[Framework para 2.12]. [Framework para 2.13].

<a id="s8-Para_1.35"></a>**1.35** Completeness refers to the inclusion of all information necessary for a user to understand the transactions and other events and conditions being depicted, including all necessary descriptions and explanations. In some instances, completeness “may also entail explanations of significant facts about the quality and nature of the items, factors and circumstances that might affect their quality and nature, and the process used to determine the numerical depiction”.

[Framework para 2.14].

<a id="s8-Para_1.36"></a>**1.36** Neutrality refers to a lack of bias in the selection or presentation of financial information. The financial information is “not slanted, weighted, emphasised, de-emphasised or otherwise manipulated to increase the probability that financial information will be received favourably or unfavourably by users”. Neutrality does not mean information with no purpose or no influence on behaviour.

[Framework para 2.15].

<a id="s8-Para_1.37"></a>**1.37** Prudence is a subsidiary concept of neutrality. It is exercising caution when making judgements under conditions of uncertainty. The exercise of cautious prudence means that assets and liabilities are neither overstated nor understated. There is not a systematic need for more persuasive evidence to support recognition of assets and income versus liabilities and expenses. However, particular standards might contain asymmetric requirements to support information that is relevant and representationally faithful.

[Framework para 2.16]. [Framework para 2.17].

<a id="s8-Para_1.38"></a>**1.38** Faithful representation does not mean that the information is accurate in all respects. Free from error means there are no errors or omissions in the description of the effects of transactions and other events and conditions, and the process used to produce the reported information has been selected and applied with no errors in the process.

[Framework para 2.18].

<a id="s8-Para_1.39"></a>**1.39** Entities need to make reasonable estimates when monetary amounts cannot be observed directly. These estimates can be useful if clearly and accurately explained. A high level of uncertainty does not preclude use of an estimate, but it is important to consider whether other information would be more useful.

[Framework para 2.19]. [Framework para 2.20].

<a id="s8-Para_1.40"></a>**1.40** The Framework states that the most effective and efficient way to apply the fundamental qualitative characteristics (relevance and faithful representation) is to follow the three-step process:

- Identify an economic phenomenon that has information that can be useful to the users of the financial information.

- Identify the type of financial information about the phenomenon that would be most relevant.

- Determine whether that information is available, and whether it faithfully represents the economic phenomenon.

[Framework para 2.21].

<a id="s8-Para_1.41"></a>**1.41** In applying the fundamental qualitative characteristics of relevance and representational faithfulness, trade-offs might be necessary (for example, if information deemed highly relevant includes estimates with a high degree of measurement uncertainty). In some cases, recognition of the estimated amount might be useful, with appropriate accompanying information in the notes; in other cases, recognition using the highly uncertain estimate might not be appropriate, versus using another type of estimate and/or providing appropriate information in the notes.

[Framework para 2.22].

<a id="s8-chap_01_navPoint_17"></a>
### Enhancing qualitative characteristics

<a id="s8-Para_1.42"></a>**1.42** The Framework considers that the qualitative characteristics of comparability, verifiability, timeliness and understandability enhance the usefulness of information that is relevant and faithfully represented. These characteristics might also help to determine which of two ways should be used to depict a transaction or other event or condition if both ways can produce equally relevant and representationally faithful information.

[Framework para 2.23].

<a id="s8-Para_1.43"></a>**1.43** The first enhancing qualitative characteristic is comparability, over time and from one entity to another.

[Framework para 2.24].

<a id="s8-Para_1.44"></a>**1.44** The Framework does not give primacy to either comparability within an entity or comparability between entities. IFRS® 1 does make such a distinction in the context of first-time adoption of IFRS: comparability within an entity’s first IFRS financial statements is more important than comparability with entities already using IFRS.

<a id="s8-Para_1.45"></a>**1.45** Consistency must not be confused with comparability. “Consistency refers to the use of the same methods for the same items, either from period to period within a reporting entity or in a single period across entities. Comparability is the goal; consistency helps to achieve that goal”.

[Framework para 2.26].

<a id="s8-Para_1.46"></a>**1.46** The enhancing qualitative characteristic of verifiability helps to assure users that information faithfully represents the economic transactions and other events and conditions that it purports to represent. It means that “different knowledgeable and independent observers could reach consensus, although not necessarily complete agreement, that a particular depiction is a faithful representation”.

[Framework para 2.30].

<a id="s8-Para_1.47"></a>**1.47** Timeliness refers to having information available in time to be capable of influencing decisions. Information that is not provided on a timely basis is less useful to users in their decision-making.

[Framework para 2.33].

<a id="s8-Para_1.48"></a>**1.48** Classifying, characterising and presenting information clearly and concisely makes it understandable. Some phenomena are inherently complex and it is not possible to make them easily understandable. Reports excluding such information might be easier to understand; however, they would then be incomplete and, consequently, potentially misleading.

[Framework para 2.34]. [Framework para 2.35].

<a id="s8-Para_1.49"></a>**1.49** Applying the enhancing qualitative characteristics is an iterative process that does not follow a prescribed order. There might be trade-offs, such as a temporary reduction in comparability, when prospectively applying a new standard that enhances relevance and faithful representation. Disclosure might partially offset the temporary loss in comparability.

[Framework para 2.38].

<a id="s8-chap_01_navPoint_18"></a>
### Cost constraint

 

<a id="s8-Para_1.50"></a>**1.50** The cost of providing financial information that is both relevant and that faithfully represents what it purports to represent is a constraint on the provision of useful financial information. Because of the inherent subjectivity, different individuals’ assessments of the costs and benefits of reporting financial information vary, so the Board seeks to consider costs and benefits in relation to financial reporting in general terms and not solely in relation to individual reporting entities.

[Framework para 2.39]. [Framework para 2.43].

 

<a id="s8-chap_01_navPoint_19"></a>
### Materiality

 

<a id="s8-Para_1.51"></a>**1.51** As noted in [paragraph 1.29](07.md#s8-Para_1.29), materiality is a subsidiary concept of relevance. Materiality sets the threshold for determining whether an item is relevant, and is defined as follows: “Information is material if omitting it or misstating it could influence decisions that the primary users of general purpose financial reports make on the basis of those reports, which provide financial information about a specific reporting entity”. On 31 October 2018, the IASB issued amendments to the definition of ‘material’ in IAS 1 and IAS 8. [See chapter 4 para 37](10.md#s28-Para_4.37). “In other words, materiality is an entity-specific aspect of relevance based on the nature or magnitude, or both, of the items to which the information relates in the context of an individual entity’s financial report”.

[Framework para 2.11].

### Financial statements and the reporting entity

<a id="s8-chap_01_navPoint_idp1684609784"></a>
#### Financial statements

<a id="s8-Para_1.52"></a>**1.52** Financial statements (used interchangeably with ‘general purpose financial statements’, unless otherwise noted) are a subset of general purpose financial reporting. Financial statements provide information about economic resources of the reporting entity, claims against the entity, and changes in those resources and claims.

[Framework para 3.1].

<a id="s8-Para_1.53"></a>**1.53**Financial statements include the statement of financial position, statement(s) of financial performance, and other statements and notes.

[Framework para 3.3].

<a id="s8-chap_01_navPoint_idp1684620280"></a>
#### Reporting period

<a id="s8-Para_1.54"></a>**1.54** Financial statements provide information for a reporting period (a specified period of time). Comparative information for at least one prior reporting period is provided, to assist in identifying and assessing changes and trends.

[Framework para 3.4]. [Framework para 3.5].

<a id="s8-chap_01_navPoint_idp1684629112"></a>
#### Forward-looking information

<a id="s8-Para_1.55"></a>**1.55** Forward-looking information about possible future transactions and events is included in financial statements if it relates to the entity’s assets or liabilities or equity that existed at the end of the reporting period, during the reporting period, or to income or expenses for the reporting period, and is useful to users of financial statements. An example of forward-looking information is information about estimated future cash flows.

[Framework para 3.6]. [Framework para 2.4].

<a id="s8-chap_01_navPoint_idp1684623608"></a>
#### Events after the reporting period

<a id="s8-Para_1.56"></a>**1.56** Information about transactions and events occurring after the end of the reporting period (subsequent events) is included in the financial statements if providing that information is necessary to meet the objective of financial statements.

[Framework para 3.6]. [Framework para 2.2].

<a id="s8-chap_01_navPoint_idp1684631288"></a>
#### Going concern assumption

 

<a id="s8-Para_1.57"></a>**1.57** Financial statements are prepared on a basis that assumes an “… entity is a going concern and will continue in operation for the foreseeable future”. This is also addressed in IAS 1.

[Framework para 3.9]. [Framework para 4.33]. [Framework para 4.34].

 

<a id="s8-chap_01_navPoint_idp1684666776"></a>
#### The reporting entity

<a id="s8-Para_1.58"></a>**1.58** A reporting entity can be a single entity, portion of an entity, or include more than one entity, and is not necessarily a legal entity.

[Framework para 3.10].

<a id="s8-chap_01_navPoint_idp1684671000"></a>
#### Consolidated, unconsolidated, combined financial statements

 

<a id="s8-Para_1.59"></a>**1.59** Consolidated financial statements include financial statements of a parent (controlling entity) and subsidiary, as a single reporting entity.

[Framework para 3.11]. [Framework para 3.15].

<a id="s8-Para_1.60"></a>**1.60** Unconsolidated financial statements include parent-only financial statements. They can be useful information – for example, when claims against the parent do not give rise to a claim against the subsidiaries, or when equity claims against the parent are dependent on distributable reserves of the parent only. Alternatively, parent-only information can be provided in the footnotes to consolidated financial statements. A parent might be required, or choose, to prepare unconsolidated financial statements in addition to consolidated financial statements.

[Framework para 3.11]. [Framework para 3.17]. [Framework para 3.18].

<a id="s8-Para_1.61"></a>**1.61** Combined financial statements include financial statements of two or more entities that are not all linked by a parent-subsidiary relationship.

[Framework para 3.12].

 

<a id="s8-chap_01_navPoint_21"></a>
### Elements of financial statements

 

<a id="s8-Para_1.62"></a>**1.62** There are two main groups of elements. The first is associated with measuring an entity’s financial position: assets, liabilities and equity. The second group comprises those elements that relate to measuring an entity’s performance: income and expenses. Classification sorts assets, liabilities, equity, income or expenses based on shared characteristics for presentation and disclosure purposes, such as the nature or function of an item.

[Framework para 4.1]. [Framework para 7.7].

<a id="s8-Para_1.63"></a>**1.63** The Framework follows an asset-liability model; that is, the definitions and determination of income and expense rest on (are derived from) the definitions of (changes in) assets and liabilities.

[Framework para 4.7].

## Assets and liabilities (paras 1.64-1.108)

 

<a id="s9-Para_1.64"></a>**1.64** An asset is “... a present economic resource controlled by the entity as a result of past events”. Economic resources, in turn, are defined as “a right that has the potential to produce economic benefits”.

[Framework para 4.3]. [Framework para 4.4].

<a id="s9-Para_1.65"></a>**1.65** The three key components of the definition of assets – rights, potential to produce economic benefits, and control – are described in the Framework as follows:

- Rights are established. They can take many forms, and might correspond to an obligation of another party to transfer economic resources to the entity. Alternatively, rights might represent rights over physical objects, or rights to use intellectual property. In some cases, it might be uncertain whether a right exists, and the existence uncertainty might need to be resolved (for example, by a court ruling). Existence uncertainty is a consideration in measurement of elements.

[Framework para 4.6]. [Framework para 4.13].

- The right carries the potential to produce future economic benefits. Low probability of receiving future economic benefits does not necessarily preclude recognition, but it is a consideration in decisions about whether the asset is recognised and how it is measured.

[Framework para 4.14]. [Framework para 4.15]. [Framework para 4.17].

- The entity can control the economic resource. Control is the present ability to direct the use of an economic resource and to obtain the economic benefits that might flow from it. It also includes the present ability to prevent others from directing the economic resource and obtaining benefits from it.

[Framework para 4.19].

[FAQ 1.65.1 – Should an entity recognise held shares controlled by another party?](07.md#s10-FAQ_1.65.1)

<a id="s9-Para_1.66"></a>**1.66** Assets generally arise through the expenditure of cash; however, expenditure will not always result in an asset, even when it is expected that economic benefits will flow. This is generally because the entity making the expenditure will not control any expected benefits.

[Framework para 4.18].

[FAQ 1.66.1 – Do costs incurred meet the criteria for recognition as an asset?](07.md#s10-FAQ_1.66.1)  
[FAQ 1.66.2 – Do costs incurred result in the control of a resource?](07.md#s10-FAQ_1.66.2)  
[FAQ 1.66.3 – Treatment of an asset contributed by government](07.md#s10-FAQ_1.66.3)

<a id="s9-Para_1.67"></a>**1.67** The definition of a liability is “a present obligation of the entity to transfer an economic resource as a result of past events”.

[Framework para 4.26].

<a id="s9-Para_1.68"></a>**1.68** The three key criteria in the definition of liability are detailed as follows:

- A present obligation is established. Obligations are sometimes established by contract and legally enforceable. However, obligations can also arise from an entity’s customary practices, policies or statements; if there is no practical ability to avoid such obligations, they are referred to as ‘constructive obligations’.

[Framework para 4.29]. [Framework para 4.31].

- The obligation requires transferring an economic resource. Even if the probability of transfer of an economic resource is low, an obligation might still meet the definition of a liability. The low probability should be considered in recognition, measurement and related notes.

[Framework para 4.37]. [Framework para 4.38].

- The present obligation exists as a result of past events. This criterion is met if the entity has already obtained economic benefits or taken an action that will entitle it to receive economic benefits and, as a result of those past events, the entity is obligated to transfer an economic. A present obligation can exist even if the transfer of economic resources cannot be enforced until a future date.

[Framework para 4.43]. [Framework para 4.46].

### Unit of account

 

<a id="s9-Para_1.69"></a>**1.69** The unit of account is “the right or the group of rights, the obligation or the group of obligations, or the group of rights and obligations, to which recognition criteria and measurement concepts are applied”. Sometimes, one unit of account might apply for recognition, and another unit of account for measurement, such as when contracts are recognised individually but measured as part of a portfolio of contracts. Entities need to consider the level of aggregation or disaggregation for presentation and disclosure purposes, and the related information to be provided in the notes to the financial statements.

[Framework para 4.48]. [Framework para 4.49].

<a id="s9-Para_1.70"></a>**1.70** Sometimes, both rights and obligations can arise from the same source. If those rights and obligations are interdependent and cannot be separated, they form a single unit of account. Executory contracts are an example of this type of single unit of account. Treating a set of rights and obligations as a single unit of account differs from offsetting assets and liabilities.

[Framework para 4.53]. [Framework para 4.54].

 

<a id="s9-chap_01_navPoint_24"></a>
### Contracts

 

<a id="s9-Para_1.71"></a>**1.71** In faithfully representing the substance of contracts, entities should consider situations when a group or series of contracts might have been designed to achieve an overall commercial effect; in such instances, it might be necessary to treat the rights and obligations arising from that group or series of contracts as a single unit of account.

[Framework para 4.62].

 

<a id="s9-chap_01_navPoint_25"></a>
### Equity

<a id="s9-Para_1.72"></a>**1.72** Equity is a residual item (that is, the residual interest in the assets of the entity after deducting all its liabilities). However, it can be sub-classified into various types of capital and reserves. These can reflect legal restrictions or the differing rights of various owners.

[Framework para 4.63]. [Framework para 4.64]. [Framework para 4.65].

<a id="s9-chap_01_navPoint_26"></a>
### Recognition

 

<a id="s9-Para_1.73"></a>**1.73** Recognition is the process of capturing an item for depiction in the financial statements. Recognition also links the elements in the balance sheet and statement of profit or loss.

[Framework para 5.1].[Framework para 5.3].

[FAQ 1.73.1 – Recognition of an asset is prohibited under a specific standard](07.md#s10-FAQ_1.73.1)

<a id="s9-Para_1.74"></a>**1.74** Elements are recognised when they meet the definition of an asset, liability, equity, income or expense, and provide information that is useful to users of financial statements based on being relevant and representationally faithful.

[Framework para 5.6].

<a id="s9-Para_1.75"></a>**1.75** The fundamental cost-benefit constraint also applies to recognition decisions. Because information might be useful to some users and not others, depending on facts and circumstances, judgement might be required, and the recognition requirements might vary between and within the Board’s standards.

[Framework para 5.8].

<a id="s9-Para_1.7"></a>**1.7****6** The first of the two criteria for recognition – relevance – depends on consideration of several factors, including the level of uncertainty in whether an asset or liability exists, and the level of probability of inflows or outflows of economic benefits. Entities should consider these factors and others that might be present in determining recognition in the financial statements. A low probability of existence of the right or obligation, or a low probability of related economic benefits, does not, of itself, preclude recognition of an asset or liability, but it is among the considerations of recognition, measurement and information to be provided relating to such uncertainties. Such information might include:

- the magnitude of possible inflows or outflows,

- possible timing, and

- factors affecting probability of occurrence.

[Framework para 5.13]. [Framework para 5.14]. [Framework para 5.15]. [Framework para 5.16].

<a id="s9-Para_1.77"></a>**1.77** The second criteria for recognition – faithful representation – depends on consideration of measurement uncertainty and other factors. Measurement uncertainty includes consideration of estimates, and the degree of measurement uncertainty associated with estimates, including the range of possible outcomes, the sensitivity and complexity of the estimates. Other factors include how related information is depicted and information provided in the notes.

[Framework para 5.19]. [Framework para 5.20].

<a id="s9-Para_1.78"></a>**1.78** The recognition criterion for income and expenses is as follows: “Income is increases in assets, or decreases in liabilities, that result in increases in equity, other than those relating to contributions from holders of equity claims”.

[Framework para 4.68].

[FAQ 1.78.1 – Asset and liability approach – when should an asset be recognised?](07.md#s10-FAQ_1.78.1)

<a id="s9-Para_1.79"></a>**1.79** The recognition criterion for expenses is the mirror of the recognition criterion for income: “Expenses are decreases in assets, or increases in liabilities, that result in decreases in equity, other than those relating to distributions to holders of equity claims”.

[Framework para 4.69].

<a id="s9-Para_1.80"></a>**1.80**The asset-liability approach has replaced the matching concept as the driver of recognition of assets, liabilities, income and expense in the financial statements. However, the Framework notes that information about income and expenses is equally as important as information about assets and liabilities.

[Framework para 4.71].

<a id="s9-Para_1.81"></a>**1.81** The March 2018 Framework removed probability as a determinative criterion for whether or not an asset or liability exists, and instead treats probability considerations within the broader umbrella of uncertainty. Specifically, the Framework states: “An asset or liability can exist even if the probability of an inflow or outflow of economic benefits is low”.

[Framework para 5.15].

<a id="s9-Para_1.82"></a>**1.82**In limited circumstances, measurement uncertainty might be so high – even for all relevant measures – that none would provide useful information about the asset or liability. The Framework states: “In those limited circumstances, the asset or liability would not be recognised”. Whether or not the asset or liability is recognised, the entity should consider providing explanatory information about the uncertainties related to measurement, to provide useful information that is relevant and representationally faithful.

[Framework para 5.22]. [Framework para 5.23].

 

<a id="s9-chap_01_navPoint_27"></a>
### Derecognition

<a id="s9-Para_1.83"></a>**1.83** The criteria for derecognition, or removal of all or part of a recognised asset or liability from the financial statements, are:

- for an asset: when the entity loses control of all or part of the recognised asset, and

- for a liability: when the entity no longer has a present obligation for all or part of the recognised liability.

[Framework para 5.26].

<a id="s9-chap_01_navPoint_28"></a>
### Measurement

 

<a id="s9-Para_1.84"></a>**1.84**The Framework does not prescribe specific measurement bases. Instead, it describes them, stating that consideration of the qualitative characteristics is likely to result in the selection of different measurement bases for different assets, liabilities, income and expenses.

[Framework para 6.1]. [Framework para 6.2].

<a id="s9-Para_1.85"></a>**1.85** Measurement bases include historical cost and current value (which includes fair value, value in use for assets and fulfilment value for liabilities and current cost).

[Framework paras 6.4–6.22]. [Framework Table 6.1].

<a id="s9-Para_1.86"></a>**1.86** Factors to consider in selecting a measurement basis include:

- The nature of the information that the measurement basis will provide in the financial statements.

- The relevance and representational faithfulness of information under different measurement bases for the item.

- The characteristics of the asset or liability, including sensitivity to market factors or other risks.

- Whether changes in fair value of the asset or liability reflect changes in expectations of market participants, and changes in their risk preferences.

- The degree of outcome uncertainty and measurement uncertainty.

[Framework paras 6.43–6.62].

<a id="s9-Para_1.87"></a>**1.87** The enhancing qualitative characteristics of comparability, understandability and verifiability, and the cost constraint (cost-benefit) are additional considerations in choosing a measurement basis. Consistently using the same measurement basis for the same items aids comparability; however, a change in measurement basis might be justified if other factors outweigh the reduction in understandability and comparability.

[Framework para 6.63]. [Framework para 6.65]. [Framework para 6.66].

<a id="s9-Para_1.88"></a>**1.88** The total carrying amount of equity is not measured directly; it is the difference between total recognised assets and total recognised liabilities.

[Framework para 6.87].

<a id="s9-Para_1.89"></a>**1.89** Cash flow-based measurement techniques are sometimes used to estimate assets or liabilities when the measure cannot be observed directly.

[Framework para 6.91].

 

<a id="s9-chap_01_navPoint_29"></a>
### Capital maintenance

<a id="s9-Para_1.90"></a>**1.90** Most entities adopt a financial concept of capital maintenance. This concentrates on maintaining the financial capital that an entity’s owners have invested. The selection of the appropriate concept of capital maintenance depends on the needs of users. The Board does not prescribe a particular model, except when there are unusual circumstances such as hyperinflation.

[Framework para 8.1]. [Framework para 8.9].

<a id="s9-chap_01_navPoint_30"></a>
### Capital maintenance concepts, and profit

<a id="s9-Para_1.91"></a>**1.91** Capital maintenance is central to the measurement of profit. There are two concepts of capital, which are aligned with two concepts of capital maintenance:

- A financial concept of capital and the related concept of financial capital maintenance state that profit is earned if the financial (monetary) amount of net assets at the end of the period exceeds the amount at the beginning of the period, excluding distributions to and contributions from equity holders (owners) during the period.

- The financial concept of capital is the most widely adopted capital concept used in the preparation of financial statements.

- A physical concept of capital (such as operating capability) and the related concept of physical capital maintenance measure profit based on the extent to which the physical productive capacity (operating capacity) of the entity (or the resources or funds needed to achieve that capacity) at the end of the period exceed the amount at the beginning of the period, excluding distributions to and contributions from equity holders (owners) during the period.

[Framework para 8.1]. [Framework para 8.2]. [Framework para 8.3].

<a id="s9-Para_1.92"></a>**1.92**The chosen concept of capital maintenance provides a reference point for measuring profit and distinguishes between an entity’s return on capital and return of capital. Profit is the residual amount remaining after expenses (including capital maintenance adjustments, if any) have been deducted from income. If expenses exceed income, the residual is defined as a loss.

[Framework para 8.4].

<a id="s9-Para_1.93"></a>**1.93** The selection of measurement bases and the concept of capital maintenance determine the accounting model used in preparation of the financial statements. The Framework is applicable to a range of accounting models, and the Board does not prescribe a particular model except in exceptional circumstances, such as in hyper-inflationary economies.

[Framework para 8.9].

[FAQ 1.93.1 – Two concepts of capital maintenance](07.md#s10-FAQ_1.93.1)

<a id="s9-chap_01_navPoint_31"></a>
### Historical cost

 

<a id="s9-Para_1.94"></a>**1.94**Historical cost is the value of the costs incurred in acquiring or creating an asset, plus transaction costs; the historical cost of a liability is the consideration received to incur or take on the liability, minus transaction costs. Historical cost is updated over time to reflect: consumption (fulfilment) of all or part of an asset (liability); events that cause impairment of assets or increase the value of obligations to transfer economic resources to fulfil the obligation; and accrual of interest for related financing components.

[Framework para 6.5]. [Framework para 6.6]. [Framework para 6.7]. [Framework para 6.8].

 

<a id="s9-chap_01_navPoint_32"></a>
### Current value

<a id="s9-Para_1.95"></a>**1.95** Current value measures provide monetary information about assets, liabilities and related income and expense, using information updated to reflect conditions at the measurement date. Current value measurement bases include fair value, value in use for assets and fulfilment value for liabilities, and current cost.

<a id="s9-chap_01_navPoint_33"></a>
### Fair value

<a id="s9-Para_1.96"></a>**1.96** Fair value is defined as “the price that would be received to sell an asset or paid to transfer a liability in an orderly transaction between market participants at the measurement date”. In some cases, fair value can be determined directly by observing prices in an active market; in other cases, it is determined indirectly, for example, by cash flow measurement techniques, using the perspective of market participants’ pricing of assets and liabilities. Fair value is not increased (assets) or decreased (liabilities) for transaction costs or disposal costs, which are treated separately.

[Framework para 6.12]. [Framework para 6.13]. [Framework para 6.14]. [Framework para 6.15]. [Framework para 6.16]. [IFRS 13 para 9].

<a id="s9-chap_01_navPoint_34"></a>
### Value in use and fulfilment value

<a id="s9-Para_1.97"></a>**1.97** Value in use represents the present value of cash flows or other economic benefits that the entity expects to derive from use of an asset, including on its disposal. Fulfilment value is the present value of cash or other economic resources that the entity expects to be obligated to transfer in fulfilment of the liability to the counterparty, and any amounts that the entity expects to be obligated to transfer to other parties, to enable it to fulfil the liability to the counterparty. Value in use and fulfilment value reflect entity-specific assumptions and cannot be observed directly; thus they are determined using cash flow-based measurement techniques, reflecting the same factors as for fair value determinations, except from an entity-specific perspective rather than from a market participant perspective.

[Framework para 6.17].

<a id="s9-Para_1.98"></a>**1.98**Even though some standards incorporate the use of fair values (for example, IAS 19, IFRS 9, IAS 40, IAS 41 and IFRS 2), many assets (such as property, plant and equipment, intangibles and inventories) are generally recorded at historical cost, as are most liabilities. The fair value basis is generally used for a substantial proportion of assets by financial institutions. However, for a large proportion of IFRS reporting entities, the fair value basis is confined to a small minority of their assets: a proportion of their financial assets.

<a id="s9-chap_01_navPoint_35"></a>
### Current cost

<a id="s9-Para_1.99"></a>**1.99** The current cost of an asset is the cost of an equivalent asset at the measurement date, consisting of consideration that would be paid plus transaction costs that would be incurred on that date. The current cost of a liability is the reverse. Current cost is an ‘entry value’, because it reflects the market price to acquire an asset or incur a liability.

[Framework para 6.21].

### Presentation and disclosure

<a id="s9-chap_01_navPoint_idp1687854088"></a>
#### Presentation and disclosure as communication tools

<a id="s9-Para_1.100"></a>**1.100** Effective communication of information in financial statements makes them more relevant and representationally faithful, enhancing their usefulness. Therefore, the financial statements aim to accomplish the following in terms of presentation and disclosure:

- focus on presentation and disclosure objectives and principles rather than rules;

- classify information by grouping similar items and separating dissimilar items; and

- aggregate information, but avoid obscuring with unnecessary detail or excessive aggregation.

[Framework para 7.1]. [Framework para 7.2].

<a id="s9-chap_01_navPoint_idp1687852552"></a>
#### Cost-benefit considerations

<a id="s9-Para_1.101"></a>**1.101** Cost-benefit considerations apply to decisions about presentation and disclosure as they do to financial reporting more generally.

[Framework para 7.3].

<a id="s9-chap_01_navPoint_idp1687866120"></a>
#### Presentation and disclosure objectives and principles

<a id="s9-Para_1.102"></a>**1.102** The Board, in its standard-setting activity, will aim to strike a balance between providing flexibility – so that entities can provide information that they deem to be relevant and representationally faithful – and establishing requirements so that information will be comparable from period to period for a reporting entity, and comparable within a reporting period across entities. The Board will include presentation and disclosure objectives in standards to help entities to identify useful information and decide how to communicate that information most effectively.

[Framework para 7.4]. [Framework para 7.5].

<a id="s9-chap_01_navPoint_idp1687865224"></a>
#### Classification of assets and liabilities

<a id="s9-Para_1.103"></a>**1.103**Classification:

- sorts items in the financial statements – assets, liabilities, equity, income or expenses – based on shared characteristics, for the purposes of presentation and disclosure;

- avoids combining dissimilar items; and

- applies to the unit of account selected for that asset or liability.

[Framework para 7.7]. [Framework para 7.8]. [Framework para 7.9].

<a id="s9-chap_01_navPoint_idp1687871368"></a>
#### Offsetting

 

<a id="s9-Para_1.104"></a>**1.104** Offsetting is the result of recognising an asset and liability as separate units of account, and grouping them together into a single net amount in the balance sheet. This differs from treating a set of rights and obligations as a single unit of account. The Framework states, “Offsetting classifies dissimilar items together and therefore is generally not appropriate”.

[Framework para 7.10]. [Framework para 7.11].

 

<a id="s9-chap_01_navPoint_idp1687875336"></a>
#### Classification of equity

<a id="s9-Para_1.105"></a>**1.105** Equity claims might need to be classified separately if they have different characteristics. Also, separation of some components of equity might be necessary to provide useful information if subject to certain legal, regulatory or other requirements.

[Framework para 7.12]. [Framework para 7.13].

<a id="s9-chap_01_navPoint_idp1687888392"></a>
#### Classification of income and expenses, profit or loss, and other comprehensive income

 

<a id="s9-Para_1.106"></a>**1.106** Income and expenses are classified based on the unit of account relating to an asset or liability, or components of an asset or liability, and recorded in the statement of profit or loss or other comprehensive income.

[Framework para 7.14]. [Framework para 7.15].

<a id="s9-Para_1.107"></a>**1.107** The statement of profit or loss is the primary performance indicator.

[Framework para 7.16].

<a id="s9-Para_1.108"></a>**1.108** The Board might call for items of income and expense to be deferred through other comprehensive income. Although, in principle, income and expenses included in other comprehensive income would be reclassified to the statement of profit or loss in a future period, there might be situations when there is no clear basis to identify that period. In such cases, the amounts might not be reclassified out of other comprehensive income.

[Framework para 7.17]. [Framework para 7.18]. [Framework para 7.19].

### <a id="s10-FAQ_1.21.1"></a>FAQ 1.21.1 – What information is needed to assess the prospects for future net cash inflows, and why?

 

Reference to standard: Framework paras 1.4, 1.13, 1.15, 6.15

  
 Reference to standing text: [1.21](07.md#s8-Para_1.21) 

  

The following information is needed to assess the prospects for future net cash inflows:

- Information about management’s discharge of its responsibilities assists existing investors, lenders and other creditors who have the right to vote on or influence management’s actions in making decisions.

- Information about the nature and amounts of economic resources and claims assists in identifying financial strengths and weaknesses. It supports the assessment of an entity’s liquidity and solvency, its needs for additional financing and the likelihood of raising that finance. That information can also help users to assess management’s stewardship of the entity’s economic resources. Information about priorities and payment requirements of existing claims helps users to predict how future cash flows will be distributed among those with a claim against the reporting entity.

- Changes in a reporting entity’s economic resources and claims result from the entity’s performance and from other events or transactions such as issuing debt or equity instruments. Users need to be able to distinguish between these changes in order to assess the potential for future cash flows.

- Financial performance information is essential to understand the return that is generated from the use of available resources. It provides insight into how well management has discharged its responsibilities to make efficient and effective use of resources. Information about the variability and components of return supports the assessment of the uncertainty of future cash flows.

- Cash flow information during a period assists users in assessing the entity’s ability to generate future net cash inflows. It shows how cash is obtained and spent, including borrowings and repayment of debt, cash distributions to investors and other factors that might impact upon the entity’s liquidity or solvency.

 

<a id="s10-chap_01_navPoint_41"></a>
### <a id="s10-FAQ_1.51.1"></a>FAQ 1.51.1 – What items are generally material, regardless of their individual size?

 

Reference to standard:   
 Reference to standing text: [1.51](07.md#s8-Para_1.51) 

  

The following items reported in financial statements are generally material, irrespective of their size.

The nature and materiality of the information affect its relevance, and the nature of information alone can be sufficient to determine its relevance. Determining whether information is material or not is a matter of professional judgement. The following items often qualify as material, regardless of their individual size:

- Related party transactions.

- A transaction or adjustment that changes a profit to a loss, and vice versa.

- A transaction or adjustment that takes an entity from having net current assets to net current liabilities, and vice versa.

- A transaction or adjustment that affects an entity’s ability to meet analysts’ consensus expectations.

- A transaction or adjustment that masks a change in earnings or other trends.

- A transaction or adjustment that concerns a segment or other portion of the entity’s business that has been identified as playing a significant role in the entity’s operations or profitability.

- A transaction or adjustment that affects an entity’s compliance with loan covenants or other contractual requirements.

- A transaction or adjustment that has the effect of increasing management’s compensation (for example, by satisfying requirements for the award of bonuses).

- Changes in laws and regulations.

- Non-compliance with laws and regulations.

- Fines against the entity.

- Legal cases.

- Deterioration in relationships with individual or groups of key suppliers, customers or employees.

- Dependency on a particular supplier, customer or employee.

 

<a id="s10-chap_01_navPoint_42"></a>
### <a id="s10-FAQ_1.51.2"></a>FAQ 1.51.2 – What items are not material?

 

Reference to standard:   
 Reference to standing text: [1.51](07.md#s8-Para_1.51) 

  

An item that is not material is not relevant, cannot influence a user’s decisions and need not be reported in the financial information. If immaterial items are reported in financial information, they can interfere with decision-making, because excessive detail might obscure the relevant information. (On 31 October 2018, the IASB issued amendments to the definition of ‘material’ in IAS 1 and IAS 8, clarifying that the reference to obscuring information addresses situations in which the effect is similar to omitting or misstating that information. These amendments should be applied for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2020. Earlier application is permitted.) However, determining what is material is a matter of professional judgement, and it would be inappropriate to set fixed monetary limits or rules.

 

<a id="s10-chap_01_navPoint_43"></a>
### <a id="s10-FAQ_1.65.1"></a>FAQ 1.65.1 – Should an entity recognise held shares controlled by another party?

 

Reference to standard: Framework para 4.4  
 Reference to standing text: [1.65](07.md#s9-Para_1.65) 

  

Entity A enters into a legal arrangement to act as trustee for entity B by holding listed shares on entity B’s behalf. Entity B makes all investment decisions, and entity A will act according to entity B’s instructions. Entity A will earn a trustee fee for holding the shares. Any dividends or profit (loss) from the investments belong to entity B. Should entity A recognise shares held on behalf of entity B?

Entity A should not recognise the listed shares as its asset, even though it is in possession of the shares. Entity A does not control the investment’s future economic benefits. Benefits from the investments flow to entity B, and entity A earns a trustee fee for holding the shares regardless of how they perform. The listed shares do not, therefore, meet the criteria of an asset in entity A’s balance sheet.

 

<a id="s10-chap_01_navPoint_44"></a>
### <a id="s10-FAQ_1.66.1"></a>FAQ 1.66.1 – Do costs incurred meet the criteria for recognition as an asset?

 

Reference to standard: Framework para 4.14; IAS 38 para 57  
 Reference to standing text: [1.66](07.md#s9-Para_1.66) 

  

Entity C incurs start-up costs but has not met the criteria (contained in para 57 of IAS 38) for recognising the costs of constructing an intangible asset. Entity C might have an expectation of benefits arising from its expenditure, as the activity might lead to an intangible asset and a further expectation that this will lead to economic benefits (profits) flowing to the entity. Do these start up costs meet the criteria for recognition as an asset?

Until the development phase is entered, the entity C does not have sufficient expectation or control of any economic benefits that might flow, and the expenditure does not meet the definition of an asset.

 

<a id="s10-chap_01_navPoint_45"></a>
### <a id="s10-FAQ_1.66.2"></a>FAQ 1.66.2 – Do costs incurred result in the control of a resource?

 

Reference to standard: Framework para 4.19  
 Reference to standing text: [1.66](07.md#s9-Para_1.66) 

  

Entity D is streamlining its operations. It decides to move one of its factories to Asia to reduce operating costs and is able to reduce future cash outflow significantly by doing so. Redundancy payments were made to employees currently working in the present factory as part of the relocation exercise. Can the redundancy payments be recognised as an asset?

The redundancy payments should not be recognised as an asset. The redundancy payments do not give entity D control over a resource, although it results in entity D reducing its future cash outflows. An asset should only be recognised if the entity controls certain resources.

 

<a id="s10-chap_01_navPoint_46"></a>
### <a id="s10-FAQ_1.66.3"></a>FAQ 1.66.3 – Treatment of an asset contributed by government

 

Reference to standard: Framework para 4.14; IAS 20 para 23  
 Reference to standing text: [1.66](07.md#s9-Para_1.66) 

  

Entity E receives a property from the government for the development of a leisure park, as part of the government’s efforts to increase tourism in the country. The property’s fair value can be reliably measured and entity E has control over the leisure park’s development and operation. How should entity E account for the property?

The grant of the property is a government grant; the property should, therefore, be measured either at fair value or at nominal amount. [IAS 20 para 23].

Entity E has control over the property from which future economic benefits are expected to flow to entity E when the leisure park is in operation. The property’s value can also be reliably measured. The definition and recognition criteria of assets are both met.

The government grant should be presented in the balance sheet in accordance with IAS 20.

 

<a id="s10-chap_01_navPoint_47"></a>
### <a id="s10-FAQ_1.73.1"></a>FAQ 1.73.1 – Recognition of an asset is prohibited under a specific standard

 

Reference to standard: Framework para 1.2; IAS 38 paras 21(b),63  
 Reference to standing text: [1.73](07.md#s9-Para_1.73) 

  

Entity F is in the beverage business. Its brand is known throughout the world. The brand can be sold and has a market value. Can entity F recognise the brand as an asset on its balance sheet?

Management should not recognise the brand as an asset, even though it meets the definition of one. Management is prohibited from recognising internally generated brands, mastheads, publishing titles, customer lists and items similar in substance. The recognition criteria for intangible assets are more narrowly defined by IAS 38, limiting recognition to assets for which cost can be measured reliably, rather than cost or value. The cost cannot be distinguished from the cost of developing the business as a whole. Such items are not recognised as intangible assets, as the cost cannot be reliably measured.

Where similar assets are acquired from a third party, the consideration given to acquire those assets is distinguishable from the general costs of developing the business as a whole and would, therefore, be recognised, provided they also meet the criteria of control and the probable flow of future economic benefits.

 

<a id="s10-chap_01_navPoint_48"></a>
### <a id="s10-FAQ_1.78.1"></a>FAQ 1.78.1 – Asset and liability approach – when should an asset be recognised?

 

Reference to standard: Framework paras 4.68, 5.5, 1.17  
 Reference to standing text: [1.78](07.md#s9-Para_1.78) 

  

Entity G, a telecom entity, invoices its customers for call charges on a monthly basis. At the beginning of March, the total value of invoices that entity G sent out to its customers was C500,000. The invoices relate to calls made by customers in February. The invoices are payable by the customers by the end of April. When should entity G recognise income for the customer calls?

Entity G should recognise income of C500,000 in the income statement at the end of February. A corresponding increase in assets (accounts receivable) should be recognised in the balance sheet.

Under accrual accounting, the effects of transactions and other events are recognised when they occur. They are reported in the financial statements of the periods to which they relate. Revenue is not accelerated or deferred to match the timing of the receipt of cash or the raising of the invoices.
