## Introduction

 

<a id="s363-Para_A2.1"></a>**A2.1** Consolidated financial statements of an issuer of debt or equity securities are normally required by regulators around the world. These requirements are usually met by presenting consolidated financial statements prepared under IFRS or local GAAP.

<a id="s363-Para_A2.2"></a>**A2.2** Financial information might be required for part or parts of a group, in some circumstances. This is usually in connection with a transaction of some nature – disposals, distributions, business combinations, spin-offs and initial public offerings (IPOs) are examples of transactions where a specific type of financial information is required.

<a id="s363-Para_A2.3"></a>**A2.3** The financial information might be needed for parts of a group that are under common control but are not part of a legal sub-group, where the preparation of consolidated financial statements would be appropriate. Financial statements of entities that are under common control are primarily prepared for use in a transaction. These are most often described as ‘combined’ or ‘carve out’ financial statements.

<a id="s363-Para_A2.4"></a>**A2.4** ‘Combined financial statements’ are financial information that includes economic activities that are bound together by common control but are not a legal group. These are usually prepared by aggregating the financial statements of segments, separate entities or components of groups that fail to meet the definition of a ‘group’ under IFRS 10.

<a id="s363-Para_A2.5"></a>**A2.5** ‘Carve out financial statements’ are the financial statements of a division or lesser business component(s) of a larger entity; the term is commonly used to refer to equity carve outs, spin-offs and split-offs. Carve out financial statements may also be combined financial statements if, for example, financial information from components of separate legal sub-groups are aggregated.

<a id="s363-Para_A2.6"></a>**A2.6** The term ‘new reporting entity’ is used to describe the economic activity, usually part of an existing group that is the basis of the combined financial statements.

<a id="s363-Para_A2.7"></a>**A2.7** The preparation of combined financial statements can require the exercise of considerable judgement. Each transaction is different and each set of combined financial statements will present unique challenges. The nature of the transaction, the boundary of the reporting entity, the quality of the accounting records, the past practices and policies of the parent, and the views of the relevant regulator are very important.

<a id="s363-Para_A2.8"></a>**A2.8** IFRS does not include specific guidance on the preparation of combined financial statements, although the Conceptual Framework and other standards include relevant information for preparing combined financial statements. However, it is accepted that there are circumstances in which combined financial statements can claim to be prepared in accordance with IFRS.

<a id="s363-Para_A2.9"></a>**A2.9** There is no set method that results in combined financial statements being prepared in accordance with IFRS. There is a common set of questions that need to be asked; the answers will determine whether IFRS combined financial statements can be prepared and how that might be accomplished, as well as what disclosures might be required.

<a id="s363-Para_A2.10"></a>**A2.10** This guidance is designed to assist in the preparation of combined financial statements. Each set of combined financial statements presents unique questions and challenges. Nothing should be read as overriding the accounting standards or relevant regulatory requirements.

<a id="s363-Para_A2.11"></a>**A2.11** This guidance sets out the questions and describes some of the common practices that are followed in preparing combined financial statements under IFRS.

<a id="s363-Para_A2.12"></a>**A2.12** This guidance does not address the accounting issues that might arise in the current parent or ultimate controlling party.

<a id="s363-Para_A2.13"></a>**A2.13** The preparation of combined financial statements, particularly for a capital markets transaction, always presents a unique set of issues. Management should ensure that it has consulted its auditors and professional advisers. Management should also understand the views and requirements of the relevant regulators.

## Step 1: Determine the purpose of the combined financial statements and understand the relevant regulatory requirements

<a id="s364-Para_A2.15"></a>**A2.15** Consolidated financial statements and combined financial statements both present historical financial information. Consolidated financial statements require the consolidation of the parent company and all of its subsidiaries. Combined financial statements present the historical financial information of specific economic activities.

<a id="s364-Para_A2.16"></a>**A2.16** A key assumption underlying the preparation of combined financial statements is that there is a binding element for the economic activities throughout the period (see further guidance in [para A2.42](87.md#s365-Para_A2.42)). The binding element is not necessarily direct legal ownership, although common control is almost always present. The binding element is the characteristic based on which the economic activities are ‘bound’ together throughout a period (for example, legal ownership or common control).

<a id="s364-Para_A2.17"></a>**A2.17** ‘Carve out’ financial statements are the financial statements of a division or lesser business component(s) of a larger entity. The term ‘carve out’ arises because the business unit’s financial statements are viewed as being ‘carved out’ of the larger consolidated financial statements. There is no accounting definition of a carve out transaction; the term is commonly used to refer to equity carve outs, spin-offs and split-offs.

<a id="s364-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_3"></a>
### What is the objective of the combined financial statements?

 

<a id="s364-Para_A2.18"></a>**A2.18** Combined financial statements are usually prepared in connection with a contemplated transaction, such as an initial public offering (IPO) through a spin-off of a division or major line of business, or in connection with a planned distribution to shareholders. Alternatively, the combined financial statements might serve as the basis for final purchase price discussions in a merger and acquisition transaction.

<a id="s364-Para_A2.19"></a>**A2.19** The purpose and the users of the combined financial statements might influence how the financial information is prepared and presented. The working assumption in this guidance is that non-standard financial statements are required that comply with IFRS.

<a id="s364-Para_A2.20"></a>**A2.20** Few countries have specific regulatory requirements for the preparation of non-standard financial statements. See [FAQ A2.20.1](87.md#s369-FAQ_A2.20.1) for examples of regulatory requirements relating to combined financial information.

[FAQ A2.20.1 – What regulatory information sources are there for combined financial statements?](87.md#s369-FAQ_A2.20.1)

<a id="s364-Para_A2.21"></a>**A2.21** If there are specific regulatory requirements, those must be followed. The resulting financial information might or might not comply with IFRS. For example, a reporting entity preparing combined financial statements under IFRS subject to SEC regulation will be expected to follow the SEC guidance. The combined financial statements would normally comply with IFRS as well. Financial information prepared on the basis of the UK’s guidance in Standards for Investment Reporting (SIR) 2000, ‘Investment reporting standards applicable to public reporting engagements on historical financial information’, might be described as complying with IFRS. The basis of preparation should include a statement of compliance with IFRS, but it should also be amended to include compliance with (SIR) 2000.

<a id="s364-Para_A2.22"></a>**A2.22** Many other countries find the existing guidance of other regulators useful, but their regulators are broadly looking for combined financial statements prepared in accordance with IFRS. Most combined financial statements are prepared with a view to capital market transactions. Management should understand the perspective of the relevant regulator in the early stages, even if the regulator has not published comprehensive guidelines on preparing combined financial statements. The views of the regulator might be obvious from similar transactions that have occurred, public statements, company law and similar sources.

<a id="s364-Para_A2.23"></a>**A2.23** Different regulators might take different views, and this could have a profound impact on the nature, extent and even acceptability of the combined financial statements.

  
<a id="s364-Para_A2.24"></a>**A2.24** Combined financial statements might be prepared in a jurisdiction that has no specific guidance, or they might be prepared for another purpose, such as providing to lenders, suppliers or counterparties. It might be useful to voluntarily follow a set of regulatory guidance, because it is likely to be better understood by users.

<a id="s364-Para_A2.25"></a>**A2.25** The basic objectives of financial reporting are to provide useful information for assessing management stewardship and allowing users to assess the ability of the reporting entity to generate cash flows. These objectives remain relevant when preparing combined financial statements.

<a id="s364-Para_A2.26"></a>**A2.26** The combined financial statements are inherently a ‘temporary’ measure. They are prepared for a specific objective at a point in time for a specified number of reporting periods. It would be unusual for combined financial statements to be prepared for a number of successive periods. After a certain period of time, the reporting entity will either be in a position to prepare consolidated financial statements, or it would have been subsumed into a larger group, following the transaction or the restructuring.

 

<a id="s364-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_4"></a>
### Can ‘fair presentation’ or ‘true and fair view’ be achieved for combined financial statements?

<a id="s364-Para_A2.27"></a>**A2.27** Both the purpose of reporting and the relevant regulator might require a ‘clean’ audit opinion on the combined financial statements (that is, a ‘presents fairly’ or ‘true and fair view’ opinion). That requirement might define how the financial information is prepared.

<a id="s364-Para_A2.28"></a>**A2.28** There are many examples of combined financial statements of a new reporting entity that assert compliance with IFRS that have been subject to a normal audit. An audit opinion might refer to ‘true and fair’ or ‘fair presentation in accordance with IFRS’.

<a id="s364-Para_A2.29"></a>**A2.29** The more complex the pre-transaction structure and the more adjustments and allocations that are made during the preparation, the less likely it is that combined financial statements can be created that provide a ‘true and fair view’.

<a id="s364-Para_A2.30"></a>**A2.30** However, there are circumstances in which the combined financial statements should be described as ‘drawn up in accordance with the basis of preparation described in Note X’; these ‘basis of preparation’ combined financial statements are not general purpose financial statements in accordance with IFRS.

<a id="s364-Para_A2.31"></a>**A2.31** The more complex the pre-transaction structure and the more adjustments and allocations that have to be made, the further the financial information will be pushed towards a ‘basis of preparation’ and away from ‘in accordance with IFRS’. Therefore, the assurance given will refer to compliance with the basis of preparation as set out.

<a id="s364-Para_A2.32"></a>**A2.32** The nature of the audit opinion is principally governed by local regulation and the nature of the financial information. ‘Basis of preparation’ is accepted by some regulators. Other regulators (for example, the Hong Kong Stock Exchange) require audit opinions that refer to full compliance with IFRS.

<a id="s364-Para_A2.33"></a>**A2.33** An ‘emphasis of matter’ paragraph is also commonly used in audit opinions on combined financial statements prepared in accordance with IFRS. The ‘emphasis of matter’ paragraph might explain, for example, that the new reporting entity has not operated as a separate entity, or that the combined financial statements are not necessarily indicative of results that would have occurred if the business had been a separate stand-alone entity during the period presented.

## Step 2: Determine the new reporting entity

 

<a id="s365-Para_A2.34"></a>**A2.34** A reporting entity in a typical capital market transaction is a group headed by a legal entity. However, if combined financial statements are required, the legal structure will seldom have been in place throughout the track record period: the legal group might only be established when the transaction is executed; or there might be a group restructuring before the transaction, so the reporting entity’s history includes common control acquisitions or combinations of entities that are not legally connected. Setting the ‘boundary’ of the new reporting entity is the basis of financial reporting. This process can be challenging and will involve judgement.

<a id="s365-Para_A2.35"></a>**A2.35** ‘Common control’ exists where control lies with the same ultimate party or parties bound by a contract.

<a id="s365-Para_A2.36"></a>**A2.36** The IFRS Framework defines a reporting entity as follows: “A reporting entity is an entity that is required, or chooses, to prepare financial statements. A reporting entity can be a single entity or a portion of an entity or can comprise more than one entity. A reporting entity is not necessarily a legal entity."

[IFRS Framework para 3.10].

<a id="s365-Para_A2.37"></a>**A2.37** An operating segment of a larger group, a group of entities that are legally unconnected but operating together under common control to achieve a single business objective, or entities bound together by contract could all be a reporting entity in appropriate circumstances. The IFRS framework states that “if a reporting entity comprises two or more entities that are not all linked by a parent-subsidiary relationship, the reporting entity’s financial statements are referred to as ‘combined financial statements'".

[IFRS Framework para 3.12].

<a id="s365-Para_A2.38"></a>**A2.38**The IFRS framework acknowledges that the appropriate boundary of a reporting entity could be difficult to determine. This may be the case if the reporting entity is not a legal entity and does not comprise only legal entities linked by a parent-subsidiary relationship. The framework notes that the information needs of the primary users of the reporting entity should be taken into account when determining the appropriate boundary. Users require a faithful presentation. Therefore, according to the framework, the information presented requires that:

- “the boundary of the reporting entity does not contain an arbitrary or incomplete set of economic activities;

- including that set of economic activities within the boundary of the reporting entity results in neutral information; and

- a description is provided of how the boundary of the reporting entity was determined and of what constitutes the reporting entity.”

[IFRS Framework paras 3.13 and [3.14](09.md#s20-Para_3.14)].

 

<a id="s365-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_6"></a>
### Can a ‘business’ without legal boundaries be a reporting entity?

<a id="s365-Para_A2.39"></a>**A2.39** A reporting entity does not need to be a legal entity. A separate vehicle that is not a legal entity and that does not have a separate legal ‘personality’ has the potential to be a reporting entity if it is a separately identifiable financial structure (for example, some type of partnership, branch or segment).

<a id="s365-Para_A2.40"></a>**A2.40** A reporting entity would not therefore be limited to entities structured as legal entities. It can comprise a portion of an entity, or two or more entities.

<a id="s365-Para_A2.41"></a>**A2.41** The boundary of a reporting entity that is not a legal entity should be set in a way that users who rely on the financial statements will be able to obtain relevant financial information. The financial statements should represent faithfully the economic activities of the entity (see [para A2.38](87.md#s365-Para_A2.38) above).

<a id="s365-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_7"></a>
### What factors indicate that economic activities are bound together and form a reporting entity?

 

<a id="s365-Para_A2.42"></a>**A2.42** There are generally two key considerations to assess when determining whether a reporting entity exists as a basis for preparing combined financial statements, as follows:

- whether the assets and liabilities are under common control or common management (usually with common control, see [paras A2.43](87.md#s365-Para_A2.43) to [A2.48](87.md#s365-Para_A2.48) below); and

- whether the economic activity of the reporting entity is or will be bound together for a purpose through, for example:

- a legal re-organisation of a group or groups that has occurred after the reporting date, but before publication of the financial statements;

- a legal re-organisation that will happen in connection with a proposed IPO, disposal or similar transaction; or

- an agreement that was signed and in place throughout the periods covered by the combined financial statements.

 

<a id="s365-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_8"></a>
### Must common control be present for a reporting entity to exist?

<a id="s365-Para_A2.43"></a>**A2.43** It would be rare for a reporting entity to be identified where common control is absent.

<a id="s365-Para_A2.44"></a>**A2.44** A reporting entity where the various entities or businesses are under common control will have a single governing body that governs the relevant activities. Users can assess whether that governing body has made efficient and effective use of the resources if the entities have been under common control.

<a id="s365-Para_A2.45"></a>**A2.45** Combined financial statements can only be prepared for the period when entities were under common control. If the entity acquired another entity from a third party during the track record period of the combined financial statements, acquisition accounting is applied from the date when control was obtained.

<a id="s365-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_9"></a>
### Can an entity prepare combined financial statements if the economic activities of the proposed reporting entity are not under common control?

 

<a id="s365-Para_A2.46"></a>**A2.46** It is rarely appropriate to prepare combined financial statements where the economic activities have been under common management that is not accompanied by common control.

<a id="s365-Para_A2.47"></a>**A2.47** Common management, where it is not supported by contractual arrangements or common control, is seldom a sufficient basis to establish a reporting entity.

[FAQ A2.47.1 – Can common management be a basis for common control?](87.md#s369-FAQ_A2.47.1)

  
 <a id="s365-Para_A2.48"></a>**A2.48** A common business is seldom sufficient to establish a reporting entity. A private equity fund might decide to acquire a number of businesses in a single industry over a short period of time and then consolidate these into a group to achieve economies of scale and synergies. The purchase of each individual business is a business combination, and acquisition accounting is required. The entities acquired have a common business, and combining financial information over several years might be useful for investors. However, preparing combined financial statements for the acquired businesses and the acquirer would conflict with the requirement to exclude the financial information of an acquiree before acquisition.

### Do the principles of consolidation accounting apply when preparing the combined financial statements of the reporting entity?

<a id="s365-Para_A2.50"></a>**A2.50** Combined financial statements must follow all of the recognition, measurement and disclosure requirements of IFRS to claim compliance with IFRS. The basic objectives of financial reporting should apply, providing useful information for:

- assessing management stewardship;

- allowing users to assess the ability of the reporting entity to generate cash flows; and

- allowing comparability over time.

<a id="s365-Para_A2.51"></a>**A2.51** A re-organisation is often effected to form a new reporting entity. A new entity might be formed to acquire only a portion of the existing legal group as the listing business.

<a id="s365-Para_A2.52"></a>**A2.52** Practice varies as to whether the combined financial statements present a non-controlling interest. This is a complex area that depends on a number of factors, including whether or not the non-controlling interest survives the planned transaction. Management should consider what treatment will both provide useful information and comply with IFRS.

<a id="s365-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_12"></a>
### What is the disposal group approach, and when might it be appropriate for combined financial statements?

<a id="s365-Para_A2.53"></a>**A2.53** A new reporting entity, when preparing combined financial statements for a transaction, might include businesses that were legally part of the reporting entity during the track record period but will not be part of the new reporting entity going forward. A re-organisation is effected at or near the point of the transaction, so that only the continuing business (the new reporting entity) forms the new legal group. Management needs to decide how to present the financial results and financial position of the reporting entity in the combined financial statements, particularly where there has been a re-organisation that includes the disposal of some elements of the new reporting entity.

<a id="s365-Para_A2.54"></a>**A2.54** The two approaches commonly seen in practice are:

- to exclude all businesses and activity that will not form part of the new legal group from the combined financial statements (carve out basis); or

- to include such businesses and activity and present them as a disposal group or discontinued operations (disposal group basis).

<a id="s365-Para_A2.55"></a>**A2.55** Regulators might have a preference for one type of presentation; for example, regulators in the US would generally expect the disposal group basis approach to be followed. The approach chosen will depend on several factors and will involve the exercise of judgement.

<a id="s365-Para_A2.56"></a>**A2.56** Users often prefer to have information relating only to the continuing business; so, where possible, management might seek to apply the carve out approach in the combined financial statements. It is reasonable to follow the carve out approach only where certain criteria are met. These criteria are not defined under IFRS, but regulators in some jurisdictions (for example, the UK and US) have more explicit guidance that generally shares similar concepts. These are:

- the business that is being disposed of is dissimilar to the remainder of the new reporting entity;

- the business that is being disposed of has been separately managed; and

- separate historical books and records exist for the business being disposed of.

<a id="s365-Para_A2.57"></a>**A2.57** The disposal group approach is an alternative basis for presenting the combined financial statements of a new reporting entity. The disposal group approach has both listing and non-listing businesses (in the case of an IPO) during the track record period where it is not appropriate or possible to use the carve out approach. The disposal group approach consolidates all of the businesses to be disposed of, up to the date of disposal or discontinuance.

<a id="s365-Para_A2.58"></a>**A2.58** The financial information of the disposed businesses or discontinued operations is included in the new reporting entity up until this date; they are presented as a disposal group separately from the continuing business.

<a id="s365-Para_A2.59"></a>**A2.59** The concept of a ‘disposal group’, in the context of preparing combined financial statements, is similar to the definition of a disposal group in accordance with IFRS 5: “a group of assets to be disposed of, by sale or otherwise, together as a group in a single transaction, and liabilities directly associated with those assets that will be transferred in the transaction”.

<a id="s365-Para_A2.60"></a>**A2.60** A disposal group does not necessarily need to meet the definition of a ‘discontinued operation’ as defined in IFRS 5. The disposal group treatment is applied only for those balance sheets where the requirements of IFRS 5 have been met as of the balance sheet date.

<a id="s365-Para_A2.61"></a>**A2.61** The disposal group approach is commonly used where the new reporting entity includes entities that will not be transferred on a re-organisation.

<a id="s365-Para_A2.62"></a>**A2.62** Management should consider the specific facts of each individual case.

<a id="s366-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_13"></a>
## Step 3: Consider whether the combined financial statements can comply with IFRS

<a id="s366-Para_A2.63"></a>**A2.63** Combined financial statements are those where management is seeking to claim compliance with IFRS and that are prepared based on historical data. Historical financial information is past information that has been prepared in accordance with statutory requirements and relevant accounting standards. A core assumption is that the historical financial information is drawn from distinct or separate accounting records and that the information can be audited.

<a id="s366-Para_A2.64"></a>**A2.64** ‘Pro-forma financial information’, on the other hand, is hypothetical information that seeks to illustrate a particular outcome or scenario on an ‘as-if’ basis. Such financial information includes effects of events or transactions that did not occur (non-historical), or occurred earlier or later than the date recognised in the financial statements. This term is used in different ways in different jurisdictions.

<a id="s366-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_14"></a>
### Historical or pro-forma?

 

<a id="s366-Para_A2.65"></a>**A2.65** A new reporting entity being prepared for a transaction is rarely a neat sub-group that has prepared consolidated financial statements that present only and all of the economic activities and transactions that encompass the new reporting entity, including debt, taxes, share-based payments, share of corporate assets, management services and charges. The reporting entity can prepare and present consolidated financial statements in those circumstances.

<a id="s366-Para_A2.66"></a>**A2.66** Most new reporting entities preparing for a transaction will not have the financial information in the form and nature described in [paragraph A2.65](87.md#s366-Para_A2.65). Management might need to make adjustments, including the allocation of certain assets, liabilities and expenses that are relevant to the new reporting entity but that have not been accounted for within the legal entities within the boundary of the reporting entity.

<a id="s366-Para_A2.67"></a>**A2.67** Combined financial statements prepared in accordance with IFRS will frequently include adjustments or allocations to reflect the items described in [paragraph A2.65](87.md#s366-Para_A2.65). Determining what is appropriately included as historical financial information is a key area of judgement in preparing combined financial statements. The combined financial statements should avoid ‘as-if’ adjustments, but there is limited guidance on what to adjust and how. The goal is to avoid ‘as-if’ or ‘pro-forma’ financial information. The meaning and interpretation of the term ‘pro-forma’ might differ from territory to territory, and there might be some differences in how pro-forma financial information is prepared. However, pro-forma financial information does not comply with IFRS.

[EX A2.67.1 – Historical information prepared in accordance with IFRS](87.md#s369-EXA2.67.1)  
[EX A2.67.2 – Financial statements with significant adjustments](87.md#s369-EXA2.67.2)

<a id="s366-Para_A2.68"></a>**A2.68** Some jurisdictions regard the allocation of historical expenses incurred by the larger group to the reporting entity as historical information; this is because the allocation is based on actual past expenses incurred by the larger group, allocated to the reporting entity on a rational basis.

<a id="s366-Para_A2.69"></a>**A2.69** Other jurisdictions might regard any allocations as a pro-forma adjustment. [EX A2.67.2](87.md#s369-EXA2.67.2) sets out a basis of preparation from a set of pro-forma financial statements.

<a id="s367-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_15"></a>
## Step 4: Deal with the practical issues of preparation

<a id="s367-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_16"></a>
### Which accounting policies should be used in the combined financial statements prepared in accordance with IFRS?

<a id="s367-Para_A2.70"></a>**A2.70** Accounting policies should be appropriate for the purpose of the combined financial statements.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.71"></a>**A2.71** The accounting information previously recorded about the circumscribed area of economic activities will be the basis for preparing the combined financial statements. It might then be necessary to make adjustments to present the combined financial statements in accordance with the chosen accounting policies.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.72"></a>**A2.72** Different approaches are used in different circumstances:

- Using the accounting policies followed in the consolidated financial statements of the group (the current parent) to which the respective economic activities belong. This is frequently the case where a seller is preparing information to present to its existing investors or potential purchasers, such as information on parts of a legal group that are to be sold.

- Using the accounting policies that will be applied in the financial statements of the new reporting entity to which the respective economic activities will belong. This is often appropriate where a business is being demerged from an existing group into a new and separate legal entity. The combined financial statements are intended to show the financial performance of the economic activities forming part of that new reporting entity.

- Applying the acquirer’s accounting policies where the combined financial statements of the target company are prepared for a proposed acquisition, to present the financial performance of the acquiree under the acquirer’s accounting policies. This approach is required in certain jurisdictions, including the UK.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.73"></a>**A2.73** The choice of accounting policies will depend on the intended users of the financial information and the purpose of reporting. The fact that different accounting policies might be applied does not cause issues with regard to IFRS compliance; IFRS requires management to select appropriate policies that result in information that is relevant and reliable.

<a id="s367-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_17"></a>
### Purchase accounting adjustments from previous transactions

 

<a id="s367-Para_A2.74"></a>**A2.74** The new reporting entity might include businesses or entities that were previously acquired by the current parent. The treatment of purchase price adjustments and goodwill related to the new reporting entity, recognised by the current parent, can vary.

 

<a id="s367-Para_A2.75"></a>**A2.75** The predecessor method of accounting for common control business combinations and re-organisations generally uses the carrying values at the highest level of common control (see Appendix 1 [paras A1.19 to A1.21](86.md#s358-Para_A1.19)). The current parent is unlikely to form part of the new reporting entity. The question arises as to whether adjustments should be made to the combined financial statements of the new reporting entity, to reflect the carrying values of the current parent. Examples of such allocations are any goodwill and related purchase price adjustments recorded by the current parent that relate to the new reporting entity.

 

<a id="s367-Para_A2.76"></a>**A2.76** Parent entities rarely allocate goodwill and purchase price adjustments when using IFRS in separate financial statements. There is no requirement for ‘push-down accounting’ under IFRS, and company law in many jurisdictions would make push-down accounting difficult.

 

<a id="s367-Para_A2.77"></a>**A2.77** ‘Push-down accounting’ refers to establishing a new accounting basis for an acquired business in its separate financial statements following an acquisition; effectively, the parent's goodwill and purchase accounting adjustments are included in the separate financial statements.

 

<a id="s367-Para_A2.78"></a>**A2.78** Goodwill and the related purchase price adjustments might be included in the combined financial statements of the new reporting entity. The financial track record of the new reporting entity would be seen by many users and regulators as incomplete if it did not reflect the cost of the business to the current parent entity. In essence, the goodwill and purchase accounting should not be stranded in the current parent company but should accompany the business to which it relates.

 

[EX A2.78.1 – Allocation of goodwill](87.md#s369-EXA2.78.1)

 

<a id="s367-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_18"></a>
### Tangible and intangible assets

 

<a id="s367-Para_A2.79"></a>**A2.79** The new reporting entity will recognise all of the assets in the combined financial statements that its constituent entities control. Challenges can arise where the new reporting entity has used corporate assets such as brands or patents, and these might or might not be transferred to the new reporting entity. Similarly, a reporting entity might have historically controlled assets that will not be transferred to it as part of the transaction.

 

<a id="s367-Para_A2.80"></a>**A2.80** Assets might be attributed to the new reporting entity based on legal ownership, usage or through an inter-company sharing arrangement, such as an operating or lease agreement. The attribution should reflect the legal substance of arrangements that existed throughout the track record period.

 

<a id="s367-Para_A2.81"></a>**A2.81** Assets that have been controlled by a part of the new reporting entity during the track record period might not transfer to the new reporting entity as part of the transaction. Similar to the discussion on disposal groups (see [para A2.55](87.md#s365-Para_A2.55)), two approaches are seen in practice. One or the other might be preferred by the relevant regulator or might be more appropriate in the specific circumstances. The two approaches are as follows: to include the asset until the date of the transaction and make clear disclosure that it will not transfer; or to exclude the asset from the combined financial statements during the track record period, again making clear disclosure.

 

[FAQ A2.81.1 – How might intangible assets be allocated in combined financial statements?](87.md#s369-FAQ_A2.81.1)

 

<a id="s367-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_19"></a>
### Debt

<a id="s367-Para_A2.82"></a>**A2.82** The new reporting entity might have debt for which it is legally obligated, and this will be included in its combined financial statements. This will include third party debt issued directly by the legal entities that form the new reporting entity.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.83"></a>**A2.83** Third party debt of the current parent might also be assigned or legally assumed by the new reporting entity. This debt should be included in the combined financial statements of the new reporting entity for all periods in which the debt was outstanding.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.84"></a>**A2.84** Lease liabilities would follow the related right of use asset. The allocation of interest income and costs would follow the way in which the related debt and debt instruments have been apportioned.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.85"></a>**A2.85** There might be other circumstances in which debt should be included in the combined financial statements of the new reporting entity. Management should carefully consider the presentation that is most meaningful to users of the combined financial statements. For example, if the new reporting entity will have interest-bearing obligations to the current parent, this should be presented as debt financing. The basis for allocation should be consistent with the terms of the transaction, demerger or disposal agreement as relevant.

<a id="s367-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_20"></a>
### What income and expenses should be included in the combined financial statements of the new reporting entity?

 

<a id="s367-Para_A2.86"></a>**A2.86** The combined financial statements of the new reporting entity should include all economic activity arising from its business. A new reporting entity that has been part of a larger group will seldom have a free-standing financial track record.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.87"></a>**A2.87** Income and expenses from its economic activities might be recorded elsewhere in the group and might need to be allocated to the new reporting entity, for its combined financial statements to be described as prepared in accordance with IFRS. All relevant expenses should be included in the combined financial statements of the new reporting entity if a reasonable basis of allocation can be established.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.88"></a>**A2.88**Expenses incurred at the corporate level might be with third parties for services performed at the corporate level for lower-level entities. These might include payroll, accounting, centralised purchasing, marketing, executive management, lease expenses, advertising, legal, insurance, selling and administrative expenses. Some expenses − such as pensions, and other employee benefits − might only be recorded at the parent or corporate level.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.89"></a>**A2.89** The combined financial statements should reflect a reasonable allocation of expenses from the parent or other units of the group providing the services. The method of allocation might be by specific identification, legal obligation or in a manner that best reflects the nature of how the expense is incurred, such as headcount and time usage.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.90"></a>**A2.90** The allocation of corporate overhead involves judgement, but it should reflect the incremental costs incurred to service the new reporting entity. Inter-company transactions that have been eliminated in consolidated financial statements, such as purchases and sales, should be reinstated in the combined financial statements.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.91"></a>**A2.91** Estimating and allocating transactions not previously allocated to the reporting entity is not the same as creating transactions that have never happened (similar to ‘pro-forma’ information). Allocations are acceptable, provided that there is an appropriate basis, and the basis is clearly disclosed in the combined financial statements. The disclosure should include how allocated items were identified, and the method and effect of allocation.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.92"></a>**A2.92** The allocations should be made using high-quality information. Allocations should not be made if the information required to make reasonable and supportable estimates does not exist. Management should instead provide sufficient disclosure in the notes to enable readers of the combined financial statements to understand the nature of the expenses that have not been included in the new reporting entity.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.93"></a>**A2.93** Clear and meaningful disclosure in the notes to the combined financial statements is essential for a good understanding of the financial statements.

[EX A2.93.1 – Allocations in combined financial statements](87.md#s369-EXA2.93.1)

<a id="s367-Para_A2.94"></a>**A2.94** The UK’s SIR 2000 and US SEC guidance (see [FAQ A2.20.1](87.md#s369-FAQ_A2.20.1)) might be useful when determining how to allocate expenses to the new reporting entity. Factors usually considered when doing an allocation include:

- whether the assets and liabilities will be part of the new reporting entity;

- whether there was any intra-group recharge between the parent and the new reporting entity (for example, legal, accounting, finance or employee benefit expenses); and

- whether the recharges represent amounts actually incurred, and how the basis of the recharge has been determined.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.95"></a>**A2.95** There are a few areas that have specific guidance issued by regulators. These are discussed at [paragraph A2.96](87.md#s367-Para_A2.96) onwards.

 

<a id="s367-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_21"></a>
### Income taxes

 

<a id="s367-Para_A2.96"></a>**A2.96** Accounting for income taxes in combined financial statements almost always presents challenges. The ideal situation is a new reporting entity that was a legal sub-group filing a group tax return or individual tax returns, and that will continue under the same tax regime after the transaction. This situation rarely occurs in practice. However, the new reporting entity needs to apply the requirements of IAS 12 in preparing the combined financial statements.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.97"></a>**A2.97** Management needs to analyse the tax circumstances of the new reporting entity, including its legal status after the transaction, to account for income taxes. There are two approaches broadly seen in practice to apply IAS 12 to the combined financial statements of the new reporting entity: the separate tax return method; and the tax incurred method.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.98"></a>**A2.98** The separate tax return method aggregates the tax position of the individual entities of the new reporting entity. Legal entities that comprise the new reporting entity and previously filed separate tax returns have a clear tax position. Current tax expense and tax assets and liabilities are accounted for in accordance with the tax returns. Deferred tax assets and liabilities arise from the individual book tax differences.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.99"></a>**A2.99** However, some groups prepare consolidated tax returns that include all entities in the group and allocate tax expense under an inter-company or tax-sharing arrangement.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.100"></a>**A2.100** If management chooses to follow the separate return method to determine current tax expense in the track record period, differences will arise between the tax expense or benefit (under the separate tax return basis) and taxes paid or received under the legal tax-sharing arrangement. These differences are normally treated as a capital contribution or distribution to or from the parent entity, and they are included in the reconciliation of invested capital between periods.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.101"></a>**A2.101** Management needs to give careful consideration to carrying forward tax losses, and it needs to ensure that these will be available to the new reporting entity before recording deferred tax assets.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.102"></a>**A2.102** The tax incurred method of determining tax expense might be appropriate if the parent recharged taxes to the entities that will comprise the new reporting entity.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.103"></a>**A2.103** Management should assess the recoverability of deferred tax assets, as normal, using the financial results of the new reporting entity for all periods presented. However, assumptions made in assessing the realisation of deferred tax assets and the establishment of uncertain tax positions should reflect the facts as they existed at the historical dates; they should not incorporate the benefit of hindsight.

[EX A2.103.1 – Allocation of tax charge](87.md#s369-EXA2.103.1)

 

<a id="s367-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_22"></a>
### Interest expense

 

<a id="s367-Para_A2.104"></a>**A2.104** Interest expense should be included in the combined financial statements of the new reporting entity if:

- the carve out entity has historically been allocated an interest charge;

- the new reporting entity will assume the debt associated with the interest charge;

- the debt will be repaid by the new reporting entity using the proceeds of an offering of securities; or

- the debt on the parent’s books specifically related to the activity of the new reporting entity.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.105"></a>**A2.105** Capital structures are usually complex in transactions involving a new reporting entity that needs to prepare combined financial statements. A regulator might not insist on including interest expense. However, it usually allows an interest charge to be included if a reasonable allocation of interest can be made, irrespective of whether interest was previously charged to the new reporting entity.

[EX A2.105.1 – Allocation of interest income and expense](87.md#s369-EXA2.105.1)

 

<a id="s367-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_23"></a>
### Pension plans

 

<a id="s367-Para_A2.106"></a>**A2.106** The employees of the new reporting entity might have participated and/or continue to participate in the parent’s employee benefit plans. It is rare that the new reporting entity will have its own defined benefit plans at the time of a transaction or that it will be legally assigned the pension liability for its own employees.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.107"></a>**A2.107** The appropriate treatment of the employee benefit plans in the combined financial statements will depend on several factors, the most significant of which will be the nature of the plans after the transaction. Two approaches are frequently observed, as set out in [paragraphs A2.108](87.md#s367-Para_A2.108) and [A2.109](87.md#s367-Para_A2.109).

<a id="s367-Para_A2.108"></a>**A2.108** The employee benefit plans of the parent might become, in effect, multi-employer plans, with the employees of the new reporting entity as participants in the multi-employer plans. Contributions to the plan are included in the profit or loss account of the new reporting entity, or it might be appropriate to allocate employee benefit expense to the new reporting entity.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.109"></a>**A2.109** Alternatively, a portion of the pension assets might be assigned to the new reporting entity, which assumes all existing obligations for the employees that are transferred to it, effectively creating a new defined benefit plan for the new reporting entity. This is seldom likely to have been effected by the date of the transaction, although it is likely to have been announced or agreed with employees. It might be appropriate to reflect pension assets and liabilities in the combined financial statements, with appropriate disclosure. Calculation of pension assets and liabilities in these circumstances is complex, and it might require the involvement of actuaries.

 

<a id="s367-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_24"></a>
### Impairments

<a id="s367-Para_A2.110"></a>**A2.110** The parent of the new reporting entity might have recorded impairment charges related to non-financial assets that will be included in the new reporting entity. Likewise, it might have avoided recording impairments, because of synergies in the wider group that will no longer exist after the transaction.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.111"></a>**A2.111** The new reporting entity will need to determine its cash-generating units (CGUs) for impairment testing of non-current assets, and groups of CGUs for impairment testing of goodwill and assets that benefit more than one CGU.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.112"></a>**A2.112** Management will need to test goodwill and other non-current assets with indefinite lives. It will need to separately assess if any events have occurred to the stand-alone operations that would trigger the need to perform a non-financial asset impairment test for each period covered by the combined financial statements. This assessment would be made without the benefit of hindsight.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.113"></a>**A2.113** Non-financial asset impairments should not be apportioned to the carve out entity; they should be based on the actual assets allocated to the carve out entity and the carve out entity’s stand-alone results.

<a id="s367-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_25"></a>
### Share-based payments

<a id="s367-Para_A2.114"></a>**A2.114** Share-based payments awarded to employees of the new reporting entity by a related party, or other holder of an economic interest in the new reporting entity (that is, parent, other group company or shareholder), as compensation for services provided to the entity are accounted for as share-based payment transactions, following the guidance in IFRS 2. This is required under IFRS, unless the transfer is clearly for a purpose other than compensation for services to the reporting entity, such as settlement of an obligation to the employee that is not related to employment.

<a id="s367-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_26"></a>
### Inter-company transactions and balances

 

<a id="s367-Para_A2.115"></a>**A2.115** Previous interactions between the new reporting entity and other entities in the group should be re-assessed for classification; they are likely to be ‘third party’ transactions in the combined financial statements.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.116"></a>**A2.116** Entities in the previous group will normally be regarded as related parties during the track record period, and so the requirements of IAS 24 should be applied.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.117"></a>**A2.117** Further to the additional disclosure requirements under IAS 24, some practical issues could arise:

- Previous trading balances might include trading and financing elements, and these should be separated in the new reporting entity’s balance sheet.

- Classification as non-current might change.

- Receivables from related parties might not be recoverable, because parental guarantees fell away.

- Additional liabilities might crystallise on separation from the previous group.

[EX A2.117.1 – Allocation of management fees and other expenses](87.md#s369-EXA2.117.1)

 

<a id="s367-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_27"></a>
### Transaction costs

<a id="s367-Para_A2.118"></a>**A2.118** Combined financial statements are usually prepared in accordance with a transaction of some kind, and so the issue of transaction costs will frequently arise.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.119"></a>**A2.119** There are three main issues, in practice, concerning transaction costs:

- the IPO costs that are eligible for capitalisation;

- the allocation of IPO costs among existing shareholders and shareholders contributing new funds; and

- whether transaction costs incurred in anticipation of an IPO can be deferred.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.120"></a>**A2.120** An IPO of the new reporting entity’s shares will often follow a group re-organisation. Management should make a clear distinction between group re-organisation costs and IPO costs. The latter only represent the incremental equity issuance costs; this will not include the costs of the re-organisation, such as staff redundancies, relocation, legal expenses and tax costs. Costs of a re-organisation or restructuring should be expensed as incurred.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.121"></a>**A2.121** New shares are issued to investors in an IPO to raise additional capital and, along with existing shares, they subsequently become listed on a stock exchange. Costs incurred in listing existing shares on a stock exchange are not transaction costs relating to the issue of an equity instrument. These costs are incurred to make the existing shares more marketable, and they do not relate to raising new capital.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.122"></a>**A2.122** Costs incurred in issuing new shares to raise capital (‘primary offering’) in an IPO are transaction costs of the equity instruments, and they are a deduction from equity.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.123"></a>**A2.123** Costs that are incurred to raise capital and to list existing shares are allocated between the new shares and the existing shares on a reasonable basis. Any costs incurred to list existing shares, or to list new shares arising from a demerger, are not equity transaction costs, and they are charged to the income statement.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.124"></a>**A2.124** Qualifying transaction costs incurred in anticipation of issuing equity shares can be deferred on the balance sheet until the transaction occurs, or they can be shown as a deduction from equity. Costs must be expensed if the transaction is not assessed as probable. Costs previously written off should not be reinstated.

<a id="s367-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_28"></a>
### Presentation of equity

<a id="s367-Para_A2.125"></a>**A2.125** The new reporting entity is seldom a separate legal entity throughout the entire track record period, and so it has no share capital and reserves in its own right. If the separation from the previous group has not finished, any equity represents the parent’s net investment in the new reporting entity. Typically, this is labelled ‘invested capital’ or ‘parent company investment’ in the combined financial statements.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.126"></a>**A2.126** If legal share capital exists, it would be shown separately in equity (in which case, the presentation of earnings per share is also meaningful – see [para A2.129](87.md#s367-Para_A2.129)).

<a id="s367-Para_A2.127"></a>**A2.127** Whether or not legal accounts (such as share capital or share premium), are shown, those ‘recyclable’ balances that require separate disclosure under other IFRSs should be disclosed separately (for example, hedging reserves).

<a id="s367-Para_A2.128"></a>**A2.128** Special attention should be given to outstanding balances that still exist between the new reporting entity and its parent. Balances without the characteristics of debt should be included as part of the invested capital.

<a id="s367-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_29"></a>
### Presentation of earnings per share

 

<a id="s367-Para_A2.129"></a>**A2.129** IAS 33 requires presentation of earnings per share (EPS) for an entity “that files, or is in the process of filing, its financial statements with a securities commission or other regulatory organisation for the purpose of issuing ordinary shares in a public market”.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.130"></a>**A2.130** Presentation is often less meaningful, because the combined financial statements are unlikely to have legal share capital (see [para A2.125](87.md#s367-Para_A2.125)). Some regulators accept omission of this disclosure in such a case. Once the shares are issued, EPS figures become meaningful for capital markets, and so they should be adjusted retrospectively. Some regulators, notably the SEC, require the shares issued to the existing investors, on formation of the combined entity legal structure, to be used as the denominator in calculating historical EPS.

[EX A2.130.1 – No EPS presented](87.md#s369-EXA2.130.1)

<a id="s367-Para_A2.131"></a>**A2.131** Care should be taken if the new reporting entity’s invested capital fluctuates significantly throughout the track record period, caused by, for example, share issuances for cash or management buy-out. Where it is practicable, management should highlight the impact of such transactions on the number of shares used, for the purpose of calculating the EPS figures.

 

<a id="s367-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_30"></a>
### Application of hindsight

 

<a id="s367-Para_A2.132"></a>**A2.132** The general principles of not applying hindsight and presenting the business as run by management at the time, mean that it is appropriate to keep comparative periods closed. For example, if a lawsuit is settled by the time the combined financial statements are prepared, the accruals that were made in the track record period are not ‘trued up’ to the actual settlement amount.

 

<a id="s367-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_31"></a>
### Cash flows

 

<a id="s367-Para_A2.133"></a>**A2.133** The presentation of the carve out entity's statement of cash flows can be challenging. For example, inter-company transactions that would not have been reflected in the consolidated statement of cash flows at the parent company level should be reflected in the cash flow statement at the carve out entity level. These inter-company transactions need to be properly classified in the combined financial statements.

 

<a id="s367-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_32"></a>
### Transition to IFRS

<a id="s367-Para_A2.134"></a>**A2.134** The requirements of IFRS 1 should be applied when financial statements are prepared for a new reporting entity. This guidance focuses on specific issues related to the preparation of combined financial statements, and it does not address the general requirements of IFRS 1 that are relevant for all first-time adopters.

There are two options set out in paragraph D16 of IFRS 1 for a subsidiary (including carve out entities) that adopts IFRS at a date later than the group headed by its parent (or entity that has significant influence or joint control over it). It can measure its assets and liabilities at either:

- the carrying amounts that would be included in the parent’s consolidated financial statements, based on the parent’s date of transition to IFRSs … ; or

- the carrying amounts required by IFRS 1, based on the subsidiary’s date of transition to IFRSs.

[IFRS 1 para D16].

<a id="s367-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_33"></a>
### Reconciliations

<a id="s367-Para_A2.135"></a>**A2.135** IFRS 1 requires reconciliations between previous GAAP and IFRS, explaining how the transition affected the financial position and performance of the reporting entity. A new reporting entity is unlikely to have previously prepared financial statements, and so no reconciliation can be prepared.

<a id="s367-Para_A2.136"></a>**A2.136** A new reporting entity that is spun off from an existing IFRS reporter would not have any reconciling items, because it can apply the same exemption that is available for a subsidiary transitioning after its parent has transitioned.

<a id="s367-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_34"></a>
### Opening balance sheet

<a id="s367-Para_A2.137"></a>**A2.137** IFRS 1 requires the preparation and presentation of an opening balance sheet as a starting point of the financial statements. This requirement is also applicable for combined financial statements.

<a id="s367-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_35"></a>
### Other complex areas

<a id="s367-Para_A2.138"></a>**A2.138** This guidance does not address complex areas of accounting that largely depend on individual facts and circumstances or are governed by local regulations (for example, calculation and presentation of non-controlling interest or foreign currency translation differences).

<a id="s368-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_36"></a>
## Step 5: Make clear disclosures

 

<a id="s368-Para_A2.139"></a>**A2.139** Clear and transparent disclosures are fundamental to the understanding of combined and carve out financial statements. This type of financial information is usually prepared to represent specific circumstances, and these are often governed by local regulatory requirements.

<a id="s368-Para_A2.140"></a>**A2.140** Disclosure notes should address at least:

- the purpose for which the combined financial statements are prepared;

- how the new reporting entity was identified and the significant judgements that were made; the boundary of the new reporting entity should be clearly described in the basis of preparation, including the explanation as to which entities are included in the combined financial statements;

- a list of combining entities;

- whether the combined financial statements are prepared in accordance with IFRS or a basis of preparation;

- the principal accounting policies followed in preparing the combined financial statements;

- how the allocation of assets, liabilities, income and expenses was performed;

- how inter-company transactions were reflected in the combined financial statements;

- how EPS was calculated, if applicable;

- the critical judgements and estimates involved in the preparation of combined financial statements; and

- disclosure requirements of all applicable IFRSs, if the combined financial statements claim compliance with IFRS.

### <a id="s369-FAQ_A2.47.1"></a>FAQ A2.47.1 – Can common management be a basis for common control?

 

Reference to standard: [A2.47](87.md#s365-Para_A2.47)  
Reference to standing text:   

  

 

A fund manager might be responsible for managing portfolios of different funds but often has no beneficial interests in those funds. The decision-making process requires the fund manager to request approval from the unit holders on major decisions. The funds are under common management, but there is no basis for aggregating all funds managed by the same fund manager.

 

The aggregated funds do not represent a reporting entity; fund holders or creditors of one fund do not have access to the assets of other funds, and it is not possible to hold a residual interest in aggregate of all of the funds.

### <a id="s369-FAQ_A2.49.1"></a>FAQ A2.49.1 – How might a reporting entity be established from entities that will be legally bound together through a re-organisation?

 

Reference to standard: [A2.49](87.md#s365-Para_A2.49)  
Reference to standing text:   

  

 

![](assets/226.png)

 

Entities A and B comprise a single business, operating independently from entity C, under individual X’s common control. The entities are legally unconnected, but they will form a new legal group through the insertion of a new company (NewCo) before an IPO. Although entities A and B were legally unconnected before the re-organisation, it might be possible to prepare combined financial statements for the new reporting entity. They were under common control for the entire historical track record period, they represent an area of economic activity and they will be legally bound together before the proposed IPO.

 

<a id="s369-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_41"></a>
### <a id="s369-FAQ_A2.49.2"></a>FAQ A2.49.2 – How might a reporting entity be established from entities that have been legally bound together under a contractual arrangement?

 

Reference to standard: [A2.49](87.md#s365-Para_A2.49)  
Reference to standing text:   

  

 

![](assets/227.png)

 

Entities A and B are separate businesses but have been managed together under a single contractual arrangement between the parent and two non-controlling interest (NCI) shareholders. Synergies are achieved by managing the two businesses together, and a contractual arrangement is necessary because the two NCI shareholders have different economic interests in their respective businesses. Combined financial statements for entities A and B can be prepared because the entities are under common control, and they have been legally bound together for the entire track record period under a contractual arrangement.

### <a id="s369-FAQ_A2.49.3"></a>FAQ A2.49.3 – How might a reporting entity be established from separate entities managed together as a single economic entity?

 

Reference to standard: [A2.49](87.md#s365-Para_A2.49)  
Reference to standing text:   

  

 

![](assets/228.png)

 

The parent’s chief operating decision-maker monitors the group’s performance based on its three business segments: entities A and B, which constitute one operating segment; and entity C and entity D, each of which is an operating segment. Each operating segment has its own management team responsible for operating activities, financial forecasts and performance.

 

Entities A and B are under common control and have been managed together, by the same segmental management team, as a single economic entity for the entire track record period. Therefore, combined financial statements can be prepared for entities A and B, because they can be seen as one reporting entity.

### <a id="s369-EXA2.67.1"></a>EX A2.67.1 – Historical information prepared in accordance with IFRS

 

Reference to standard: [A2.67](87.md#s366-Para_A2.67)  
Reference to standing text:   

  

 

**Example**

 

Group X intends to dispose of its sub-group Y, together with one other directly owned subsidiary of group X, subsidiary Z. Management prepares combined financial statements for sub-group Y and subsidiary Z.

 

Group X acquired sub-group Y four years ago; it did a purchase price allocation (PPA), which resulted in goodwill being recognised for the acquisition in group X’s consolidated financial statements, in accordance with IFRS.

 

**Analysis**

 

Management prepares combined financial statements using the amounts in group X’s consolidated financial statements for sub-group Y and subsidiary Z. The goodwill and other PPA adjustments that were reflected in group X’s consolidated financial statements are included in the sub-group financial statements.

 

The combined financial statements are based on financial statements that were prepared using only historical information, and it might be possible to claim compliance with IFRS.

 

<a id="s369-chap_Appendix_2_navPoint_45"></a>
### <a id="s369-EXA2.67.2"></a>EX A2.67.2 – Financial statements with significant adjustments

 

Reference to standard: [A2.67](87.md#s366-Para_A2.67)  
Reference to standing text:   

  

 

**Example**

 

Group X has two major areas of operations – in metals and coal. Management plans a demerger that will spin-off the metals business into a new sub-group and create two new listed entities: A Metals and A Coal.

 

Each shareholder’s investment in group X will be divided into separate investments in A Metals and A Coal.

 

**Analysis**

 

Group X has prepared the A Metals combined financial statements from historical information relating to A Metals, extracted from group X’s audited financial statements and the underlying accounting records.

 

The income statements and cash flows for A Metals have been presented on the basis that the demerger has occurred and that A Metals existed as a stand-alone group for the three-year track record period required by the listing authority. The financial information has been prepared incorporating the following adjustments:

 

- it excludes activities of the coal business that had been in the same legal entities as the metals business in certain jurisdictions;

- asset impairments that had been recognised during the track record period are reversed as an adjustment to opening retained earnings; and

- additional costs are recorded to present A Metals as if it had been operating as a separate listed entity rather than as part of group X, without consideration of group X’s actual expenses incurred.

 

The adjustments result in information that is prepared on a pro-forma basis and is not in accordance with IFRS.

### <a id="s369-FAQ_A2.81.1"></a>FAQ A2.81.1 – How might intangible assets be allocated in combined financial statements?

 

Reference to standard: [A2.81](87.md#s367-Para_A2.81)  
Reference to standing text:   

  

 

A new reporting entity (business units planned for sale) and several other business units of the same group used a brand name owned by the parent entity. The brand name is patented and legally owned by the parent. Should the brand name be included in the combined financial statements prepared in accordance with IFRS?

 

Scenario 1 – Buyer does not acquire the brand name

 

It would not be appropriate to include the brand name as an asset on the new reporting entity’s balance sheet, because the brand will not be sold to the buyer. If the parent previously charged the business units a royalty for the use of the brand name, the combined financial statements should include that expense. However, if the parent did not previously charge a royalty to the businesses, this fact should be disclosed, but no ‘as-if’ adjustment should be made; consideration should also be given to whether any amortisation of the brand at the parent level needs to be allocated to the new reporting entity

 

.Scenario 2 – Buyer acquires the brand name

 

If the new buyer acquires the brand name with the new reporting entity, it would be appropriate to include the parent’s historical cost of the brand name asset in the combined financial statements of the new reporting entity. The income statement should include the relevant cost of amortisation of the brand name; the carrying value of intangible assets, however, is frequently nil.

The following describes the most significant allocations and assumptions when preparing the combined financial statements for the Product A operations 20X5 and for the period January – May 20X6:

 

Allocations of certain group common services provided by Sigma, including financial and other support functions.

### <a id="s369-EXA2.117.1"></a>EX A2.117.1 – Allocation of management fees and other expenses

 

Reference to standard: [A2.117](87.md#s367-Para_A2.117)  
Reference to standing text:   

  

 

Extracts from Delta’s combined financial statements on demerger – 31 December 20X6

 

The combined financial statements have been prepared on a ‘carve out’ basis from the Delta PLC (‘Delta’) consolidated financial statements for the purposes of presenting the financial position, results of operations and cash flows of Epsilon on a stand-alone basis. The combined financial statements of Epsilon reflect assets, liabilities, revenues and expenses directly attributable to Express, including management fee allocations recognised on a historical basis in the accounting records of Delta on a legal entity basis. Although it is not possible to estimate the actual costs that would have been incurred if the services performed by Delta had been purchased from independent third parties, the allocations are considered by the directors of Delta and management of Epsilon to be reasonable. However, the financial position, results of operations and cash flows of Epsilon are not necessarily representative or indicative of those that would have been achieved if Epsilon had operated autonomously or as an entity independent from Delta.

 

**Management fees**

 

Delta uses a cost recovery mechanism to recover certain central management and other similar costs that it incurs at a corporate level. The management fees reflected in the combined financial statements are based on the amounts historically due, and they have been recorded in the accounts of the individual legal entities within Epsilon under the contractual cost recovery mechanism. An appropriate proportion of the remuneration of personnel for Delta and Epsilon, including their salaries and pension costs, is included in these management fees. These management fees have either been directly attributed to individual operations of Epsilon or, for costs incurred centrally, allocated between the relevant Delta businesses and Epsilon operations on an arm’s length basis. A complete discussion of the relationship that Epsilon has with Delta and other Delta entities, including a description of the costs that have historically been charged to Epsilon, is included in note X to these combined financial statements.

 

**Pension and post-retirement costs**

 

Epsilon operates a number of pension plans around the world. Delta is the sponsoring employer for two of the pension plans and, consequently, these pension plans qualify as Group plans for Epsilon, in accordance with paragraph 34(a) of IAS 19. Due to their qualification as Group plans, Epsilon recognises in the combined financial statements a cost equal to the contribution payable for the period.

 

**Interest**

 

The interest charge reflected in the combined financial statements is based on the interest charge historically incurred by the entities included in Epsilon on specific external borrowings or financing provided by other Delta companies.

 

**Taxation**

 

The tax charge attributable to Epsilon is based on the tax charge attributable to the individual entity or group of Delta entities in the relevant individual tax jurisdictions, on a separate return basis. Tax liabilities that might arise from any separation from Delta tax groups of the operations of Epsilon in specific countries have not been reflected in these combined financial statements.

 

**Goodwill**

 

Goodwill recorded at a consolidated Delta level, and attributable to Epsilon as a result of previous business combinations with parties outside the Delta group of companies, has been recorded in these combined financial statements.

 

**Share-based payments**

 

A number of Epsilon employees participate in Delta’s performance share schemes. For the purposes of these combined financial statements, transfers of Delta’s equity instruments to employees of Epsilon have been reflected as equity-settled, share-based payment transactions.
