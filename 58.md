## Overview of IAS 41 (paras 33.1 - 33.45)

 

<a id="s236-chap_33_navPoint_2"></a>
### Introduction

<a id="s236-Para_33.1"></a>**33.1** IAS 41 prescribes the accounting treatment for biological assets, except for bearer plants, during the period of growth, degeneration, production and procreation and for the initial measurement of agricultural produce at the point of harvest.

<a id="s236-Para_33.2"></a>**33.2**Bearer plants are included within IAS 16’s scope. The produce growing on these bearer plants are within IAS 41’s scope.

[IAS 41 para IN1].

<a id="s236-chap_33_navPoint_3"></a>
### Scope

 

<a id="s236-Para_33.3"></a>**33.3** IAS 41 applies to the following when they relate to agricultural activity:

- Biological assets, except for bearer plants.

- Agricultural produce at the point of harvest.

- Government grants related to biological assets.

[IAS 41 para 1].

<a id="s236-Para_33.4"></a>**33.4** A biological asset is defined as “a living animal or plant” and includes produce growing on bearer plants.

[IAS 41 para 5]. [IAS 41 para 5C].

[FAQ 33.4.1 – What are some examples of biological assets?](58.md#s237-FAQ_33.4.1)  
  
[FAQ 33.4.2 – Are all living organisms biological assets?](58.md#s237-FAQ_33.4.2)

<a id="s236-Para_33.5"></a>**33.5** For disclosure purposes, an entity is encouraged to distinguish between consumable and bearer biological assets or between mature and immature biological assets, as appropriate.

[IAS 41 para 43].

<a id="s236-Para_33.6"></a>**33.6** Consumable biological assets are those that are to be harvested as agricultural produce or sold as biological assets (for example, beef cattle or crops for harvest such as wheat). Bearer biological assets, on the other hand, are those from which other biological assets or agricultural produce are obtained (for example, dairy cattle or fruit trees).

[IAS 41 para 44].

<a id="s236-Para_33.7"></a>**33.7**Mature biological assets are those that have attained harvestable specifications (in the case of consumable biological assets) or are able to sustain regular harvests (in the case of bearer biological assets). Immature biological assets have not yet reached that stage.

[IAS 41 para 45].

<a id="s236-Para_33.8"></a>**33.8** Agricultural produce is defined as “the harvested produce of the entity’s biological assets”. [IAS 41 para 5]. IAS 41 applies to agricultural produce at the point of harvest.

[IAS 41 para 3].

[FAQ 33.8.1 – What are examples of biological assets and agricultural produce?](58.md#s237-FAQ_33.8.1)

<a id="s236-Para_33.9"></a>**33.9** Agricultural activity is defined as “the management by an entity of the biological transformation of biological assets for sale, into agricultural produce, or into additional biological assets”.

[IAS 41 para 5].

[FAQ 33.9.1 – What are examples of agricultural activity?](58.md#s237-FAQ_33.9.1)

<a id="s236-Para_33.10"></a>**33.10**Biological transformation is defined as “the processes of growth, degeneration, production, and procreation that cause qualitative or quantitative changes in a biological asset”.

[IAS 41 para 5].

[FAQ 33.10.1 – What is biological transformation?](58.md#s237-FAQ_33.10.1)

<a id="s236-Para_33.11"></a>**33.11**Agricultural activities are distinguished by the fact that management facilitates and manages biological transformation and is capable of measuring the change in the quality and quantity of biological assets.

[IAS 41 para 6].

<a id="s236-Para_33.12"></a>**33.12**Management of biological transformation normally takes the form of activity to enhance, or at least stabilise, the conditions necessary for the process to take place. This management distinguishes agricultural activity from other activities. For example, harvesting from unmanaged sources (such as ocean fishing) is not agricultural activity.

[IAS 41 para 6(b)].

[FAQ 33.12.1 – What are examples of management of biological transformation?](58.md#s237-FAQ_33.12.1)  
  
[EX 33.12.2 – Absence of management of biological transformation](58.md#s237-EX33.12.2)

<a id="s236-Para_33.13"></a>**33.13**The standard does not apply to the following:

- Land related to agricultural activity which is dealt with by IAS 16 or IAS 40.

- Right-of-use assets arising from a lease of land related to agricultural activity which are dealt with by IFRS 16.

- Intangible assets related to agricultural activity which are dealt with by IAS 38.

- Leases of bearer plants held by a lessee are within the scope of IFRS 16 but leases of biological assets other than bearer plants held by a lessee are within the scope of IAS 41.

- Agricultural produce after the point of harvest. It is likely that such produce will be inventory within the scope of IAS 2.

- Bearer plants related to agricultural activity which are dealt with by IAS 16. Produce growing on bearer plants remains within IAS 41’s scope.

- Government grants related to bearer plants which are dealt with by IAS 20.

[IAS 41 para 2]. [IAS 41 para 3]. [IAS 41 para 5C]. [IFRS 16 para 3(b)]. [IFRS 16 para BC68].

[EX 33.13.1 – Classification of agricultural land ultimately destined for sale](58.md#s237-EX33.13.1)  
  
[EX 33.13.2 – Classification of agricultural land where agricultural use is considered incidental](58.md#s237-EX33.13.2)  
  
[EX 33.13.3 – Logging road not expected to be used during more than one period](58.md#s237-EX33.13.3)  
  
[EX 33.13.4 – Logging road expected to be used during more than one period](58.md#s237-EX33.13.4)  
[EX 33.13.5 – Treatment of consumable biological assets after harvesting](58.md#s237-EX33.13.5)  
  
[EX 33.13.6 – Treatment of produce growing on bearer biological assets before and after harvesting](58.md#s237-EX33.13.6)

<a id="s236-Para_33.14"></a>**33.14** Contracts to sell biological assets are accounted for either under IFRS 9 or as executory contracts. Contracts fall within the scope of IFRS 9 if they meet the definition of a derivative or if they can be net settled and are not expected to result in physical delivery of goods to meet the entity's own purchase, sale or usage requirements.

[IFRS 9 para 2.5]. [IFRS 9 para 2.6].

[EX 33.14.1 – Contract within the scope of IFRS 9](58.md#s237-EX33.14.1)  
[EX 33.14.2 – Contract outside the scope of IFRS 9](58.md#s237-EX33.14.2)  
[EX 33.14.3 – Existence of an onerous contract](58.md#s237-EX33.14.3)

<a id="s236-Para_33.15"></a>**33.15**Common activities in the agricultural sector include the use of grower contracts or outsourcing contracts whereby the cultivation of biological assets are outsourced to farmers. IAS 41 does not deal specifically with grower contracts or outsourcing contracts. Consideration should be given to the requirements of:

- IFRS 10, to establish whether the farming operation should be consolidated by someone other than the farmer; see [chapter 26 para 58](45.md#s170-Para_26.58);

- IFRS 16, to assess whether the outsourcing contract contains a lease to which IFRS 16 applies; see [chapter 15 para 5](33.md#s107-Para_15.5); and

- IFRS 15, to establish whether a sale can be recognised on transfer of the biological assets to the grower or farmer, for example the sale of seeds and related products with an obligation to repurchase the harvested produce; for revenue recognition criteria, see [chapter 11 para 153](19.md#s69-Para_11.153) and, for agent-principal assessment, see [chapter 11 para 264](19.md#s71-Para_11.264).

 

<a id="s236-chap_33_navPoint_4"></a>
### Recognition

 

<a id="s236-Para_33.16"></a>**33.16**Biological assets, including produce growing on a bearer plant and agricultural produce, should be recognised when:

- an entity controls the asset as a result of past events;

- it is probable that future economic benefits associated with the asset will flow to the entity; and

- the fair value or cost of the asset can be measured reliably

[IAS 41 para 10].

[EX 33.16.1 – Point of recognition for offspring of biological assets](58.md#s237-EX33.16.1)  
[FAQ 33.16.2 – When is the point of recognition of bearer biological produce?](58.md#s237-FAQ_33.16.2)

 

<a id="s236-chap_33_navPoint_5"></a>
### Measurement

 

<a id="s236-Para_33.17"></a>**33.17** Biological assets, including produce growing on a bearer plant, should be measured, both at initial recognition and at each subsequent reporting date, at fair value less costs to sell, except where fair value cannot be reliably measured. The fair value model accrues the additional value related to the biological asset as biological transformation takes place, rather than at the time of harvest.

[IAS 41 para 12].

[FAQ 33.17.1 – How is the fair value of bearer produce ascertained?](58.md#s237-FAQ_33.17.1)  
[FAQ 33.17.2 – Do you include tax cash flows in the fair value model?](58.md#s237-FAQ_33.17.2)

<a id="s236-Para_33.18"></a>**33.18** At the point of harvest, agricultural produce should be measured at fair value less costs to sell. This amount will then represent cost for the purposes of IAS 2 or another applicable standard.

[IAS 41 para 13].

<a id="s236-Para_33.19"></a>**33.19**Costs to sell are the incremental costs incurred in selling the asset, and they exclude finance costs and income tax expense.

[IAS 41 para 5].

[FAQ 33.19.1 – What are costs to sell?](58.md#s237-FAQ_33.19.1)

<a id="s236-Para_33.20"></a>**33.20** Fair value, in the context of IAS 41, has the same meaning as in IFRS 13 – that is, “the price that would be received to sell an asset or paid to transfer a liability in an orderly transaction between market participants at the measurement date”. [IAS 41 para 8]. Guidance that is specific to the fair value measurement of biological assets and agricultural produce is contained in [chapter 5 para 137](11.md#s34-Para_5.137).

<a id="s236-Para_33.21"></a>**33.21** Gains or losses that arise on measuring biological assets or agricultural produce at fair value less costs to sell are recognised in the income statement in the period in which they arise.

[IAS 41 para 26]. [IAS 41 para 28].

[EX 33.21.1 – Recognition of valuation gains and losses](58.md#s237-EX33.21.1)  
[FAQ 33.21.2 – How do you account for subsequent expenditure?](58.md#s237-FAQ_33.21.2)  
[FAQ 33.21.3 – How do emission trading schemes affect a forest's fair value?](58.md#s237-FAQ_33.21.3)

 

<a id="s236-chap_33_navPoint_6"></a>
### Inability to measure fair value reliably

 

<a id="s236-Para_33.22"></a>**33.22**There is a presumption in IAS 41 that fair value can be measured reliably for a biological asset. This presumption can be rebutted only on initial recognition. Therefore, measurement at cost is permissible only when a reliable measure of fair value is unavailable at the point of initial recognition. An entity that has measured biological assets at fair value less costs to sell cannot revert to a cost model if a reliable measure of fair value ceases to be available.

[IAS 41 para 31].

[EX 33.22.1 – Disruption of an active market after initial recognition](58.md#s237-EX33.22.1)

<a id="s236-Para_33.23"></a>**33.23**When fair value cannot be reliably established, biological assets should be measured at cost less accumulated depreciation and impairment losses, if any. [IAS 41 para 30]. Cost, depreciation and impairment losses should be determined in accordance with IAS 2, IAS 16 and IAS 36 as appropriate. [IAS 41 para 33]. Refer to [chapter 25 para 18](44.md#s165-Para_25.18), [chapter 22 para 79](39.md#s143-Para_22.79) and [chapter 24 para 6](41.md#s154-Para_24.6) respectively.

<a id="s236-Para_33.24"></a>**33.24** Although a reliable measure of fair value might not be available at the point of initial recognition, it might subsequently become available (for example, if an active market opens). Biological assets are measured at fair value less costs to sell from the point at which the reliable measure of fair value becomes available.

[IAS 41 para 30].

<a id="s236-Para_33.25"></a>**33.25**Biological assets that are measured at fair value less costs to sell are excluded from the measurement provisions of IFRS 5. [IFRS 5 para 5]. However, where biological assets are classified as held for sale (or are included in a disposal group that is classified as held for sale), fair value is presumed to be reliably measurable. Biological assets previously measured at cost must be carried at fair value less costs to sell where the classification criteria of IFRS 5 are met. [IAS 41 para 30]. Refer to [chapter 30 para 20](53.md#s218-Para_30.20).

<a id="s236-Para_33.26"></a>**33.26**IAS 41 presumes that the fair value of agricultural produce at the point of harvest will always be measurable. The cost approach is not permissible.

[IAS 41 para 32].

 

<a id="s236-chap_33_navPoint_7"></a>
### Government grants

 

<a id="s236-Para_33.27"></a>**33.27**IAS 41 contains guidance on recognising, in profit and loss, grants receivable in respect of biological assets measured at fair value less costs to sell. These grants are outside IAS 20’s scope.

[IAS 20 para 2].

<a id="s236-Para_33.28"></a>**33.28**An unconditional grant is recognised as income when it becomes receivable. A conditional grant, on the other hand, should be recognised as income only when the conditions are satisfied. The threshold is thus higher than under IAS 20 which requires recognition when there is reasonable assurance that the conditions will be complied with.

[IAS 41 para 34]. [IAS 41 para 35].

[EX 33.28.1 – Government grant subject to annual planting](58.md#s237-EX33.28.1)  
[EX 33.28.2 – Government grant paid up-front](58.md#s237-EX33.28.2)  
[EX 33.28.3 – Government grant subject to certification](58.md#s237-EX33.28.3)

<a id="s236-Para_33.29"></a>**33.29**Where biological assets are carried at cost less accumulated depreciation and impairment losses, the recognition in profit or loss of any government grants related to those assets is dealt with in accordance with IAS 20. Grants that do not relate specifically to biological assets (for example, a grant to build a new milking stable) fall within IAS 20’s scope, refer to [chapter 17 para 2](37.md#s125-Para_17.2).

[IAS 41 para 37].

<a id="s236-Para_33.30"></a>**33.30**A number of jurisdictions have introduced emission trading schemes relating to agricultural activities, particularly forestry. There is no detailed guidance on accounting for emission trading schemes relating to agricultural activities.

 

<a id="s236-chap_33_navPoint_8"></a>
### Accounting for bearer plants

 

<a id="s236-Para_33.31"></a>**33.31**A bearer plant is defined as “a living plant that:

- is used in the production or supply of agricultural produce;

- is expected to bear produce for more than one period; and

- has a remote likelihood of being sold as agricultural produce, except for incidental scrap sales.”

[IAS 41 para 5].

<a id="s236-Para_33.32"></a>**33.32**The following are not bearer plants:

- Plants grown to be harvested and sold as produce, such as lumber.

- Annual crops which do not bear produce for more than one period, such as maize and wheat.

- Plants cultivated to produce agricultural produce, where the bearer plant will also be harvested and sold as agricultural produce (for example, trees that are cultivated both for their fruit and their lumber). If the sales from the wood are incidental scrap sales, the tree still meets the definition of a bearer plant.

[IAS 41 para 5A].

<a id="s236-Para_33.33"></a>**33.33**Bearer plants that no longer bear produce are commonly cut down and sold as scrap at the end of their life. Such incidental scrap sales would not prevent the plant from being a bearer plant.

[IAS 41 para 5B].

[FAQ 33.33.1 – What are considered bearer plants?](58.md#s237-FAQ_33.33.1)

<a id="s236-Para_33.34"></a>**33.34**Biological assets that meet the definition of ‘bearer plants’ are measured either at cost or revalued amounts, less accumulated depreciation and impairment losses under IAS 16.

[IAS 16 para 29]. [IAS 16 para 30]. [IAS 16 para 31].

[FAQ 33.34.1 – How do you measure bearer plants?](58.md#s237-FAQ_33.34.1)

<a id="s236-Para_33.35"></a>**33.35**Immature bearer plants are similar to an item of property, plant and equipment being constructed before its intended use and are measured at accumulated cost until the bearer plants are in the location and condition necessary for them to be capable of operating in the manner intended by management (that is, when bearer plants reach maturity).

[IAS 16 para 22A].

[FAQ 33.35.1 – How do you determine when bearer plants reach maturity?](58.md#s237-FAQ_33.35.1)

<a id="s236-Para_33.36"></a>**33.36**The aggregate carrying value of biological assets within IAS 41’s scope should be presented separately on the face of the balance sheet.

[IAS 1 para 54(f)].

<a id="s236-chap_33_navPoint_10"></a>
### Presentation - Income statement

 

<a id="s236-Para_33.37"></a>**33.37**Disclosure of the aggregate gain or loss arising during the current period on initial recognition of biological assets and agricultural produce, and from the change in fair value less costs to sell of biological assets, is required. [IAS 41 para 40]. There is no detailed guidance on where the aggregate gain or loss should be presented, so this disclosure can be made on the face of the income statement or in the notes to the financial statements. IAS 1 requires separate presentation on the face of the income statement where such presentation is relevant to an understanding of an entity’s financial performance.

[IAS 1 para 85].

[FAQ 33.37.1 – Where should gains or loss be presented in the income statement?](58.md#s237-FAQ_33.37.1)  
[FAQ 33.37.2 – Should gains or losses be disaggregated?](58.md#s237-FAQ_33.37.2)

<a id="s236-Para_33.38"></a>**33.38**Agricultural activity is often exposed to climatic, disease and other natural risks, which could result in a material item of income or expense. [IAS 41 para 53]. The nature and amount of that item should be presented separately on the face of the income statement or in the notes, in accordance with IAS 1.

[IAS 1 para 97].

 

<a id="s236-chap_33_navPoint_11"></a>
### Disclosure

 

<a id="s236-Para_33.39"></a>**33.39**IAS 41 includes a broad range of disclosures relating to biological assets. Qualitative disclosures include a description of each group of its biological assets, the nature of the entity’s activities involving each group of biological assets and entity’s financial risk management strategies related to agricultural activity.

[IAS 41 para 41]. [IAS 41 para 46]. [IAS 41 para 49(c)].

<a id="s236-Para_33.40"></a>**33.40**Quantitative disclosures include non-financial measures or estimates of physical quantities of biological assets and a reconciliation of changes in the carrying amounts of biological assets, either in total or per group of biological assets if this gives more relevant information. This reconciliation should show, amongst other things, fair value gains or losses, purchases, sales and decreases due to harvest.

[IAS 41 para 46]. [IAS 41 para 50].

<a id="s236-Para_33.41"></a>**33.41**The carrying value of each group of biological assets does not need to be disclosed separately, but such disclosure is encouraged.

[IAS 41 para 43].

<a id="s236-Para_33.42"></a>**33.42**An entity is encouraged to disclose, by group or otherwise, the amount of the change in fair value less costs to sell included in profit or loss due to physical changes and the amount due to price changes. However, the standard acknowledges that this information is generally less useful where the production cycle is less than one year.

[IAS 41 para 51].

<a id="s236-Para_33.43"></a>**33.43**The standard requires specific disclosures where biological assets are measured at cost less any accumulated depreciation and impairment losses. The disclosures include, amongst other things, a description of the biological assets, an explanation of why fair value cannot be measured reliably, the depreciation method and useful lives used, and the gross carrying amount and accumulated depreciation at the beginning and end of the period.

[IAS 41 para 54]. [IAS 41 para 55].

<a id="s236-Para_33.44"></a>**33.44**If the fair value of biological assets previously measured using the cost model becomes reliably measurable during the period, there should be disclosure of a description of the biological assets, an explanation of why fair value has become reliably measurable, and the effect of the change.

[IAS 41 para 56].

<a id="s236-Para_33.45"></a>**33.45**Other disclosures include biological assets whose title is restricted or pledged as security, the amount of commitments for development or acquisitions, the entity's financial risk management strategies related to agricultural activity, and details about government grants related to agricultural activity, and details about government grants related to agricultural activity.

[IAS 41 para 49]. [IAS 41 para 57].

#### <a id="s237-FAQ_33.4.1"></a>FAQ 33.4.1 – What are some examples of biological assets?

 

Reference to standard: IAS 41 para 5  
  Reference to standing text: [33.4](58.md#s236-Para_33.4)  

  

  
 Biological assets include, amongst others, the following:

- Sheep, pigs, beef cattle, dairy cattle, poultry and fish.

- Trees in a plantation.

- Plants for harvest (such as wheat, maize and vegetables).

- Trees, plants and bushes from which agricultural produce is harvested (such as fruit trees, tea bushes, vines, tobacco plants and sugar cane).

Bearer plants are accounted for under IAS 16. All other biological assets when they relate to agricultural activity are accounted for under IAS 41.

 

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1721363928"></a>
#### <a id="s237-FAQ_33.4.2"></a>FAQ 33.4.2 – Are all living organisms biological assets?

 

Reference to standard: IAS 41 para 5  
  Reference to standing text: [33.4](58.md#s236-Para_33.4)  

  

  
Apart from obvious agricultural organisms, are other living organisms (such as cultures, cells, bacteria and viruses used in the pharmaceutical and biotechnology industries) biological assets?

Generally, such organisms would not be regarded as animals or plants under either the common or scientific usages of those words. The Collins English Dictionary defines a plant as "any living organism that typically synthesises its food from inorganic substances, lacks specialised sense organs, and has no powers of locomotion". Arguably, such a definition could encompass cultures, etc.

However, agricultural activity is the management by an entity of the biological transformation of biological assets for sale, into agricultural produce, or into additional biological assets. It is implicit throughout the standard that the kind of activities anticipated to be within its scope are ‘normal’, commonly understood agricultural activities. There is a further implication in IAS 41 that it applies to homogeneous (or, at least, highly comparable) assets or groups of assets. Many of the organisms used in the pharmaceutical and biotechnology industries are unique, or even patented, and therefore do not sit comfortably within the standard’s requirements.

Cultures, cells, bacteria, viruses and similar organisms used in the pharmaceutical and biotechnology industries are not considered to be biological assets that fall within IAS 41’s scope.

Also, the development of organisms for research purposes does not qualify as agricultural activity, as they are not being developed for sale or for transformation into agricultural produce or additional biological assets.

However, if a pharmaceutical or biotechnology company does undertake agricultural activity (for example, it grows plants from which particular drugs are developed or it develops cultures for use in dairy products), that activity will fall within IAS 41’s scope.

#### <a id="s237-FAQ_33.9.1"></a>FAQ 33.9.1 – What are examples of agricultural activity?

 

Reference to standard: IAS 41 para 5  
  Reference to standing text: [33.9](58.md#s236-Para_33.9)  

  

  
Examples of agricultural activity include:

- Raising livestock, fish or poultry.

- Stud farms (for example, breeding horses or cattle).

- Forestry.

- Cultivating vineyards, orchards or plantations.

- Floriculture.

 

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1711759560"></a>
#### <a id="s237-FAQ_33.10.1"></a>FAQ 33.10.1 – What is biological transformation?

 

Reference to standard: IAS 41 para 5  
  Reference to standing text: [33.10](58.md#s236-Para_33.10)  

  

  
Biological transformation is a natural change in a biological asset. It includes growth of living animals or plants, reduction in output due to age or disease, and the production of new biological assets through a managed reproductive programme. The following are examples of biological transformation in the context of cattle:

- Calves are born (procreation).

- Calves grow into mature cattle (growth).

- Dairy cattle yield milk (production).

- Beef cattle are reared for meat (production).

All of these changes are observable, measurable and have a direct relationship to future economic benefits. Hence, biological transformation has an effect on the value of biological assets.

 

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1711760712"></a>
#### <a id="s237-FAQ_33.12.1"></a>FAQ 33.12.1 – What are examples of management of biological transformation?

 

Reference to standard: IAS 41 para 6(b)  
  Reference to standing text: [33.12](58.md#s236-Para_33.12)  

  

  
The following are examples of management of biological transformation:

- Providing shelter and food for cattle as well as maintaining their health.

- Growing fish in net cages for subsequent slaughter or sale.

- A managed breeding programme carried out to produce animals for sale.

 

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1711778760"></a>
#### <a id="s237-EX33.12.2"></a>EX 33.12.2 – Absence of management of biological transformation

 

Reference to standard: IAS 41 para 6(b)  
  Reference to standing text: [33.12](58.md#s236-Para_33.12)  

  

  
A company manages a game park in South Africa. It has stocked the park with a range of wild animals indigenous to South Africa, as well as exotic animals from other continents.

The game park offers customers guided safari-type tours. The park is also used for incidental hunting as part of a culling programme. It is run with a view to profit, but this does not represent agricultural activity as there is, in this case, no management of the transformation of the biological assets; only control of the number of animals. Hence, the game park does not fall within IAS 41’s scope.

[IAS 41 para 6].

Similarly, zoos, aquariums and other parks where animals are held mainly for recreational purposes rather than agricultural activity would not be within IAS 41’s scope. The breeding that takes place is incidental to the main activity of providing a recreational facility. It is likely that such animals should be accounted for as tangible assets in the scope of IAS 16 (refer to [chapter 22 para 8](39.md#s141-Para_22.8)).

#### <a id="s237-EX33.13.1"></a>EX 33.13.1 – Classification of agricultural land ultimately destined for sale

 

Reference to standard: IAS 41 para 2  
   Reference to standing text: [33.13](58.md#s236-Para_33.13)  

  

An entity purchases land that it cultivates for agricultural purposes. The land will be used to grow various crops such as sugar cane, cotton and corn. The entity’s business plan is to continue with agricultural activity for a number of years, which will achieve two strategic objectives: first, to generate cash flows to fund investments; and, secondly, to demonstrate the viability of the land for agricultural purposes. At the end of the period, the land will be sold, in lots, to third parties.

While the land is being used for agricultural purposes, it should be classified as property, plant and equipment. A classification as non-current assets held for sale is only appropriate when the held for sale criteria in IFRS 5 are met. If a transfer to investment property is appropriate, the criteria governing transfers in IAS 40 should be complied with.

 

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1711544904"></a>
#### <a id="s237-EX33.13.2"></a>EX 33.13.2 – Classification of agricultural land where agricultural use is considered incidental

 

Reference to standard: IAS 41 para 2  
  Reference to standing text: [33.13](58.md#s236-Para_33.13)  

  

  
Entity A purchases vacant land that is to be used for the construction of a shopping mall. Permissions and administrative approvals, which need to be obtained prior to the commencement of construction, are expected to take up to 18 months to obtain. Entity A uses the land for agricultural activity for the period prior to obtaining the required approvals.

The agricultural use of the land can be seen as incidental to the principal objective of building a shopping mall. Classification of the land as investment property might be appropriate from initial recognition.

 

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1712512120"></a>
#### <a id="s237-EX33.13.3"></a>EX 33.13.3 – Logging road not expected to be used during more than one period

 

Reference to standard: IAS 41 para 2  
   Reference to standing text: [33.13](58.md#s236-Para_33.13)  

  

  
 Entities in the forestry industry often construct roads to provide them with access to their forest holdings. These roads are typically referred to as ‘logging roads’.

An entity incurs costs in the construction of a temporary logging road that will be discarded once the forest area to which it provides access is cleared. Clearance of the land accessed by the road will take place over a three month period. The entity prepares financial statements annually, and the three months fall within one accounting period.

Any costs incurred in the construction of the logging road should be expensed as incurred, because the entity does not expect to use the logging road during more than one period as required by IAS 16.

 

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1712516472"></a>
#### <a id="s237-EX33.13.4"></a>EX 33.13.4 – Logging road expected to be used during more than one period

 

Reference to standard: IAS 41 para 2  
  Reference to standing text: [33.13](58.md#s236-Para_33.13)  

  

  
 Entities in the forestry industry often construct roads to provide them with access to their forest holdings. These roads are typically referred to as ‘logging roads’.

An entity incurs costs in the construction of a logging road that is expected to provide future access to the entity’s forests. The road is expected to be used for a period of 10 years.

The costs incurred in the construction of the logging road should be capitalised as an asset under IAS 16 and should be depreciated over the estimated useful life of 10 years. This is because the road will be used during more than one period and will enable the entity to access the future economic benefits embodied in its forests.

 

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1712528120"></a>
#### <a id="s237-EX33.13.5"></a>EX 33.13.5 – Treatment of consumable biological assets after harvesting

 

Reference to standard: IAS 41 para 3  
  Reference to standing text: [33.13](58.md#s236-Para_33.13)  

  

  
Entity A raises cattle, slaughters them at its abattoirs and sells the carcasses to the local meat market.

The cattle are biological assets while they are living animals. When they are slaughtered, biological transformation ceases and the carcasses meet the definition of agricultural produce. Hence, entity A should account for the cattle in accordance with IAS 41. At the point of harvest, the fair value less costs to sell of the carcasses is taken as the cost of the inventory for the purpose of IAS 2, which applies thereafter.

[IAS 41 para 13]. [IAS 2 para 20].

 

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1712534264"></a>
#### <a id="s237-EX33.13.6"></a>EX 33.13.6 – Treatment of produce growing on bearer biological assets before and after harvesting

 

Reference to standard: IAS 41 para 3  
   Reference to standing text: [33.13](58.md#s236-Para_33.13)  

  

  
Entity B is involved in planting vines, harvesting grapes and producing wine.

While the grapes (the produce) grow on the vines, they are biological assets. When the entity harvests the grapes, their biological transformation ceases and they become agricultural produce.

Entity B should account for the grapes on the vine in accordance with IAS 41. At the point of harvest, the harvested grapes should be recognised at fair value less costs to sell and treated as inventory in accordance with IAS 2.

The exclusion of agricultural produce, after the point of harvest, from IAS 41’s scope means that the standard does not deal with activity such as the processing of grapes into wine or raisins. The Board considered including such activity within IAS 41’s scope, but concluded that it could not be differentiated with sufficient clarity from other manufacturing processes.

[IAS 41 App B para B11].

 

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1712548472"></a>
#### <a id="s237-EX33.14.1"></a>EX 33.14.1 – Contract within the scope of IFRS 9

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para 2.6(b) Reference to standing text: [33.14](58.md#s236-Para_33.14)  

  

Entity A grows cocoa beans. At the beginning of the cocoa growing season, it entered into a contract to sell 100 tonnes of cocoa beans at a fixed price. Entity A has a past practice of settling similar contracts net, because it has entered into the contract as part of its hedging strategy and has no intention of making a physical delivery of the cocoa beans to this counterparty.

Entity A has a contract to sell a non-financial item. The contract does not qualify for the ‘own use’ exemption in paragraph 2.5 of IFRS 9, because entity A has a past practice of settling similar contracts net. [IFRS 9 para 2.6(b)]. Hence, this and all similar contracts fall within the scope of IFRS 9 and are measured at fair value in accordance with that standard.

 

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1712558328"></a>
#### <a id="s237-EX33.14.2"></a>EX 33.14.2 – Contract outside the scope of IFRS 9

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para 2.6(b)Reference to standing text: [33.14](58.md#s236-Para_33.14)  

  

Entity B grows sugar beet. At the beginning of the growing season, it entered into a contract to sell its entire harvest to a local sugar processor at a fixed price.

The contract is intended to be settled by the physical delivery of sugar beet in accordance with entity B's normal sales practice. There is no history of entity B settling similar contracts net. Accordingly, this contract falls outside the scope of IFRS 9, so it is not measured at fair value. However, such a contract might meet the definition of an onerous contract under IAS 37 and would be measured in accordance with that standard (refer to [chapter 16 para 71](36.md#s120-Para_16.71)). The existence of a contract, whether onerous or not, does not affect the biological asset's fair value.

 

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1712570872"></a>
#### <a id="s237-EX33.14.3"></a>EX 33.14.3 – Existence of an onerous contract

 

Reference to standard: IAS 37 para 66  
   Reference to standing text: [33.14](58.md#s236-Para_33.14)  

  

Entity X grows salmon commercially for sale. It has entered into a contract to deliver the salmon in the future at a fixed price. At the reporting date, the contract price is lower than the current forward price used in the fair value less costs to sell discounted cash flow calculation of the salmon. The contract qualifies for the ‘own use’ exemption in IFRS 9.

Does the existence of a fixed price sale contract, at a price below the current forward price of the biological asset, indicate that the contract might be onerous?

An onerous contract might exist. An assessment will have to be performed by including the fair value less costs to sell of the biological asset, as it is measured at the reporting date, as one of the necessary costs to fulfil the contract. The implication is that the entity might simultaneously have a biological asset measured at fair value less costs to sell and a liability for an onerous contract. Note that the fixed price in the sales contract can not be used to measure the fair value of the biological asset. [IAS 41 para 16]. Since the measurement under IAS 37 for onerous contracts only includes unavoidable costs, it will normally differ conceptually from the fair value measurement under IFRS 13.

### Recognition

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1712586104"></a>
#### <a id="s237-EX33.16.1"></a>EX 33.16.1 – Point of recognition for offspring of biological assets

 

Reference to standard: IAS 41 paras 10, 11  
  Reference to standing text: [33.16](58.md#s236-Para_33.16)  

  

A pregnant ewe is a biological asset, as defined by IAS 41. Its offspring will also be biological assets, but they will not be recognised as separate assets until the recognition criteria in paragraph 10 are satisfied. Control of livestock may be evidenced by legal ownership and the branding or otherwise marking of the animal on acquisition, birth or weaning.

[IAS 41 para 11].

Control of new-born lambs would therefore be evidenced on birth and not only on weaning. Separate biological assets will be recognised when the lambing has proved successful and the offspring are healthy. The value of the unborn lamb will affect the value of the ewe, and the ewe’s fair value will decrease after lambing.

 

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1712590712"></a>
#### <a id="s237-FAQ_33.16.2"></a>FAQ 33.16.2 – When is the point of recognition of bearer biological produce?

 

Reference to standard: IAS 41 para 10  
   Reference to standing text: [33.16](58.md#s236-Para_33.16)  

  

  
IAS 41 provides no specific guidance for produce growing on bearer plants, other than the general recognition criteria. Bearer produce is therefore likely to be recognised immediately after the preceding harvest. The entity controls the bearer produce, because it controls the bearer plant. The previous harvest is evidence that there will be future economic benefit. The fair value can be measured reliably in most cases, but could be close to zero. We would not expect a material gain on initial recognition of bearer produce immediately after the last harvest.

Without any other evidence, fair value of the bearer produce would accrue evenly between harvests, taking into account major cash outflows related to periodic activities including fertilising, pruning or other value increasing activities. However, if evidence of specific growing cycles can be obtained, the fair value of the bearer produce could accrue exponentially.

For example, fresh fruit bunches growing on palm oil plants should be fair valued while they grow and not only once it is harvested.

### Measurement

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1712599416"></a>
#### <a id="s237-FAQ_33.17.1"></a>FAQ 33.17.1 – How is the fair value of bearer produce ascertained?

Reference to standard: IAS 41 para 12  
  Reference to standing text: [33.17](58.md#s236-Para_33.17)  

  

  
A cash flow model is the most likely valuation method for bearer produce. The cash flow model should include all directly attributable cash inflows and outflows. The inflows will be the expected price in the market of the harvested produce. The outflows will be those incurred in growing the asset and getting it to market (for example, direct labour, fertiliser and transport to market). Contributory asset charges will be included for both the land and the bearer plant if they are owned by the entity. This ‘notional’ rent removes cash flows attributable to those assets, so the remaining value relates solely to the produce. On day 1 after the previous harvest, the next harvest is likely to have a fair value close to zero.

#### <a id="s237-FAQ_33.19.1"></a>FAQ 33.19.1 – What are costs to sell?

 

Reference to standard: IAS 41 para 5  
Reference to standing text: [33.19](58.md#s236-Para_33.19)  

  

  
Costs to sell are the incremental costs incurred in selling the asset and include:

- commissions to brokers and dealers;

- levies by regulatory agencies and commodity exchanges; and

- transfer taxes and duties.

The word ‘incremental’ in the definition of ‘costs to sell’ excludes costs that are included in the fair value measurement of a biological asset, such as transport costs.

[IAS 41 App B para B22].

The relevant costs to sell are those that would be incurred when selling the biological asset in its current state. If fair value is determined based on a cash flow model, costs to sell the final produce would also be included as a cash outflow. When measuring agricultural produce, there will only be one ‘costs to sell’.

#### <a id="s237-EX33.21.1"></a>EX 33.21.1 – Recognition of valuation gains and losses

 

Reference to standard: IAS 41 paras 26, 28  
   Reference to standing text: [33.21](58.md#s236-Para_33.21)  

  

  
This example illustrates that gains and losses can arise on initial recognition of both biological assets and agricultural produce.

An entity purchased 100 beef cattle at an auction for C100,000 on 30 June 20X7. Subsequent transportation costs were C1,000. The entity would have to incur the same transportation costs if it had sold its cattle in this auction. In addition, there would be a 2% auctioneer’s fee on the market price of the cattle payable by the seller. The entity also incurred C500 on veterinary expenses.

On 31 December 20X7, the market value of the cattle in the most relevant market increases to C110,000. Transportation costs of C1,000 would have to be incurred by the seller to get the cattle to the relevant market. An auctioneer’s fee of 2% on the market price of the cattle would be payable by the seller.

On 1 March 20X8, the entity sold 18 cattle at auction for C20,000 and incurred transportation charges of C150. In addition, there was a 2% auctioneer’s fee on the market price of the cattle paid by the seller.

On 15 June 20X8, the fair value of the 82 cattle was C82,820. 42 cattle were slaughtered on that day, with a total slaughter cost of C4,200. The total market price of the carcasses on that day was C48,300, and the estimated transportation cost to sell the carcasses is C420. No other selling costs are expected.

On 30 June 20X8, the market price of the remaining 40 cattle was C44,800. The estimated transportation cost is C400. In addition, there would be a 2% auctioneer’s fee on the market price of the cattle payable by the seller.

The entity applies the fair value model to recognise biological assets, as required by the standard, and reports on 30 June and 31 December each year and determines fair value on these dates.

#### <a id="s237-FAQ_33.21.2"></a>FAQ 33.21.2 – How do you account for subsequent expenditure?

 

Reference to standard: IAS 41 para B62  
  Reference to standing text: [33.21](58.md#s236-Para_33.21)  

  

IAS 41 does not explicitly prescribe how to account for subsequent expenditure related to biological assets. Such costs could include feeding, veterinary services, planting, weeding, irrigation, fertiliser, and harvesting and slaughtering costs. The Board did not prescribe the accounting for subsequent expenditure as it was seen as unnecessary with a fair value measurement approach. Costs incurred affect the fair value and, ordinarily, costs and fair value changes are both taken to profit or loss.

[IAS 41 App B para B62].

Many agricultural businesses follow a policy of capitalising some of these costs, particularly those relating to the development of immature plants or livestock up to the point at which they are productive. Also, immature bearer plants are accounted for under IAS 16 similar to an item of property, plant and equipment being constructed before its intended use and are measured at accumulated costs using the principles of IAS 16.

The treatment of subsequent expenditure, capitalising costs to biological assets is permissible under IAS 41. However, difficulties might be encountered in defining what should be capitalised and what should be expensed. Therefore, many entities adopt a policy of treating all such expenditure as a cost of production. An entity should establish a policy and carefully consider individual subsequent costs either for capitalisation to biological assets or for expensing directly to the income statement.

IAS 16 can be used by analogy if the entity adopts the policy of capitalising such costs. Using this analogy, costs that relate to the development of biological assets are capitalised using the criteria in IAS 16, and they are adjusted periodically by the remeasurement of the biological assets at their fair value. Management should use judgement to determine which costs would be eligible for capitalisation (for example, labour costs of those employees directly involved with the management of biological assets and other similar direct costs are likely to be capitalised, whilst labour costs related to selling staff and other similar indirect costs are unlikely to be capitalised).

The policy choice applied will directly affect disclosures such as the reconciliation of changes in carrying amounts of biological assets, expenditure classification in the income statement as well as cash flow information, so the accounting policy for the treatment of subsequent expenditure should be disclosed. #### <a id="s237-FAQ_33.21.3"></a>FAQ 33.21.3 – How do emission trading schemes affect a forest's fair value?

 

Reference to standard: IAS 41 para 8  
  Reference to standing text: [33.21](58.md#s236-Para_33.21)  

  

  
 A number of jurisdictions have introduced emission trading schemes relating to agricultural activities, particularly forestry. In the forestry industry, emission trading schemes typically involve the forester receiving credits from a government agency as carbon is sequestered by the growing trees. The forester is liable to surrender credits when the trees are harvested or otherwise lost (for example, through fire). Refer to [chapter 16 para 85](36.md#s120-Para_16.85) that considers the accounting for emission trading schemes.

Participation in an emissions trading scheme alters the economics of forestry. It is, therefore, inevitable that this will be reflected in the forest’s fair value.

Frequently, the fair value of forest assets is estimated using a discounted cash flow methodology. The difference in cash flows between a forest participating in an emissions trading scheme and one that does not, is essentially that cash inflows will need to be adjusted for the fair value of carbon credits as they are earned and cash outflows for the fair value of carbon credits when they need to be surrendered. In addition, there will be some outflows to cover the costs of administering participation in the scheme. These additional cash flows, and their timing, will affect the forest’s fair value.

### Inability to measure fair value reliably

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1700911848"></a>
#### <a id="s237-EX33.22.1"></a>EX 33.22.1 – Disruption of an active market after initial recognition

 

Reference to standard: IAS 41 para 31  
  Reference to standing text: [33.22](58.md#s236-Para_33.22)  
 

  

  
An entity is involved in raising cattle (biological assets) and recognises them at fair value less costs to sell. The fair value was based on a market-determined price at local cattle markets. A series of natural disasters has impacted cattle prices, making them highly volatile. The entity considers that the prices in these markets reflect abnormal conditions, and as such are unreliable, and wishes to adopt a cost-based approach for the current period.

Can the entity revert to the cost model for biological assets that were previously measured at fair value less costs to sell, when fair value cannot be measured reliably?

No, the entity cannot revert to the cost model. The entity has measured the biological assets at fair value less costs to sell from initial recognition. It should continue to apply the fair value model. The entity could provide additional disclosures around the price volatility, in order to give relevant information to users.

 

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_17"></a>
### Government grants

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1700927592"></a>
#### <a id="s237-EX33.28.1"></a>EX 33.28.1 – Government grant subject to annual planting

 

Reference to standard: IAS 41 paras 34, 35  
 Reference to standing text: [33.28](58.md#s236-Para_33.28)  
 

  

Entity A is in the business of growing and selling rice. The costs of rice plantation activities are high and the government has offered grants to growers as an incentive to remain in the industry and maintain the food supply. These grants will be awarded when the entity plants its crop. There are no other conditions attached to the grant. The grants are available for a three-year period.

The grant should be recognised in profit or loss when the conditions attaching to the government grant are met which would be at the point in each of the three years when the price crop is planted.

 

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1700942056"></a>
#### <a id="s237-EX33.28.2"></a>EX 33.28.2 – Government grant paid up-front

 

Reference to standard: IAS 41 paras 34, 35  
  Reference to standing text: [33.28](58.md#s236-Para_33.28)  

  

  
Entity B is in the business of growing and selling rice. The costs of rice plantation activities are high and the government has offered grants to growers as an incentive to remain in the industry and maintain the food supply. These grants will be awarded when the entity plants its crop, provided that the entity continues in the business. The purpose of the grant is to reduce the entity’s operating costs during each year. The grants are available for a three-year period.

The full grant is paid at the beginning of the three-year period, but is partially repayable if entity B ceases its rice business. The amount repayable depends on how much of the three-year period remains outstanding.

The grant should be recognised as income when it becomes unconditional. Hence, at the point in each year when an amount of the grant becomes non-refundable, that amount should be recognised as income. The balance is deferred to future periods.

 

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1700945768"></a>
#### <a id="s237-EX33.28.3"></a>EX 33.28.3 – Government grant subject to certification

 

Reference to standard: IAS 41 paras 34, 35  
  Reference to standing text: [33.28](58.md#s236-Para_33.28)  

  

  
For many years, entity C has grown fruit trees using traditional methods. From 20X0, the entity wishes to switch to organic methods. In order to assist with the costs of converting to organic farming, annual grant aid is available for three years, provided certain conditions are met. For example, the land must be registered as ‘in conversion to organic farming’ with an Organic Inspection Body (OIB) throughout the three-year period. A condition of the grant is that the registration must be certified by the OIB every year to signify that the company complies with the organic requirements.

The grant should be recognised as income when it becomes unconditional. Hence, at the point in each year when the land is certified by the OIB to be compliant and a portion of the grant becomes non-refundable, the relevant amount will be recognised as income.

### Accounting for bearer plants

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1700955112"></a>
#### <a id="s237-FAQ_33.33.1"></a>FAQ 33.33.1 – What are considered bearer plants?

 

Reference to standard: IAS 41 paras 5A, 5B  
 Reference to standing text: [33.33](58.md#s236-Para_33.33)  

  

An entity farms walnut trees for the harvest of walnuts and, ultimately, for the valuable timber obtained at the end of the trees’ productive lives. Should the trees be accounted for under IAS 16 or IAS 41?

The last criterion in the definition of bearer plant is that the likelihood that the plant will be sold as agricultural produce must be remote, except for incidental scrap sales. If part of the objective of growing the trees is to harvest valuable lumber at the end of the trees’ productive lives, it seems unlikely that this criterion will be met. The walnut tree would be within the scope of IAS 41.

Can the bearer plant accounting be applied, by analogy, to livestock reared solely as breeding animals?

No, all biological assets that are animals remain within the scope of IAS 41.

Is there a choice to continue to account for bearer plants under IAS 41, rather than under IAS 16?

No. If the plant meets the definition of a bearer plant, it must be accounted for in accordance with the requirements of IAS 16.

 

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1700838248"></a>
#### <a id="s237-FAQ_33.34.1"></a>FAQ 33.34.1 – How do you measure bearer plants?

 

Reference to standard: IAS 16 para 43  
  Reference to standing text: [33.34](58.md#s236-Para_33.34)  
 

  

  
What depreciation method should be applied to bearer plants?

The depreciation method chosen should reflect the pattern in which the future economic benefits of the plants are expected to be consumed by the entity. IAS 16 does not specify a particular method that must be used, so judgement will need to be applied.

Are bearer plants subject to impairment?

Bearer plants accounted for under IAS 16 will fall in the scope of IAS 36 for impairment testing. This applies irrespective of whether the cost or revaluation model is used.

Can borrowing costs be capitalised as part of cost?

Yes. Borrowing costs are capitalised until substantially all of the activities necessary to prepare the bearer biological asset for its intended use are complete. This is likely to be when the bearer plant reaches maturity and depreciation commences.

Can notional land rent be capitalised as part of the cost of bearer plants?

No. The notional land rent is not a cost under IAS 16 and therefore cannot be included in the cost of bearer plants. If the land is actually leased from a third party, the right-of-use asset depreciation can be capitalised as part of the cost of the bearer plant. Interest on the lease liability might also be eligible for capitalisation, refer to borrowing costs above.

Can bearer plants be measured under the revaluation method?

Yes. The choice of using the revaluation method in IAS 16 is available. It has to be used for all assets within the same class. Bearer plants could be such a class.

 

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1697989592"></a>
#### <a id="s237-FAQ_33.35.1"></a>FAQ 33.35.1 – How do you determine when bearer plants reach maturity?

 

Reference to standard: IAS 16 para 22A  
  Reference to standing text: [33.35](58.md#s236-Para_33.35)  

  

  
Maturity is often referred to as the condition that the bearer plant is in when it is capable of operating in the manner intended by management. There is no specific guidance on when a bearer plant reaches maturity. For example, a palm oil tree might start to grow produce after two years, but only reach its maximum yield after seven years. A grape vine might take many years to produce the right quantity and quality of fruit. Entities will need to determine their own reasonable accounting policy for determining when bearer plants reach maturity.

### Presentation – Balance sheet

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1698010328"></a>
#### <a id="s237-FAQ_33.36.1"></a>FAQ 33.36.1 – How are bearer plants presented in the balance sheet?

 

Reference to standard: IAS 1 para 54  
  Reference to standing text: [33.36](58.md#s236-Para_33.36)  
 

  

  
Should the same physical biological asset be treated as two different assets in the balance sheet?

Yes. IAS 41 makes a difference between:

- a bearer plant which is accounted for under IAS 16; and

- produce growing on bearer plants which is a biological asset under IAS 41.

There is also a different category of asset (that is, the agricultural produce which is the harvested produce of the entity’s biological assets).

The entities will have to recognise and measure the produce growing on the bearer plant and the bearer plant separately.

IAS 1 requires property, plant and equipment to be disclosed separately from biological assets within the scope of IAS 41.

[IAS 1 para 54].

 

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1698015704"></a>
#### <a id="s237-FAQ_33.36.2"></a>FAQ 33.36.2 – Are biological assets current or non-current?

 

Reference to standard: IAS 1 para 66  
  Reference to standing text: [33.36](58.md#s236-Para_33.36)  
 

  

The presentation of biological assets as current or non-current will depend on their nature. Normally, consumable assets (such as wheat or broiler chickens) would be presented as current assets, whereas bearer assets (such as dairy cattle) would be presented as non-current assets. Consumable biological assets will typically be an integrated part of the operating cycle. However, where the period to maturity of consumable assets (such as forests) is long, presentation as non-current might be appropriate.

### Presentation – Income statement

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1698033624"></a>
#### <a id="s237-FAQ_33.37.1"></a>FAQ 33.37.1 – Where should gains or losses be presented in the income statement?

 

Reference to standard: IAS 1 para 85  
  Reference to standing text: [33.37](58.md#s236-Para_33.37)  

  

If an entity chooses to show gains and losses arising under IAS 41 on the face of the income statement, the question arises as to where they should be presented.

The standards are silent on this issue. While the final sale of agricultural produce typically falls within the scope of IFRS 15, gains and losses are not revenue under IFRS 15. Fair value gains are income in accordance with the Conceptual Framework. Fair value losses are expenses. Fair value gains can be shown as part of total income but separately from revenue.

The example following IAS 41 indicates that the change in fair value could be presented near the top of the income statement. Because the change in fair value can be either a positive or a negative amount, it is most appropriate to present the change in fair value after revenues and other income, but before expenses. The presentation should take into consideration the characteristics of the entity’s production.

The appendix to IAS 41 provides an illustrative example of how the presentation and disclosure requirements of the standard might be put into practice.

 

<a id="s237-chap_33_navPoint_idp1698045016"></a>
#### <a id="s237-FAQ_33.37.2"></a>FAQ 33.37.2 – Should gains or losses be disaggregated?

 

Reference to standard: IAS 1 para 85  
  Reference to standing text: [33.37](58.md#s236-Para_33.37)  
 

  

  
The standard does not require or encourage disaggregating the gain or loss. Income under IAS 41 might be classified into:

- Gain or loss on initial recognition of biological assets. Initial losses on biological assets typically arise when a biological asset is purchased. The cost of the biological asset is often higher than the fair value less costs to sell, as the latter represents an exit price, and transaction expenses therefore create a loss. Initial gains on biological assets arise when new biological assets are generated (for example, when a calf or a piglet is born).

- Changes in fair value less costs to sell of biological assets. This represents the difference in value from period to period, normally on an aggregated basis. It is, therefore, sometimes difficult to distinguish from the initial gain due to procreation. The value typically increases due to growth, procreation and higher prices, but could decrease due to degeneration, sickness and lower prices.

- Gain or loss on harvest and initial recognition of agricultural produce. At the point of harvest, the agricultural produce is required to be measured at fair value less costs to sell. There is, therefore, a final fair value amount that must be recognised as income that reflects the last stage of the value creation of the biological process, up to the point when harvested produce is transferred to inventory. There could be further costs involved in preparing the inventory for market.
