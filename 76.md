### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.57.1"></a>FAQ 46.57.1 – Is a highly probable forecast foreign currency debt issuance eligible as a hedged item in a cash flow hedge of foreign currency risk?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para 6.5.2  
 Reference to standing text: [46.57](74.md#s298-Para_46.57) 

  

  
**Question**

Is a highly probable forecast foreign currency debt issuance eligible as a hedged item in a cash flow hedge of foreign currency risk?

**Illustration**

On 1 January 20X1, it is highly probable that company X (with EUR functional currency) will issue, on 1 July 20X1, USD100 million of five-year, fixed-rate debt, with quarterly coupons. On 1 January 20X1, the EUR:USD spot and six-month forward exchange rates are 1:1. The proceeds from the issuance of the debt are needed to finance the expansion of the company’s production facilities in Europe. The company is concerned that the EUR:USD exchange rate will fluctuate, such that additional USD debt will need to be issued in order to lock in EUR100 million in proceeds, which in turn will affect the interest incurred on the foreign currency debt to be issued.

Therefore, on 1 January 20X1, company X enters into a six-month forward to buy EUR and sell USD at 1:1. This transaction is on market at zero cost, because the six-month forward rate is 1:1.

Is the variability in functional currency equivalent proceeds, expected to be received from the forecast issuance of debt denominated in a currency other than the reporting entity’s functional currency, eligible for designation as the hedged transaction in a cash flow hedge of foreign currency risk?

**Answer**

No. The hedged item (risk of changes in foreign exchange rates before the forecast issuance of foreign currency debt) does not affect profit or loss where the transaction is settled or in subsequent periods. In this situation, company X is concerned about foreign exchange spot movements between the hedge inception date and the debt issuance date, specifically the risk associated with converting the foreign currency-denominated debt proceeds into its functional currency. While this represents a risk from an economic and cash flow perspective and will impact interest expense in future periods, the impact on future interest expense is not the risk being hedged.

However, see [FAQ 46.64.2](76.md#s304-FAQ_46.64.2) for the implications where the currency of issuance is not yet known.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.57.2"></a>FAQ 46.57.2 – Can the parent apply hedge accounting toon  a forecast capital contribution in its separate financial statements?

 

Reference to standard: IAS 39 para 36/IFRS 9 para 6.5.2  
 Reference to standing text: [46.57](74.md#s298-Para_46.57) 

  

  
**Question**

Can the parent apply hedge accounting to a forecast capital contribution in its separate financial statements?

**Illustration**

A parent company has subsidiaries with different functional currencies to its own, that are measured at cost in the parent’s IFRS separate financial statements. This solution addresses the accounting in the separate financial statements.

The parent occasionally makes capital contributions to its subsidiaries. When it is decided that a capital contribution will be made, an amount in foreign currency (the currency of the subsidiary) for the capital contribution is purchased. The actual payment of the capital contribution generally takes place at a later time (for example due to the legal requirements in the country of the subsidiary). The currency is put on deposit by the parent in the meantime.

The parent would like to apply (cash flow) hedge accounting on the forecast capital contribution using the deposit in foreign currency as the hedging instrument.

**Answer**

No. It is not possible to apply hedge accounting as the forecast cash flows do not affect profit or loss. Hence the conditions for hedge accounting in paragraph 36 of IAS 39/paragraph 6.5.2 of IFRS 9 are not met. This is supported by the following facts:

- When the cash is paid to the subsidiary, the fair value of the parent’s investment in the subsidiary increases by the fair value of the cash paid (at spot rate on that date). Hence, there is no foreign exchange risk in the forecast capital contribution: the increase in the investment in subsidiaries is not affected by how foreign exchange rates move between when the parent buys the cash and when it transfers it to the subsidiary. This is the same as for a hedge of a forecast debt issuance, where the foreign currency value of the debt issued is the same as the foreign currency amount received on issuance and hence there is no net foreign exchange exposure that would qualify for hedge accounting.

- Whilst there will be an effect on profit or loss on a subsequent sale of the subsidiary, this too is not affected by the change in foreign exchange rates between when the parent buys the cash and when it pays it to the subsidiary. Rather it arises from changes in foreign exchange rates after the cash injection has been made. Again this is similar to a forecast bond issuance where a subsequent buy-back of the debt could impact profit or loss, but the resulting gain or loss would not be affected by changes in foreign exchange rates before the debt is issued but only rather changes in exchange rates post issuance.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.59.1"></a>FAQ 46.59.1 – In what situation can an entity hedge a net position?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para 6.6.1  
 Reference to standing text: [46.59](74.md#s298-Para_46.59) 

  

  
 **Question**

In what situation can an entity hedge a net position?

**Illustration**

A EUR-functional currency entity has a sales department that sells certain items in USD. At the same time, the purchasing department buys certain products in USD. Each department is unaware of the other’s activities, but both want to hedge their forecast USD sales and purchases respectively. Assume that the sales department has highly probable forecast sales of USD100,000 in six months’ time, so it enters into a forward contract with the entity’s central treasury department (that is, a separate entity within the same group). The purchasing department has highly probable forecast purchases of USD90,000, also in six months’ time, and it enters into an internal derivative with the central treasury department. Both the sales and purchasing departments view their derivative as a hedging instrument, but the group cannot apply hedge accounting, because the derivative is internal to the group and so it is eliminated on consolidation.

However, in order to hedge the group’s net exposure, the treasury department enters into a forward contract with a bank for USD10,000. By doing this, the group is economically hedged. However, under IAS 39, it was not possible to designate the net position of USD10,000 (comprised of USD100,000 sales and USD90,000 purchases) as a hedged item. Instead, the group had to designate USD10,000 out of the USD100,000 of future sales as the hedged item. This did not reflect the entity’s risk management strategy and is not how the entity tracked the appropriateness of the economic hedge relationship.

Under IFRS 9, can the group designate the net position of USD10,000 as the hedged item?

**Answer**

Yes, under IFRS 9, a net position that incorporates offsetting positions can be designated as a hedged item, provided all items included in the group are individually eligible as hedged items and the items in the group are managed together on a group basis for risk management purposes. This means that the group can now apply hedge accounting to a net position comprised of sales of USD100,000 and purchases of USD90,000 with a USD10,000 derivative, which mirrors the entity’s risk management strategy.

However, the hedging gains and losses on recycling must be presented as a separate line item in P&amp;L (separate from the hedged items). An entity cannot present the post-hedging results of its commercial activities for the individual line items affected.

[IFRS 9 App B para B6.6.15]. [IFRS 9 App B para B6.6.16].

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.64.1"></a>FAQ 46.64.1 – Can an entity achieve hedge accounting if it adds an additional derivative to a pre-existing hedge relationship?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para 6.3.4  
 Reference to standing text: [46.64](74.md#s298-Para_46.64) 

  

  
 **Question**

Can an entity achieve hedge accounting if it adds an additional derivative to a pre-existing hedge relationship?

**Illustration**

Entity A, which has USD as its functional currency, takes out a 10-year floating-rate loan in EUR (a foreign currency). It wants to eliminate its exposure to variability in cash flows from changes in interest rates, so it enters into a floating-to-fixed interest rate swap in EUR. To reduce volatility in P&amp;L, it designates the swap in a cash flow hedge.

In a later period, it also wants to eliminate the foreign currency exposure, so it takes out a USD:EUR fixed-fixed cross-currency interest rate swap to eliminate its exposure.

Under IFRS 9, can entity A achieve hedge accounting by designating the cross-currency interest rate swap as hedge of the combination of the debt and the existing interest rate swap?

**Answer**

Yes. Under IFRS 9, the aggregated exposure (that is, a combination of the debt instrument plus the interest rate swap) is eligible to be designated as the hedged item, without needing to de-designate the original interest rate hedge. This is consistent with the entity’s strategy of simply overlaying the second derivative to eliminate the net foreign currency risk.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.64.2"></a>FAQ 46.64.2 – Is a highly probable forecast debt issuance eligible as a hedged item in a cash flow hedge of interest rate risk if the currency of issuance is not yet known? (Example of an aggregated hedge)

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para 6.3.4  
 Reference to standing text: [46.64](74.md#s298-Para_46.64)  Corporate Treasury

  

  
 **Question**

Is a highly probable forecast debt issuance eligible as a hedged item in a cash flow hedge of interest rate risk if the currency of issuance is not yet known?

**Illustration**

At 1 January 200X, entity A, whose functional currency is the Euro, intends to issue a variable interest rate debt in six months’ time in order to finance future activities. Depending on the market conditions existing at 1 July 200X, entity A will decide whether the debt is issued in Euros or in US dollars. If the debt is issued in US dollars, then at the debt issuance date (1 July 200X) entity A will enter into a cross-currency swap in order to convert the US dollar exposure on the debt to a Euro exposure.

Management wants to hedge its exposure to variable interest rates. On 1 January 200X, it contracts a forward-starting interest rate swap (that is, an interest rate swap that will start on 1 July 200X) which is denominated in Euros.

**Answer**

Yes. Under IFRS 9, an aggregated exposure that is a combination of a forecast transaction that could qualify as a hedged item and a derivative can be designated as a hedged item, provided that the aggregated exposure is highly probable and, once it has occurred and is therefore no longer forecast, it is eligible as a hedged item.

As a result, the proposed designation is acceptable, provided that it is in line with entity A’s risk management strategy and objectives. In the illustration, the designated hedged item would be the highly probable variable interest payments in Euros (entity A’s functional currency), arising either from the Euro debt or the aggregated exposure (US dollar debt swapped into Euros by using the cross-currency swap).

A hedge of a forecast foreign currency debt issuance for only foreign currency risk would not be eligible for a cash flow hedging relationship. See [FAQ 46.57.1](76.md#s304-FAQ_46.57.1).

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.64.3"></a>FAQ 46.64.3 – Is it possible to layer basis swaps on top of a hedged item in a hedge relationship?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para 6.3.4  
 Reference to standing text: [46.64](74.md#s298-Para_46.64)  Corporate Treasury

  

  
 **Illustration**

According to its business plan, entity A will have liquidity requirements between EUR200 million and EUR300 million during the next three-year period. In order to meet this funding requirement, management decides to enter into a revolving facility with bank B. The entity can draw up to EUR350 million at a variable rate, being either SONIA three-month + spread or SONIA one-month + spread. The chosen rate is SONIA three-month.

Its risk management strategy regarding the exposure to interest rate risk is to fix the interest rate of at least 60% of its floating-rate debt. In order to comply with this strategy, the treasurer has contracted an interest rate swap where entity A receives SONIA three-month and pays 2.6% fixed rate on a principal amount of EUR200 million with a three-year maturity.

One year later, due to differences in liquidity of SONIA one-month and SONIA three-month markets, entity A decides to roll its revolving facility on a SONIA one-month basis and enters into a basis swap receive SONIA one-month/pay SONIA three-month in order to avoid discrepancies between the interest rate swap and the borrowing.

Can entity A layer the basis swap on top of the revolving facility and designate the combined exposure in a hedge relationship?

**Answer**

Yes. Under IFRS 9, it is possible to designate a risk exposure and a derivative as a hedged item (commonly referred to as ‘aggregated exposure’). As per IFRS 9 the items that constitute the aggregated exposure remain accounted for separately, however, an entity considers the combined effect of these items for the purpose of assessing hedge effectiveness and measuring ineffectiveness.

In this illustration, entity A designated the floating-to-fixed interest rate swap as the hedging instrument in a cash flow hedge of the interest rate risk exposure on its floating-rate loan (the ‘first level hedge relationship’). One year later, the loan’s reference rate is changed from three-month to one-month SONIA, which increases entity A’s exposure to variability in cash flows on the aggregated exposure (that is, the floating-rate loan and the interest rate swap that are included in the first level hedge relationship). Under IFRS 9 entity A can designate the basis swap as the hedging instrument in a cash flow hedge of the aggregated exposure (the ‘second level hedge relationship’). When the second level hedge relationship starts, the first level hedge relationship continues as a separate hedge relationship.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.64.4"></a>FAQ 46.64.4 – Is it possible to have a non-zero hypothetical derivative when adding hedging instruments (layering arrangements) into a hedge relationship

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para 6.3.4  
 Reference to standing text: [46.64](74.md#s298-Para_46.64)  Corporate Treasury

  

  
**Question**

Should an existing cash flow hedge relationships using crude oil swaps be de-designated and re-designated when the crack spread swaps are entered into? If yes, should the hypothetical derivative representing the forecast jet fuel purchases be reset to zero when the crack spread swaps are layered on top of the crude oil swaps?

**Illustration**

An airline company has a well-defined jet fuel risk management strategy, which consists of hedging a portion of its long-term jet fuel exposure using crude oil swaps (up to three years forward), and adding crack spread swaps (swaps from crude oil to jet fuel) within the last 12 months to maturity of the exposure. Jet fuel hedging markets are not very liquid beyond one year, so hedging with crude oil swaps is economically more effective. The basis spread between crude oil and jet fuel is also much less volatile beyond one year, which decreases the need for hedging basis risk longer term. The airline company expects the hedge to continue to be highly effective, even without the addition of the crack spread swap.

The airline designates the crude oil swaps as cash flow hedges of the forecast jet fuel purchases. Management’s aim is to achieve hedge accounting with the whole package of hedging instruments (crude oil swaps plus crack spread swaps).

**Answer**

Under IFRS 9, management has a choice. It can choose to de-designate the previous hedge relationship and designate a new hedge relationship, or it can leave the original hedge relationship unchanged and add a second hedge relationship by applying the permission in IFRS 9 to designate an aggregated exposure as the hedged item.

If the entity elects the first option, the original hedge relationship is de-designated and re-designated when the crack spread swaps are entered into. The hypothetical derivative should be reset to zero.

Alternatively, under IFRS 9, it is possible to designate the risk exposure and the derivative jointly as a hedged item (the jet fuel exposure and crude oil swaps, commonly referred to as an ‘aggregated exposure’). The items that constitute the aggregated exposure remain accounted for separately; however, the entity considers the combined effect of these items for the purpose of assessing hedge effectiveness and measuring ineffectiveness. The entity sets up a second hedge relationship by designating the crack spread swap as the hedging instrument in a cash flow hedge (the ‘second level hedge relationship’) where the aggregated exposure is the hedged item. When the second level hedge relationship starts, the first level hedge relationship continues as a separate hedge relationship, so there is no need to reset the original hypothetical derivative.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.64.5"></a>FAQ 46.64.5 – What is the impact on the hypothetical derivative method when an entity de-layers hedged risks?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para 6.3.4  
 Reference to standing text: [46.64](74.md#s298-Para_46.64)  Corporate Treasury

  

  
**Background**

Ineffectiveness in a cash flow hedge relationship is often determined using a hypothetical derivative, the fair value of which is compared with that of the hedging instrument. The hypothetical derivative typically should have a zero fair value at inception of the hedge relationship.

Generally, a modification to a hedging relationship represents a de-designation and re-designation (see IFRS IC’s January 2016 agenda decision regarding transition to IFRS 9). However, the substance of some modifications is such that a part of the hedge relationship continues, while a part ceases or is de-designated. This might be the case, for example, where the hedging of certain risk components is discontinued. There is little guidance in IFRS 9 regarding the application of the hypothetical derivative method, particularly in the event of a partial de-designation of a hedging relationship (where this is in line with a revised risk management strategy).

In our opinion, therefore, it is acceptable in certain, limited circumstances not to reset the hypothetical derivative to one with a fair value of zero on the re-designation of the modified hedging relationship. We see this to be a reasonable application of the hypothetical derivative method and in line with the spirit of both IAS 39 and IFRS 9.

Accordingly, we believe that these limited circumstances might be met if the hedging relationship meets the following criteria:

- the risk exposure(s) being hedged in the modified hedging relationship was (were) explicitly designated as being hedged in the original hedging relationship;

- the original hedging relationship would have met all of the qualifying criteria for hedge accounting in paragraph 6.4.1 of IFRS 9 if it had not been modified; and

- the previously hedged risk that is being removed from the hedging relationship is a separately identifiable and reliably measurable risk component of the hedged item.

This solution sets out the application of these principles to various scenarios.

**Scenario 1A**

Entity A, with EUR functional currency, issued a USD floating-rate debt. At the same time, it entered two derivatives – a receive USD floating, pay USD fixed swap, and a receive USD fixed, pay EUR fixed CCIRS . Entity A designated these derivatives together as a cash flow hedge of both benchmark USD interest rates and FX risk. The terms of the combined swap exactly match those of the debt, and so no ineffectiveness arises (credit risk and currency basis are ignored in this example, for simplicity). Some time later, entity A no longer wishes to hedge the USD:EUR FX risk, and terminates the receive USD fixed, pay EUR fixed CCIRS, leaving only a USD swap hedging variability in USD benchmark rates.

Since entity A continues to designate the USD swap as a hedging instrument in the modified hedge relationship, does entity A need to reset the fixed leg on the hypothetical derivative?

**Answer to scenario 1A**

No. The receive USD floating, pay USD fixed swap can continue to be used in a cash flow hedge of the USD debt. This derivative will have a non-zero fair value, due to movements in interest rates since the initial hedge designation.

In this case:

- cash flow variability arising from changes in USD benchmark rates was explicitly designated and being hedged in the original hedge relationship;

- the original hedging relationship would have met all of the qualifying criteria for hedge accounting in paragraph 6.4.1 of IFRS 9 if it had not been modified; and

- the FX risk that is being removed from the hedging relationship is a separately identifiable and reliably measurable risk component of the USD debt.

Accordingly, we believe that the pay USD floating, receive USD fixed component of the existing hypothetical derivative, which would have had a zero fair value at the inception of the original hedge of USD benchmark interest rates, can continue to be used to calculate ineffectiveness. The use of this hypothetical derivative will give rise to little or no ineffectiveness (in the absence of credit risk).

**Scenario 1B**

Entity B, also with EUR functional currency, issued a USD100 million debt paying USD benchmark interest rate + 2%. At the same time, entity B entered a single derivative to hedge both currency and benchmark interest rates – being a receive USD benchmark interest rate + 2%, pay EUR 5% CCIRS. At the time, the pay leg in a receive USD benchmark interest rate + 2% swap would have been 5%.

Entity B designated this derivative as a cash flow hedge of both benchmark USD interest rates and FX risk. The terms of the CCIRS exactly match those of the debt, and so no ineffectiveness arises (credit risk and currency basis are again ignored in this example, for simplicity). Some time later, due to a change in its risk management strategy, entity B no longer wishes to hedge the USD:EUR FX risk. Accordingly, entity B enters an at-market receive EUR fixed, pay USD fixed CCIRS, with EUR cash flows that exactly match those paid under the existing CCIRS. Due to movements in FX and interest rates, this instrument is a receive EUR 5%, pay USD 6% CCIRS.

In combination with the original CCIRS, this new derivative creates a receive USD benchmark interest rate + 2%, pay USD 6% swap, which will be used to continue entity B’s cash flow hedge of variability in cash flows attributable to USD benchmark interest rates.

Since entity B continues to hedge the USD floating-rate loan for changes in USD benchmark interest rates (using the two CCIRSs together as a hedging instrument in the modified hedge relationship), does entity B need to reset the fixed leg on the hypothetical derivative?

**Answer to scenario 1B**

No. Entity B can combine the two CCIRSs to create a receive USD benchmark interest rate + 2%, pay USD 6% swap that can be used in a cash flow hedge of the USD debt, as long as the ineffectiveness arising from this designation does not preclude hedge accounting. This derivative will have a non-zero fair value due to movements in interest and FX rates since the initial hedge designation.

In this case:

- cash flow variability arising from changes in USD benchmark rates was explicitly designated and being hedged in the original hedge relationship;

- the original hedging relationship would have met all of the qualifying criteria for hedge accounting in paragraph 6.4.1 of IFRS 9 if it had not been modified; and

- the FX risk that is being removed from the hedging relationship is a separately identifiable and reliably measurable risk component of the USD debt.

Accordingly, we believe that the pay USD floating, receive USD fixed component of the existing hypothetical derivative can continue to be used to calculate ineffectiveness.

Nevertheless, the original hypothetical derivative comprised an at-market USD swap and an at-market fixed to fixed CCIRS (that is, both having a zero fair value). The pay leg in an at-market receive USD benchmark interest rate  + 2% swap at inception would have been 5%, and hence this is the rate used in defining the existing hypothetical derivative, if the entity does not wish to reset the hypothetical derivative’s fixed leg to current market rate. The difference between the interest rate on the fixed leg of the hedging instrument (6%) and the hypothetical derivative (5%) will give rise to ineffectiveness.

**Scenario 2**

Entity C purchases brass gears for use in its manufacturing process. The purchase cost of the gears is based on the spot price of the constituent metals (primarily copper and zinc) and a fixed unit cost for machining. Entity C purchases metal futures for both copper and zinc to hedge the entire cash flow variability arising from its brass gear purchases.

Some time later, entity C no longer wishes to hedge changes in the zinc price and terminates its zinc future. However, entity C will continue to hedge the variability in cash flows associated with the purchase of brass gears arising from changes in the copper price.

Since entity C continues to hedge the copper component of the brass gears for variability in cash flows due to changes in the copper price, does it need to reset the fixed leg on the hypothetical copper future?

**Answer to scenario 2**

Yes. Entity C must reset the hypothetical derivative. This is because the copper component was not explicitly designated as a hedged risk component in the original hedge relationship, and hence the above principles are not met.

**Scenario 3**

Entity D operates in the same business, and has the same hedging strategy, as entity C. Entity D also uses copper and zinc futures to hedge the cash flow variability of its purchases of brass gears arising from changes in the prices of copper and zinc. However, entity D has performed an analysis and concluded that both copper and zinc are eligible risk components in accordance with IFRS 9. Entity D therefore designates both derivatives as a combined hedging instrument in a hedge of the copper and zinc risk components of the forecast purchase of gears.

Some time later, entity D no longer wishes to hedge changes in the zinc price and terminates its zinc future. However, entity D will continue to hedge the copper component.

Since entity D continues to hedge the copper component of the brass gears for variability in cash flows due to changes in the copper price, does it need to reset the fixed leg on the hypothetical copper future?

**Answer to scenario 3**

No. Entity D could de-designate the zinc risk component and continue to hedge the copper risk component. The fixed leg of the hypothetical copper future does not need to be reset to give the hypothetical derivative a zero fair value.

This is because:

- the copper component was explicitly designated as a hedged risk component in the original hedge relationship;

- the original hedging relationship would have met all of the qualifying criteria for hedge accounting in paragraph 6.4.1 of IFRS 9 if it had not been modified; and

- the zinc component that is being de-designated from the hedging relationship is a separately identifiable and reliably measurable risk component of the brass gears.

In light of the difference in outcome for scenarios 2 and 3, it is recommended that entities currently applying IAS 39 that expect to move to IFRS 9 during the life of the hedge should document their hedge in the manner used in scenario 3. Doing so will enable them to retain flexibility for subsequent modifications to (that is, partial de-designations of) hedge relationships.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.64.6"></a>FAQ 46.64.6 – Can an entity apply hedge accounting under IFRS 9 to hedge the exposure on an aggregate position not already in a hedge relationship?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para 6.3.4  
 Reference to standing text: [46.64](74.md#s298-Para_46.64)  Corporate Treasury

  

  
**Illustration**

A EUR-functional entity has EUR floating-rate funding. In line with its risk management policy, it has entered into a floating-to-floating cross-currency interest rate swap (‘CCIRS’) to swap the loan to floating GBP (for example, because it has a GBP asset in its balance sheet). It does not apply hedge accounting to this CCIRS, because it is hedging away from its functional currency.

In line with its interest rate risk management strategy, the entity concurrently takes out a GBP pay fixed, receive floating interest rate swap (‘IRS’) to hedge the GBP floating interest rate risk in the aggregated exposure consisting of the CCIRS and the EUR floating-rate funding.

(It is assumed that all IFRS 9 hedge accounting requirements with respect to documentation and effectiveness are met.)

**Question**

Can the entity apply hedge accounting under IFRS 9 to the GBP IRS by designating as a hedge the aggregated exposure comprising the loan and the CCIRS?

**Answer**

Yes. There is no requirement in IFRS 9 that hedge accounting is achieved for the first-level relationship (that is, the hedge of the funding with the CCIRS) to comply with the general hedge accounting requirements for the second-level relationship (that is, the hedges of the aggregated exposure with the IRS). However, where hedge accounting is not applied to the first-level relationship, this can make the accounting for the aggregated exposure more complicated and the outcome inferior compared to achieving hedge accounting on the first-level relationship. [IFRS 9 para BC6.167]. In this case, the CCIRS will be measured at fair value through profit or loss, creating some income statement volatility.

The application of cash flow hedge accounting would result in the change in fair value of the GBP IRS being recognised in OCI to the extent that the hedge is effective. Periodically, an amount should be recycled out of the cash flow hedge reserve in line with when the hedged item affects the income statement. There is no cost of hedging to be recorded, because the hedge relationship does not contain an FX element.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.67.1"></a>FAQ 46.67.1 – Can an entity hedge a risk component of a fixed-rate debt instrument?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B6.3.10(d)  
 Reference to standing text: [46.67](74.md#s298-Para_46.67) 

  

**Question**

  
 An entity issues an 8% fixed-rate debt instrument for C100 that is repayable at par at the end of year 2. At the time of issue, the risk-free interest rate is 6%. Therefore, the credit spread on the new issue is 2%. Can the entity hedge a risk component of the fixed-rate debt instrument, for example the risk-free rate?

**Answer**

Yes.

In this scenario, the entity can designate an identifiable and separately measurable portion of the interest rate exposure as the hedged risk. Such a portion could be a risk-free interest rate or benchmark interest rate component of the debt instrument’s total interest rate exposure (that is, the entity’s own credit spread of 2% could be excluded). This is subject to the proviso that there is an economic relationship between the component selected and the hedging instrument used.

If the entity intends to designate the benchmark component of 6% as the hedged risk, it could take out a receive 6% fixed, pay floating benchmark interest rate swap to hedge the changes in the debt’s fair value due to changes in the benchmark interest rate. The hedge will be expected to meet the hedge effectiveness requirements, as the benchmark component can be separately identified and reliably measured, and there is an economic relationship between the risk-free or benchmark component and the hedging instrument.

Note that it is not necessary for the pay leg of the swap to be the same as the benchmark interest rate (receive 6%, pay benchmark interest rate). The entity could take out a receive 8%, pay floating benchmark interest rate + 2% interest rate swap and still designate the risk-free or benchmark component as the hedged component. This is because the fair value of the swap comprises the net of the present values of both the fixed and the floating legs. Therefore, increasing both sides of the swap by 2% (from receive 6%, pay benchmark interest rate to receive 8%, pay benchmark interest rate + 2%) will not change the fair value for a given change in interest rates.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.67.2"></a>FAQ 46.67.2 – Is it possible for an issuer of a debt instrument to achieve cash flow hedge accounting in a negative interest rate environment?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(b); IFRS IC January 2016 IFRIC update  
 Reference to standing text: [46.67](74.md#s298-Para_46.67) 

  

**Question**

In what circumstances can an issuer of a debt instrument achieve cash flow hedge accounting in a negative interest rate environment?

**Answer**

  
 The answer to this question will depend on the circumstances. If the debt and hedging instruments do not have an interest rate floor, the hedge will be effective in both a positive and a negative interest rate environment.

If there is a floor in the debt but not in the hedging instrument, and the floor is in the money due to a decline in interest rates and is expected to be in the money for the life of the instrument, hedge accounting cannot be applied, because there is no variability in cash flows on the hedged item. The overall cash flows would not be fixed, because the entity will pay out under the ‘receive variable’ floating leg of the hedging instrument, but it will not receive a floating payment on the debt as a result of the floor.

If the floor in the hedged item is in the money but market interest rates are expected to increase, the hedge relationship will have ineffectiveness. Depending on the size of the ineffectiveness**,**and the relative length of periods when the option is expected to be in/out of the money, this hedge might fail the economic relationship test.

If the floating leg of any hedging instrument has a floor equal to that on the debt, it should be possible to achieve hedge accounting, because all payments are now fixed. Note that any floor in the related hedged item cannot be separated, as a result of the January 2016 IFRS IC agenda decision (see [chapter 41 para 32](62.md#s257-Para_41.32) for more details on separation of caps and floors).

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.67.3"></a>FAQ 46.67.3 – When can a new benchmark rate (such as SONIA) be designated as a non-contractually specified risk component of a debt instrument?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 paras 6.3.7, B6.3.9, B6.3.10(d)  
 Reference to standing text: [46.67](74.md#s298-Para_46.67) 

  

 

**Question**

 

Before the IASB’s IBOR Reform Phase 2 amendments are applied, can a new benchmark rate (such as SONIA1) be designated as a non-contractually specified risk component of a debt instrument for the purpose of hedging a risk component under IFRS 9?

 

**Illustration**

 

In many jurisdictions, the transition to replacement benchmark rates is progressing sooner in the wholesale derivative markets than entities are transitioning loans and cash products. As a result, an entity might wish to access the increasing liquidity in wholesale markets for derivatives based on the new benchmark rate (such as SONIA for GBP derivatives), even where loans and other cash instruments are still largely transacted based on the old benchmark rate (such as GBP LIBOR2 for GBP floating-rate loans).

 

This issue relates to interest rate derivatives that are used either as:

 

- fair value hedges (for example, in a hedge of a fixed-rate instrument); or

- cash flow hedges (for example, in a hedge of a floating-rate instrument that references the old benchmark rate such as GBP LIBOR).

 

In either case, in order to reduce ineffectiveness, the entity might wish to designate the hedged item as a risk component, based on the new benchmark rate – that is, as changes in the cash flows or fair value of the hedged debt instrument attributable to changes in the new benchmark rate.

 

**Answer**

 

IFRS 9 permits an entity to designate a risk component as the hedged item, provided that the risk component is separately identifiable and reliably measurable (‘SIRM’). [IFRS 9 para 6.3.7].

 

Hence, before the IASB’s IBOR Reform Phase 2 amendments are applied, an entity can designate the hedged item as a risk component based on the new benchmark rate (such as changes in the cash flows or fair value of a hedged debt instrument attributable to changes in SONIA) only if that risk component is SIRM.

 

This is considered separately for fair value hedges and cash flow hedges below. This FAQ illustrates the issues using the example of GBP SONIA, although similar considerations apply for other new benchmark rates.

 

Fair value hedges

 

In a fair value hedge of a fixed-rate debt instrument, the cash flows being hedged are fixed and do not vary with any benchmark rate. Hence the entity will need to assess whether SONIA is a SIRM risk component – that is, whether changes in the fair value of the hedged debt instrument attributable to changes in SONIA are SIRM.

 

IFRS 9 requires the entity to make this assessment within the context of the particular market structure to which the risk (that is, SONIA) relates and in which the hedging activity takes place. [IFRS 9 App B para B6.3.9]. Management will need to consider a range of factors, including:

 

- the extent to which similar GBP fixed-rate debt instruments are compared by their spreads to SONIA;

- the extent to which entities use SONIA derivatives to manage interest rate risk on fixed-rate debt instruments, and the liquidity in the market for SONIA derivatives; and

- whether the price of fixed-rate debt instruments varies directly in response to changes in SONIA as they happen.

 

[IFRS 9 App B para B6.3.10(d)].

 

The absence of one of the above factors does not necessarily mean that the proposed benchmark (SONIA) risk component is not SIRM.

 

Cash flow hedges

 

In a cash flow hedge of floating-rate debt, the cash flows being hedged vary with a benchmark rate. If the cash flows of the debt instrument vary with GBP LIBOR, then GBP LIBOR will be a hedgeable risk component, since it is contractually specified. For another benchmark rate (such as SONIA) to be a hedgeable risk component, management would need to demonstrate that this benchmark is a component of GBP LIBOR. At the time of writing (June 2020), this is not the case for SONIA, given that SONIA and GBP LIBOR are different methods for measuring banks’ unsecured funding cost.

 

Furthermore, paragraph B6.3.9 of IFRS 9 requires an entity to consider “the market structure to which the risk or risks relate and in which the hedging activity takes place”. The relevant market for this purpose is the one for the hedged item being assessed (that is, for floating-rate loans, it is the floating-rate loan market). If new floating-rate loans are still typically indexed to GBP LIBOR, the market structure indicates that GBP LIBOR is a SIRM benchmark component of floating-rate loans. The fact that there might be reasonable liquidity in the derivatives market for SONIA derivatives, and that the entity uses some such derivatives to hedge floating-rate loans, would not, of itself, be sufficient to conclude that SONIA is a SIRM component of such loans.

 

1 Sterling Overnight Index Average

 

2 London Interbank Offered Rate

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.67.4"></a>FAQ 46.67.4 – Can an entity hedge the variability in cash flows arising from changes in the real interest rate?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para 6.3.7, 6.5.2(b) and B6.3.13-14  
Reference to standing text: [46.67](74.md#s298-Para_46.67)  
 

  

 

**Question**

 

An entity with a floating rate instrument referenced to an interest rate benchmark enters into an inflation swap (which swaps the variable interest cash flows of the floating rate instrument for variable cash flows based on an inflation index). The entity wants to designate the swap in a cash flow hedging relationship to hedge changes in the variable interest payments for changes in the real interest rate**.**

 

Can the entity designate the inflation swap in a cash flow hedge to hedge changes in the variable interest rate payments on a floating rate instrument for changes in the real interest rate?

 

**Answer**

 

No. The IFRS IC discussed this issue and published an Agenda Decision in May 2021.

The IFRS IC noted, amongst other things, that the proposed relationship does not ‘fix’ the nominal cash flows as defined by paragraph 6 of IAS 7. The IFRS IC therefore concluded that there is no exposure to variability in cash flows that is attributable to changes in the real interest rate in the proposed cash flow hedging relationship, and so the requirements in paragraphs 6.3.7 and 6.5.2(b) of IFRS 9 were not met. Consequently, the real interest rate risk component in the proposed cash flow hedging relationship did not meet the requirements in IFRS 9 to be designated as an eligible hedged item as required by paragraph 6.4.1 of IFRS 9.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.69.2"></a>FAQ 46.69.2 – When an entity hedges all of the cash flows of an entire hedged item but for only one risk, how should it measure ineffectiveness?

 

<table style="border-collapse:collapse"><tr><td style="border:0.75pt solid #000000; padding-right:5.03pt; padding-left:5.03pt; vertical-align:top"><p style="background-color:transparent"><span style="font-style:italic">Reference to standard</span>: IFRS 9 paras B6.3.21–B6.3.22 :</p></td></tr><tr><td style="border:0.75pt solid #000000; padding-right:5.03pt; padding-left:5.03pt; vertical-align:top"><p style="background-color:transparent"><span style="font-style:italic">Reference to standing text</span>: <a href="74.md#s298-Para_46.69">46.69</a></p></td></tr><tr><td style="border:0.75pt solid #000000; padding-right:5.03pt; padding-left:5.03pt; vertical-align:top"><p style="background-color:transparent"></p></td></tr></table>

 

**Question**

 

When an entity hedges all of the cash flows of an entire hedged item but for only one risk, how should it measure ineffectiveness? In particular, when using a hypothetical derivatives method, how should the entity construct the hypothetical derivative?

 

**Answer**

 

**Example 1: fixed margin**

 

Entity A enters into a contract to buy a specified commodity (for example, coffee beans or crude oil). A standard grade of the specified commodity trades on a commodity exchange, but the grade being purchased by entity A is of lower quality than the standard traded grade. The price specified in the contract is the exchange traded price less a fixed differential of CU10 per unit of commodity purchased (the discount of CU10 compensates for the grade difference).  Entity A hedges the variability in the price of its future commodity purchases with a forward contract on that commodity whose underlying is the standard grade. It designates the derivative as a hedge of all of the cash flows of the future commodity purchases for changes attributable to the benchmark standard grade price only.

 

In this case, paras B6.3.21 and B6.3.25 require that the hedged cash flows must be designated as the entire cash flows – for example. the benchmark (standard grade price) less the fixed margin (CU10). It follows that all changes to the entire cash flows attributable to the hedged benchmark are used to determine the fair value changes of the hedged item. Hence the variable leg of the hypothetical derivative should be set as the standard grade price less CU10. The fixed leg of the hypothetical derivative would then be set as the benchmark fixed price for the term of the contract less the same discount of CU10 so that the hypothetical derivative has a fair value of zero at inception of the hedge. In this case, the CU10 is included in both the fixed and the variable leg of the hypothetical derivative and would cancel out.

 

If the hedged item contains other sources of cash flow variability (such as a cap or floor on the total price) then these should also be included. For example, if the overall price in the contract is floored at CU30 per unit (that is, the contract specifies that entity A must pay a minimum of CU30 per unit) this floor should be included in the variable leg of the hypothetical derivative and can lead to ineffectiveness as per [FAQ 46.39.5](75.md#s303-FAQ_46.39.5) and [FAQ 46.67.2](76.md#s304-FAQ_46.67.2).

 

**Example 2: variable margin that is a separately identifiable and reliably measurable component**

 

Entity B enters into a contract to buy a specified commodity whose price varies with two separately identifiable and reliably measurable components, one of which is negative.  for example. the price comprises component X less component Y, where both X and Y are separately identifiable and reliably measurable components. Entity B hedges only component X, by entering into a forward contract whose underlying is X. Component Y is not hedged. Entity B designates the derivative as a hedge of all of the cash flows of the future commodity purchases for changes attributable to the price of component X only.

 

Similar to example 1 above, paras B6.3.21 and B6.3.25 require that the hedged cash flows must be designated as the entire cash flows – for example. the entire price being X minus Y. It follows that the variable leg of the hypothetical derivative should be set at inception as the entire price of X minus Y. However, given these cash flows are designated as being hedged for only one particular risk (changes in X), subsequently only the changes in component X are included in the fair value change from the hedged item and hence in the hypothetical derivative.  Both positive and negative changes in the cash flows from the unhedged component Y are excluded as they do not relate to the hedged risk. Similar to example 1 above, the effects of any cap or floor should be taken into account in the measurement of the hedged item.

 

For example, assume at the inception of the hedge, the market price for component X is CU24 and for unhedged risk component Y is CU-4 giving a market price at inception of CU20. The variable leg of the hypothetical derivative should be set at inception as the entire cash flows of CU20, ie component X (CU24) less the unhedged component Y (CU-4). The fixed leg of the hypothetical derivative would then be set as the benchmark fixed price for component X for the term of the contract less the same unhedged component of CU4 so that the hypothetical derivative has a fair value of zero at inception of the hedge. The CU-4 is not changed in subsequent periods when the hypothetical derivative is remeasured. If there is any other variability (such as a cap or floor on the total price) then these need to be taken into account (see [FAQ 46.39.5](75.md#s303-FAQ_46.39.5) and [FAQ 46.67.2](76.md#s304-FAQ_46.67.2)).

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.70.1"></a>FAQ 46.70.1 – Is it possible to designate a benchmark interest rate as the hedged risk of a fixed-rate debt instrument subsequent to its origination?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B6.3.23  
 Reference to standing text: [46.70](74.md#s298-Para_46.70) 

  

**Question**

  
 On 1 April 20X1, an entity issues a 6% fixed-rate debt instrument for C100 that is repayable at par at the end of year 5 (31 March 20X6). Interest is payable annually in arrears. At the time of issue, the benchmark interest rate is 5%.

On 1 July 20X2, the entity decides to hedge the interest rate risk on the debt instrument with an interest rate swap when the benchmark interest rate has increased to 7%. At that time, the fair value of the debt instrument is C93. Is it possible to designate the benchmark interest rate risk at the date of hedge designation as the hedged risk on the fixed-rate debt instrument even though that rate is higher than the benchmark rate at origination?

**Answer**

The entity calculates that, if it had issued the debt instrument on 1 July 20X2 when it first designated it as the hedged item for its fair value of C93, the effective yield on the instrument would have been 8.1%. Because the benchmark interest rate on 1 July 20X2 is 7% and is less than this effective yield, the entity can designate a benchmark interest rate portion of 7% that consists partly of the contractual interest cash flows and partly of the discount that is included in the difference between the current fair value of C93 and the amount repayable on maturity of C100.

### <a id="s304-EX46.71.1"></a>EX 46.71.1 – Examples of eligible risk components of non-financial items

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 paras 6.3.7(a), B6.3.8–B6.3.11  
 Reference to standing text: [46.71](74.md#s298-Para_46.71) 

  

  
 **Example 1 – Forecast purchase of a non-monetary asset in foreign currency**

Entity A is planning to buy a large piece of machinery from a foreign supplier. The forecast purchase will be denominated in a foreign currency, so the entity enters into a forward contract to hedge the risk of movements in the relevant foreign exchange rate.

The forecast purchase can be designated as a hedged item in a cash flow hedge of foreign currency risk, provided that the forecast purchase is highly probable, and the requirements for effectiveness and the other conditions for hedge accounting are met. The hedged risk (movements in exchange rates) is separately identifiable and reliably measurable, it will affect the amount paid for the machine, and so it will affect P&amp;L as the machine is depreciated.

**Example 2 – Hedge of foreign currency-denominated commodity risk**

Entity A plans to purchase a fixed quantity of a commodity in 12 months’ time in US dollars. The functional currency of entity A is the euro. Entity A wants to reduce the risk of price changes of the commodity, and it hedges its exposure to that price risk by entering into a 12-month cash-settled forward contract for this commodity. The price in the forward contract is denominated in US dollars, reflecting the fact that the underlying commodity is commonly traded in US dollars.

The purchase exposes entity A to two risks: price risk regarding the market price of the commodity; and foreign exchange risk of US dollars against the euro. Under IFRS 9, it is possible to designate the forecast purchase in its entirety, the foreign exchange risk or the market price component as the hedged item. Any of these designations is possible, because the market price component is the difference between the total price risk and the foreign currency risk, both of which could be individually designated as the hedged risk, as they are separately identifiable and reliably measurable.

### <a id="s304-EX46.71.2"></a>EX 46.71.2 – Examples of eligible contractually  and non-contractually specified risk components of a non-financial item

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B6.3.10  
 Reference to standing text: [46.71](74.md#s298-Para_46.71) 

  

  
 **Example 1 – Contractually specified component of a non-financial item**

Entity A has a contract to purchase a product (such as aluminium cans), in which a metal (such as aluminium) is used in the production process. The purchase contract specifies that the price will be the London Metal Exchange (LME) price/kg × Y grams (the standard amount of aluminium in a can) + conversion costs + transport costs.

Entity A wishes to use aluminium LME futures or forward contracts to hedge its price exposure to aluminium. IFRS 9 allows entity A to designate the LME price as the hedged risk, because it is a contractually specified component. This avoids ineffectiveness caused by having to designate all pricing elements (including conversion and delivery costs) and is in line with the entity’s risk management strategy.

**Example 2 – Non-contractually specified component of a non-financial item**

Entity A has a contract to purchase a product (such as aluminium cans), in which a metal (such as aluminium) is used in the production process. The contract does not explicitly specify the London Metal Exchange (LME) as a component of the price to be paid. However, contracts to purchase aluminium cans are commonly priced by market participants based on a building block approach, as follows:

- The first building block is the LME price for a standard grade of aluminium ingot.

- The next building block is the grade premium or discount to reflect the quality of aluminium used, as compared to the standard LME grade.

- An additional amount is added for the cost of conversion from ingot into cans and delivery costs.

- The final building block is a profit margin for the seller.

Entity A wishes to use aluminium LME futures or forward contracts to hedge its price exposure to aluminium. Even though the LME is not contractually specified (unlike example 1), IFRS 9 allows entity A to designate the LME price as the hedged risk, provided it is separately identifiable and reliably measurable, if this is how contracts are priced by market participants. This avoids ineffectiveness caused by having to designate all pricing elements (including conversion and delivery costs) and is in line with the entity’s risk management strategy.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.71.3"></a>FAQ 46.71.3 – Can fixed duty or fixed delivery charges based on the quantity transported be excluded from hedge designation in a fair value hedge relationship?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para 6.3.7  
  Reference to standing text: [46.71](74.md#s298-Para_46.71)  
Corporate Treasury

  

  
**Question**

Can fixed duty or fixed delivery charges based on the quantity transported be excluded from hedge designation in a fair value hedge relationship?

**Illustration**

Entity A enters into a firm commitment to purchase 2 tons of silver on January 20X7 for a fixed price. Included in that fixed price is a fixed duty/delivery charge. Entity A has entered into a futures contract to hedge its exposure to silver price. Entity A intends to designate only the movements in the silver price as the hedged risk. Entity A has assessed and concluded that changes in the fair value of the firm commitment that are attributable to changes in the silver price is a separately identifiable and a reliably measurable risk component.

**Answer**

Yes. Paragraph 6.3.7(a) of IFRS 9 permits an entity to designate as the hedged item only changes in the fair value that are attributable to a specific risk or risks (risk component) provided that the risk component is separately identifiable and reliably measurable.

In the above illustration, the fair value of the firm commitment varies depending on movements in the fixed charge as well as the underlying commodity price. Entity A has assessed and concluded that the silver price is a separately identifiable and a reliably measurable risk component. The silver price risk component could be designated as a hedged item under IFRS 9 (excluding the fixed duty/delivery charges).

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.71.4"></a>FAQ 46.71.4 – What should be considered when hedging components in a long-term commodity supply contract?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para 6.3.7  
  Reference to standing text: [46.71](74.md#s298-Para_46.71)  
Corporate Treasury

  

  
**Illustration**

Entity A enters into a long-term supply contract for a generic commodity whose price is set by a contractually specified formula based on three other commodities. This is a standard pricing formula frequently used in this commodity market (that is, an industry pricing norm), so the contract has been assessed not to contain separable embedded derivatives.

Example

A contract requires the counterparty to pay monthly amounts from January 20X2 to December 20X2 (being the life of the contract), calculated by reference to the three-month average price of three commodities as follows:

Pi = a × X i(3) + b ×Yi(3) + c × Zi(3)

Where: Pi is the price for month i,

X i(3), Yi(3), and Zi(3) are the average of the prices of commodity X, Y and Z respectively in the three months preceding month i, and a, b and c are constant parameters defined in the contract.

Suppose X is the price of Brent crude oil, the valuation date for each month’s supply is the end of the preceding month (so the valuation date for the January 20X2 supply is 31 December 20X1), and the payment date for each month is the 20th of that month (so the payment date for the January 20X2 supply is 20 January 20X2.

This means that, with reference only to the Brent component, the monthly prices are rolling averages so that P1 (the price for supply in January 20X2) is function of Brent prices from October 20X1 to December 20X1, P2 (the price for supply in February 20X2) is function of Brent prices from November 20X1 to January 20X2, and so on.

In accordance with its risk management strategy, entity A wishes to hedge the Brent risk component using monthly Brent futures quoted in the market, with a bottom layer approach (that is, it hedges only the first units of supply each month, up to a specified quantity, to ensure that the forecast supply being hedged is highly probable).

Entity A calculates the total exposure to Brent by summing each monthly contribution that arises under the pricing formula in the contract. For example, the Brent price in December 20X1 contributes to the average price calculated for the payment dates from January 20X2 to March 20X2; so, if the quantity being hedged is equivalent to n barrels of Brent per month, this means that the total exposure to the December 20X1 Brent price will be 3*n barrels.

Entity A hedges this exposure with a single futures contract for each month’s Brent price (maturing on 31 December 20X1, if available, or with a proxy hedge otherwise). The exposure to the Brent price for subsequent dates is hedged in the same way. If a proxy is used, it should be noted that there could be ineffectiveness due to timing differences, pricing, or differences in volumes.

**Question 1**

Can entity A designate its exposure to each month’s Brent price separately as a different hedged item (component) in a different hedging relationship? For example, can entity A designate its exposure to the December 20X1 Brent price separately from its exposure to the January 20X2 Brent price?

**Answer 1**

Yes, provided this is consistent with entity A’s risk management strategy. Given the pricing formula, each month’s Brent price exposure is separately identifiable and its contribution to the changes in cash flows is reliably measurable. Hence each month’s exposure meets the criteria in paragraphs 6.3.7(a) and B6.3.10(a) of IFRS 9 for designation as a separate component. However, entity A would need to track each component and the three months’ deliveries to which it relates separately, to ensure that amounts are recycled from OCI to profit or loss in the correct periods, which will be a complex exercise.

**Question 2**

Consider the case where a Brent futures contract maturing in December 20X1 is not available, so entity A undertakes a proxy hedge using a Brent futures contract maturing at 30 September 20X1. Entity A has assessed that there is an economic relationship between the December 20X1 price of Brent (the risk component being hedged) and the 30 September 20X1 price of Brent (the risk in the hedging instrument), and that the other conditions for hedge accounting are met. Can the September 20X1 derivative be used as a proxy for the December 20X1 price and, if so, how should entity A set the hedge ratio when applying hedge accounting?

**Answer 2**

Yes, entity A can use a September derivative as a proxy. It is using the ‘best’ (that is, most liquid) instrument available at the time when it enters into the hedge. This is a common hedging strategy. Once a better hedging instrument becomes available, it is likely to replace the September derivative with a December derivative.

Paragraph 6.4.1 of IFRS 9 requires the hedge ratio to be generally the same as that actually used (that is, that results from the quantity of the hedged item that the entity actually hedges and the quantity of the hedging instrument that the entity actually uses to hedge that quantity of hedged item). In other words, the hedge ratio must be in accordance with:

- the risk management strategy (strategy is to use proxy hedges where available); and

- the actual hedging instrument taken out (which might not be exactly 1:1, because not using a December derivative).

This paragraph also requires entity A to document how it determines the hedge ratio, which would include the methodology applied to calculate and update the hedge ratio. Moreover, entity A should periodically consider if it needs to rebalance the hedge ratio, since correlation between the December 20X1 and the 30 September 20X1 prices of Brent could change.

The same paragraph also requires entity A to document sources of ineffectiveness. In this kind of hedging relationship, these will include:

- block contract sizes that might result in differences between the actual quantities hedged under the supply contract and those in the futures contracts used to hedge;

- different maturity dates that result from the proxy hedging strategy described above, or from hedging a monthly average price specified in the contract (for example, the average price in December 20X1) with a futures contract whose underlying is a single date in the month (such as the price on 31 December 20X1);

- differences in the timing between the cash flows under the supply contract and the futures contracts used to hedge; and

- changes in the credit risk of either entity A or the counterparty to the derivative contract (although, in the case of a futures contract with daily margin requirements, these are likely to be minimal).

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.71.5"></a>FAQ 46.71.5 – Can a fair value hedge of the foreign currency risk component of a non-financial asset qualify for hedge accounting?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 paras 6.3.7, 6.4.1, B6.3.19, IFRIC agenda decision September 2019  
 Reference to standing text: [46.71](74.md#s298-Para_46.71) 

  

  
 **Illustration**

An entity might wish to designate a foreign currency liability (such as a foreign currency borrowing or lease liability) as a hedging instrument in a hedge of a foreign currency risk component of an item of property, plant and equipment.

**Question**

Can such a designation qualify for hedge accounting?

**Answer**

The IFRS IC issued an agenda decision in September 2019 on this question. It noted that the following four questions are relevant to such hedging relationships:

1. Can an entity have exposure to foreign currency risk on a non-financial asset held for consumption that could affect profit or loss?

The IFRS IC noted that, in limited circumstances, it is possible for an entity to have exposure to foreign currency risk on a non-financial asset held for consumption that could affect profit or loss. These circumstances would arise for a narrow set of non-financial assets where, at a global level, the fair value of the non-financial asset is determined only in one currency and that currency is not the entity’s functional currency.

2. If an entity has exposure to foreign currency risk on a non-financial asset, is it a separately identifiable and reliably measurable risk component?

Paragraph 6.3.7 of IFRS 9 permits an entity to designate a risk component of an item as the hedged item if, “based on an assessment within the context of the particular market structure, the risk component is separately identifiable and reliably measurable”.

The IFRS IC noted that, where the fair value of the non-financial asset is determined globally only in one currency other than the functional currency of the entity, it might be possible to determine that the foreign currency risk is separately identifiable and reliably measurable in the context of IFRS 9.

However, it should be noted that the currency in which transactions are routinely settled would not necessarily be the same currency in which the non-financial asset is priced. Therefore, it is necessary to demonstrate that there is a single currency which is used to determine fair value, and not merely for settlement.

3. Can the designation of foreign currency risk on a non-financial asset held for consumption be consistent with an entity’s risk management activities?

Paragraph 6.4.1(b) of IFRS 9 requires that, at the inception of a hedging relationship, “there is formal designation and documentation of the hedging relationship and the entity’s risk management objective and strategy for undertaking the hedge”.

Accordingly, the IFRS IC noted that it is important for an entity to demonstrate that the hedging relationship is consistent with the entity’s risk management objective and strategy.

If an entity intends to consume a non-financial asset in the ordinary course of business rather than to sell it, the entity is unlikely to be managing and hedging exposure on foreign currency risk in accordance with its risk management objective and strategy. The entity might, in fact, be seeking to manage foreign exchange risk on the foreign currency liability (rather than on the non-financial asset), and therefore it would be inconsistent with risk management objectives and strategy to designate the liability as the hedging instrument.

However, a designation consistent with the risk management objective and strategy might be possible, in limited circumstances, where:

- the entity expects to sell the non-financial asset (such as an item of property, plant and equipment) part-way through its economic life;

- the expected residual value of the asset at the date of sale is significant; and

- the entity manages and uses hedging instruments to hedge the foreign currency exposure only on the residual value of the asset.

4. What other challenges are relevant to this type of hedging relationship?

The IFRS IC noted that an entity applies all other applicable requirements in IFRS 9 in determining whether it can apply fair value hedge accounting in its particular circumstances, including requirements related to the designation of the hedged item and hedging instrument, and hedge effectiveness. For example, an entity would consider how its hedge accounting designation addresses any differences in the size, depreciation/amortisation pattern and expected sale/maturity of the hedged item and the hedging instrument.

One particular requirement that might be relevant is set out in paragraph B6.3.19 of IFRS 9. This requires that, if a layer component is designated in a fair value hedge, an entity should specify a layer component from a defined nominal amount. It will often be difficult to determine that the hedged item would have a defined nominal amount in a foreign currency, because it is likely that the fair value will fluctuate in the foreign currency based on market supply/demand and a number of other factors other than foreign currency risk. That is, unlike a financial instrument such as a bond which has a defined nominal amount in a foreign currency, the non-financial item will often not have a fixed nominal amount.

Where hedge accounting is applied, the entity should give the disclosures required by IFRS 7, including information about its risk management strategy and how it is applied to manage risk.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.72.1"></a>FAQ 46.72.1 – In determining whether a risk component is an eligible hedged component of a non-financial item, what is meant by the market structure in which the hedging activity takes place?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 paras B6.3.8–B6.3.10  
 Reference to standing text: [46.72](74.md#s298-Para_46.72)  Corporate Treasury

  

  
 **Question**

In determining whether a risk component is an eligible hedged component of a non-financial item, what is meant by the market structure in which the hedging activity takes place?

**Illustration**

To be eligible for designation as a hedged item, a risk component must be a separately identifiable component, and the changes in cash flows or fair value of the item attributable to change in that component must be reliably measurable. [IFRS 9 App B para B6.3.8].

IFRS 9 also requires the qualifying criteria for an eligible risk component to be assessed in the context of the particular market structure to which the risks relate and in which the hedging activity takes place. [IFRS 9 App B para B6.3.9].

**Answer**

In the case of a non-financial item, for there to be a hedgeable risk component it would generally be necessary for the price of the entire item to be built up from various components using a ‘building block’ approach to demonstrate an appropriate market structure. For example, it should be clear that informed buyers and sellers of this non-financial item would consider the price of the component proposed to be hedged (such as raw materials) in establishing the price of the overall non-financial item. Consideration of the approach of other market participants is necessary to demonstrate that the market structure supports the designation as a risk component.

The evidence required to support the market structure is a matter of judgement based on facts and circumstances, but should not rely solely on the entity’s own negotiating position or standard costing systems.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.73.1"></a>FAQ 46.73.1 – How should an entity determine whether changes in cash flows or fair value attributable to changes in a hedged component are separately identifiable?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 paras B6.3.8–B6.3.10  
 Reference to standing text: [46.73](74.md#s298-Para_46.73)  Corporate Treasury

  

  
 **Question**

How should an entity determine whether changes in cash flows or fair value attributable to changes in a hedged component are separately identifiable? Is it sufficient for a risk component to be an input in the manufacturing process of a non-financial item?

**Illustration**

To be eligible for designation as a hedged item, a risk component must be a separately identifiable component, and the changes in cash flows or fair value of the item attributable to change in that component must be reliably measurable [IFRS 9 App B para B6.3.8].

An entity wishes to hedge a non-contractually specified risk component of a non-financial item. If the risk component is an input in the manufacturing process of the non-financial item, is this sufficient for the risk component to be an eligible hedged item?

**Answer**

No. In general, it will be necessary for the risk component to be an input in the manufacturing process in order to be considered as ‘separately identifiable’, and so meet the criteria in IFRS 9. However, merely being an input in the manufacturing process is not, in itself, sufficient. Paragraph B6.3.9 of IFRS 9 requires that the qualifying criteria for an eligible risk component should be assessed in the context of the particular market structure to which the risks relate and in which the hedging activity takes place.

For example, wool is an input in the manufacturing process for woollen sweaters. Whether the wool price is a hedgeable risk component of the total price of the sweater depends on the market structure for the particular sweater. In the luxury apparel market, the price of the raw material (wool) will often have a low impact on the price of a woollen sweater that will instead mainly reflect the perceived value of the brand. In such a market, the wool price is unlikely to be an eligible risk component for a retailer. On the other hand, the price of a ‘no frills’ sweater of a generic design might be driven by the cost of the inputs into the manufacturing process (such as raw materials, labour and overheads) and a production margin. This is likely to give rise to a hedgeable risk component for the wool price component.

While it will, in general, be necessary for the risk component to be an input into the manufacturing process, there might be rare cases where the market structure is clear that a non-financial risk component is a separately identifiable component, even in the absence of a physical presence. For example, in some markets a long-term supply contract for natural gas might be based on a contractually specified formula that refers to indices including, inter alia, the price of crude oil. If a supply contract is in place, the crude oil price component would be considered to be an eligible risk component, because it is ‘explicitly specified’ in the contract. Equally, if a supply contract is not yet in place but the market commonly prices purchases of natural gas under long-term contracts in this manner, the crude oil price component would still be a hedgeable non-contractually specified risk component. This would be true for a highly probable forecast purchase of natural gas beyond the period for which liquid derivatives are available, despite crude oil not being an input in the manufacturing process for natural gas, when such pricing mechanism for natural gas (that is, the linkage to the crude oil prices) is the ‘industry norm’. A different conclusion could be reached in a jurisdiction where pricing for natural gas is not based on crude oil (for example, where there is a liquid spot and forward market for natural gas for the period being hedged).

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.73.2"></a>FAQ 46.73.2 – How should an entity determine whether changes in cash flows or fair value attributable to changes in a hedged component are reliably measurable?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 paras B6.3.8–B6.3.10  
 Reference to standing text: [46.73](74.md#s298-Para_46.73)  Corporate Treasury

  

  
 **Question**

How should an entity determine whether changes in cash flows or fair value attributable to changes in a hedged component are reliably measurable?

**Illustration**

To be eligible for designation as a hedged item, a risk component must be a separately identifiable component, and the changes in cash flows or fair value of the item attributable to change in that component must be reliably measurable. [IFRS 9 App B para B6.3.8].

Company A wishes to designate a risk component as a hedged item. How should company A assess if the 'reliably measurable' criterion is met?

**Answer**

Changes in cash flows or fair value attributable to changes in the hedged component would be considered reliably measurable where the price of a non-contractually specified risk component has a predictable and direct impact on the price of the entire item. A linear relationship, where changes in the price of the risk component have an equivalent (though not necessarily one-to-one) impact on the price of the entire item, in the absence of changes in other inputs, would generally create such a predictable and direct impact.

Nevertheless, a non-linear relationship might also be sufficient for a risk component to be considered an eligible hedged component. For example, the prices of many items are only updated periodically, or where input prices increase or decrease beyond a reasonable threshold, creating ‘stepped’ changes in the link between the price of the risk component and the price of the entire item. Accordingly, an entity will need to analyse the nature of the non-linear relationship, and the reasons for that relationship, to establish whether the risk component is implicit in the fair value or cash flows of the entire item and hence is an eligible hedged component.

Factors to be considered in making this assessment should include:

- Absolute magnitude of the ‘steps’, before a change in the price of the risk component influences the change in price of the entire item. For example, the price of a luxury wool sweater will have limited correlation with the price of the raw material (wool).

- Frequency of updates to the price of the entire item in response to changes in the price of the risk component.

- Sensitivity of changes in the price of the entire item to its supply and demand (and the supply and demand of substitutes), compared with changes in the price of the risk component.

- Whether changes to the price of the entire item are one-sided (such as increases only) or prices are ‘stickier’ in one direction (for example, price increases occur more frequently or in greater magnitude than decreases).

- The reason that changes in the price of the component are not passed on immediately. For example, where increases in the price of the component are not passed on because the market cannot fully absorb such changes, this might indicate that the market structure is not consistent with a ‘building block’ approach.

Whether or not the impact of a risk component on the price of the entire item is non-linear is factored into the assessment of whether it qualifies as an eligible hedged component of the non-financial item; if an eligible risk component exists, it does not impact hedge effectiveness. Accordingly, once an eligible risk component has been identified, it is assumed to have a linear impact on the hedged item. This is because the non-linear element in the overall pricing reflects the willingness of an entity to absorb certain input price movements within its profit margin rather than pass the increase on to customers. In such circumstances the underlying relationship is linear, but the entity is willing to temporarily reduce or increase its margin. Accordingly, part of the profit margin is inversely correlated with the risk component for short periods and/or small price changes, which does not preclude the use of a linear relationship for assessing hedge effectiveness. If this inverse correlation exists for longer periods or larger price changes, however, it would call into question the original assessment that there is an eligible risk component and the validity of the hedge as a risk management strategy.

Assessing whether a risk component is implicit in the fair value or the cash flows of the entire item, and whether the changes in fair value or cash flows that are attributable to the risk component are reliably measurable is highly judgemental. Practice is likely to develop based on risk management strategies undertaken by entities. Management will need robust evidence, demonstrating the particular market structure, to support its assertion that the risk component qualifies as a hedged item. Given the judgement involved this could be a significant accounting judgement under IAS 1 that would require disclosure in the financial statements.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.76.1"></a>FAQ 46.76.1 – In which circumstances can partial term hedging be allowed?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para BC6.95; IAS 39 para IG F2.17  
 Reference to standing text: [46.76](74.md#s298-Para_46.76) 

  

**Question**

In which circumstances can partial term hedging be allowed?

**Answer**

Although IFRS 9 does not specifically address partial term hedges, we believe that they are permitted in some circumstances as noted below:

- Financial instruments – The IAS 39 Implementation Guidance (at para F2.17 on partial term hedging) allows hedging a financial instrument (the hedged item) for only a portion of its cash flows or fair value, if effectiveness can be measured and the other hedge accounting criteria are met. Although IFRS 9 did not carry forward the IAS 39 Implementation Guidance, in its Basis for Conclusions, the IASB emphasised that not carrying forward the Implementation Guidance did not mean that it had rejected that guidance. Given this fact partial term hedges of financial items is still possible in some circumstances under IFRS 9. See [FAQ 46.76.2](76.md#s304-FAQ_46.76.2) for an example. [IFRS 9 para BC6.95]. [IAS 39 para IG F2.17].

- Forecast transactions – Partial term hedges are not permitted for hedges of forecast transactions. This is because there is nothing in IFRS 9 or IAS 39 to suggest that a time portion is an eligible portion of the period until the transaction occurs, and so the hedged item must be based on the full term.

For example, if a hedge of the foreign currency risk of a forecast transaction is hedged with a foreign currency derivative of shorter duration, it will not be fully effective, given the hedged item must be measured based on the full term until the forecast transaction is expected to occur. See [FAQ 46.76.3](76.md#s304-FAQ_46.76.3) for an example of ineffectiveness that could occur, and [FAQ 46.76.4](76.md#s304-FAQ_46.76.4) for an example of a potential solution based on adjusting the hedging instrument.

Additionally, even if the entity designates the hedge as a hedge of changes in spot exchange rates as part of a rolling strategy, it would need to use discounted spot under IFRS 9 to measure the effect of the hedged risk on the hedged item, which might still create ineffectiveness. See [paragraphs 46.54](74.md#s297-Para_46.54) to [46.55](74.md#s297-Para_46.55).

[IFRS 9 App B para B6.5.4].

In addition, it should be noted that the notion of ‘partial term’ does not apply to a hedging instrument as stated in [paragraph 46.106](74.md#s299-Para_46.106). Therefore, hedges of shorter-lived items will result in ineffectiveness.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.76.2"></a>FAQ 46.76.2 – Can cash flows or fair value movements be hedged for only a portion of the time to maturity of a financial asset or financial liability?

 

Reference to standard:IFRS 9 para BC6.95; IAS 39 para IG F2.17  
 Reference to standing text: [46.76](74.md#s298-Para_46.76) 

  

  
 **Question**

Can cash flows or fair value movements be hedged for only a portion of the time to maturity of a financial asset or financial liability?

**Illustration**

Entity A acquires a 10% fixed-rate government bond with a remaining term to maturity of 10 years. Entity A measures the bond at amortised cost. On the same date, to hedge against fair value exposure on the bond associated with the first five years of its life, the entity acquires a five-year pay fixed, receive floating swap. The swap has a fair value of zero at the inception of the hedge relationship.

**Answer**

Yes. The IAS 39 Implementation Guidance (at para F2.17 on partial term hedging) allows hedging a financial instrument (the hedged item) for only a portion of its cash flows or fair value, if effectiveness can be measured and the other hedge accounting criteria are met. Although IFRS 9 did not carry forward the IAS 39 Implementation Guidance, in its Basis for Conclusions, the IASB emphasised that not carrying forward the Implementation Guidance did not mean that it had rejected that guidance.

The swap could be designated as hedging the fair value exposure of the interest rate payments on the government bond until year 5, and the change in value of the principal payment due at maturity, to the extent affected by changes in the yield curve relating to the five years of the swap.

The same principle applies if the hedged item had been a financial liability instead of a financial asset with the same terms. In that situation, the entity could designate the fair value exposure of the first five years’ interest payments due to changes in interest rate only, and hedge that exposure using a five-year receive fixed, pay floating interest rate swap.

The entity is also able to achieve hedge accounting for partial term cash flow hedges of financial items. For instance, assume an entity issues a 10-year floating-rate debt and wishes to hedge the variability in the first three years of interest payments using a three-year receive floating, pay fixed interest rate swap. The entity could designate the swap as hedging the variability in cash flows arising from the first three years of interest payments.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.76.3"></a>FAQ 46.76.3 – Can foreign currency risk be hedged for a portion of the time to maturity of a hedged item (forecast sale)?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para BC6.95; IAS 39 para IG F2.17  
 Reference to standing text: [46.76](74.md#s298-Para_46.76) 

  

  
 **Question**

Company A, whose functional currency is the euro, has a forecast GBP sale in nine months’ time and wishes to hedge the foreign exchange exposure for the first three months. In order to hedge the expected foreign exchange volatility in the first three months, it entered into a three-month forward to swap GBP for euros. Could this relationship be designated as a partial term hedge of the foreign currency exposure for only the first three months?

**Answer**

No, IFRS 9 does not permit partial term hedges other than in certain specific circumstances (see [FAQ 46.76.1](76.md#s304-FAQ_46.76.1)). However, company A could potentially designate a hedge for the full period using the forward rate method, provided the mismatch is not so long as to invalidate the economic relationship. The mismatch in periods between the hedged item and the hedging instrument will potentially create ineffectiveness, because the hypothetical derivative used to measure effectiveness would need to reflect the term of the hedged item. Such ineffectiveness will arise regardless of whether the designated hedged risk is the forward or the spot foreign currency rate, due to the fact that the requirement in IFRS 9 to consider the time value of money is applicable in both circumstances.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.76.4"></a>FAQ 46.76.4 – Does designation of a forward FX contract for the discounted value, to hedge the undiscounted forecasted transaction, comply with IFRS 9's requirement to consider the time value of money?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para BC6.5.4  
 Reference to standing text: [46.76](74.md#s298-Para_46.76)  Corporate Treasury

  

  
**Question**

Does designation of a forward FX contract of 85FC, to hedge a forecast transaction of 100FC, comply with IFRS 9's requirement to consider the time value of money?

**Illustration**

An entity has a highly probable forecast transaction of 100FC in 10 years’ time. It takes out a FX forward to hedge FX risk for the first two years. The entity designates the spot element only as the hedged risk and defers the FV changes relating to forward points in the cost of hedging reserve. The entity discounts the amount of 100FC from year 10 to year 2 using an appropriate rate (such as the eight-year rate in two years’ time, based on the implied forward curve), giving a hedged amount of 85FC. This amount is used to determine the amount of the forward FX contract that the entity takes out in the market to hedge the spot FX risk during the first two years.

**Answer**

Yes. Paragraph B6.5.4 of IFRS 9 requires entities to consider the time value of money when measuring hedge ineffectiveness. The hedged amount of 85FC takes account of the effects of discounting from year 10 to year 2 (since it reflects the eight-year rate in two years’ time) and, as such, is in line with this requirement. There is no requirement to update the discount rate for changes in the yield curve after initial designation as the hedged risk is spot FX risk rather than incorporating forward points or interest rate risk. This means that the FX forward of 85FC should be effective in offsetting the spot FX risk on the hedged cash flow of 100FC over the first two years, although other sources of ineffectiveness might arise.

Note that, for the purposes of applying the cost of hedging model, the aligned forward will have a maturity of 10 years, which might also give rise to ineffectiveness.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.76.5"></a>FAQ 46.76.5 – Can a five-year foreign currency borrowing be used to hedge revenue expected in two years’ time?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 paras 6.2.2,B6.2.3  
 Reference to standing text: [46.76](74.md#s298-Para_46.76)  Corporate treasury

  

  
**Illustration**

Entity A has a highly probable foreign currency sale in two years’ time. It has also issued a five-year loan in the same currency as the forecast sale.

**Question**

Can entity A use the foreign currency loan as a hedge of the foreign exchange risk on the forecast sale, even though there is a mismatch on the timing of cash flows?

**Answer**

Yes. Under IFRS 9, entity A could designate the entire borrowing as the hedging instrument to hedge the foreign currency risk of a hedged item (the forecast sale) that has a shorter life. For hedges of foreign currency risk, the foreign currency component of the borrowing is determined in accordance with paragraph IAS 21 [IFRS 9 para B6.2.3]. It might therefore appear that the spot translation of the forecast sale and of the foreign currency borrowing will perfectly offset, and there will be no ineffectiveness despite the timing mismatch.

However, paragraph B6.5.4 of IFRS 9 requires the measurement of the ineffectiveness of a hedge relationship to reflect the time value of money, and hence ineffectiveness could arise due to a mismatch in timing of the cash flows on the hedged item (forecast transaction in two years) compared with the timing of interest and principal cash flows on the loan (over five years).

It should be noted that, for a short-term hedge between currencies with limited interest rate volatility, the impact of this discounting might not be significant. However, it could be more significant for longer-term hedges. Entities will therefore need to consider the impact of the IFRS 9 requirements on their risk management strategy.

Also see [FAQ 46.140.2](77.md#s305-FAQ_46.140.2) for further examples of the implications of taking the time value of money into account when hedging revenue.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.77.1"></a>FAQ 46.77.1 – Can management designate forecast sales in time buckets if it is unable to link the forecast future cash flows to individual sales transactions?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B6.3.18  
 Reference to standing text: [46.77](74.md#s298-Para_46.77), [46.35](74.md#s297-Para_46.35) 

  

  
**Question**

Can management designate forecast sales as the hedged item if it is unable to link the forecast future cash flows to specific individual sales transactions?

**Illustration**

Company T manufactures and sells ice cream. Its functional currency is the Euro, and 30% of its sales are made in the UK and denominated in GBP. Management forecasts highly probable sales in the UK for the next summer season on a monthly basis. Using these forecasts, the company enters into forward contracts to sell GBP in exchange for Euros. Due to the nature of its business, company T is not able to forecast or track individual sales transactions.

**Answer**

Yes. Management can designate the forecast sales as the hedged item by using the layer approach. The layer approach is specifically permitted by IFRS 9. It would designate the hedged item as the first GBP X million of highly probable cash flows from sales in specific time buckets (for example, GBP sales in each month). To qualify for hedge accounting, the designation must be sufficiently specific to ensure that, when a forecast transaction occurs, it is possible to determine objectively whether that transaction is or is not one that is hedged. In addition, IFRS 9 requires such forecast sales to be discounted from the date when they are expected to occur. In the event that the date is not aligned to that of the hedging instrument, some ineffectiveness will arise.

If the hedged cash flows do not occur in the designated time bucket, management cannot continue to defer the related hedging gains/losses in equity and must transfer them to the income statement. Under IFRS 7, an entity will need to disclose the amounts reclassified from the cash flow hedge reserve into profit or loss, because the hedged item has affected profit or loss separately from the amounts that have been transferred; this is because the hedged future cash flows are no longer expected to occur.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.79.1"></a>FAQ 46.79.1 – Could pre-payment risk be excluded from the designation of a fair value hedge of a portfolio of pre-payable assets for interest rate risk?

 

Reference to standard:  IFRS 9 para B6.3.20  
 Reference to standing text: [46.79](74.md#s298-Para_46.79) 

  

**Question**

Could pre-payment risk be excluded from the designation of a fair value hedge of a portfolio of pre-payable assets for interest rate risk?

**Answer**

  
 No. Pre-payment risk will impact the effectiveness of fair value hedges of pre-payable assets, such as mortgage loans and, therefore, should be taken into account when an entity designates the hedge relationship. The hedged mortgage loans’ contractual terms include the pre-payment option (for the borrower), which cannot be ignored.

Furthermore, pre-payment rates are primarily a function of interest rates and, hence pre-payment risk is a component of interest rate risk. Therefore, if an entity intends to hedge a portfolio of mortgage loans for interest rate risk, pre-payment risk cannot be excluded.

For instance, an entity might have a portfolio of C500 million of mortgage loans that are be pre-paid at par. The entity might wish to hedge the changes in fair value of this portfolio attributable to interest rate movements by entering into a simple receive variable, pay fixed interest rate swap. Such a swap, however, would not be a highly effective hedging instrument, because the hedged item contains a pre-payment option that is not present in the swap. However, if the entity can demonstrate (based on historical data) that C100 million of the portfolio would not pre-pay if the interest rate were to decline, it could designate that specific portion of the mortgage portfolio as the hedged item.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.79.2"></a>FAQ 46.79.2 – In a fair value hedge of pre-payable assets for interest rate risk, could cash flows be designated as a hedged item after the prepayment date?

 

Reference to standard:  IFRS 9 para B6.3.20  
 Reference to standing text: [46.79](74.md#s298-Para_46.79) 

  

**Question**

In a fair value hedge of pre-payable assets for interest rate risk, could cash flows be designated as a hedged item after the prepayment date?

**Answer**

  
 Yes. Cash flows after the pre-payment date could be designated as the hedged item to the extent that it can be demonstrated that they are ‘highly probable’. For example, cash flows after the pre-payment date might qualify as highly probable if they result from a group or pool of similar assets (for example, mortgage loans) for which pre-payments can be estimated with a high degree of accuracy or if the pre-payment option is significantly out-of-the-money.

In addition, the cash flows after the pre-payment date could be designated as the hedged item if a comparable option exists in the hedging instrument.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.81.1"></a>FAQ 46.81.1 – Can cash flows expected to occur as a result of entering into a firm commitment be designated in a cash flow hedge?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 paras 6.5.4, B6.5.3  
 Reference to standing text: [46.81](74.md#s298-Para_46.81) 

  

**Question**

Can cash flows expected to occur as a result of entering into a firm commitment be designated in a cash flow hedge?

**Answer**

  
 The IFRS 9 application guidance notes that a hedge of a firm commitment is a hedge of an exposure to a change in fair value. Accordingly, firm commitments are generally designated in fair value hedges. However, the existence of a firm commitment also supports the existence of highly probable forecast cash flows; and so, where there is some variability in timing, price or quantity under the firm commitment, we believe that the subsequent cash flow variability could alternatively be designated in a cash flow hedge of the relevant risk.

As noted in paragraph 6.5.4 of IFRS 9, the hedge of foreign currency risk on a firm commitment is specifically permitted as a cash flow hedge.

 

<a id="s304-chap_46_navPoint_133"></a>
### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.82.1"></a>FAQ 46.82.1 – How should an entity measure and recognise the fair value of a firm commitment designated in a fair value hedge?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 paras 6.5.8, 6.5.9  
 Reference to standing text: [46.82](74.md#s298-Para_46.82) 

  

**Question**

  
 On 1 January 20X6, entity A enters into a firm commitment to purchase 100,000 widgets for C5 each in six months’ time. On 31 March 20X6, the entity’s year end, the market price of the widgets has increased to C6.

The fair value of a firm commitment represents the amount that an entity would have to pay, or the amount that it would receive, upon terminating the commitment. On 1 January 20X6, the entity has an obligation to pay C500,000 in six months’ time and a right to receive 100,000 widgets in six months. The initial value of the obligation and the right are generally equal, resulting in a net fair value of zero. How should entity A reflect the change in fair value by the year end and over the remaining period of the hedge relationship?

**Answer**

At 31 March 20X6, when the widget’s market price increases to C6, the value of the right to receive widgets increases. This is because the widget’s value received in three months would be C600,000, while the obligation to pay C500,000 in three months would remain the same, resulting in a fair value that reflects the C100,000 difference adjusted for the discount that is appropriate for the remaining three-month duration of the commitment. The present value of C100,000 represents the amount that the entity could reasonably expect to receive if the counterparty to the commitment were to terminate the commitment at 31 March 20X6.

Firm commitments represent rights and obligations that are assets and liabilities, even though they are generally not recorded. If entity A designates the firm commitment as a hedged item in a hedge of the widget price risk component, it would account for the changes in the fair value of the hedged commitment due to changes in the market price of widgets in a manner similar to how that entity would account for any hedged asset or liability that it records. That is, changes in fair value (that are attributable to the risk that is being hedged) would be recognised in P&amp;L and, on the balance sheet, recognised as an adjustment of the hedged item’s carrying amount.

Because firm commitments are normally not recorded, accounting for the initial change in the fair value of the firm commitment would result in the entity recognising the firm commitment on the balance sheet. Therefore, in this example, the entity would record the present value of C100,000 as an asset on the balance sheet, with a corresponding gain in P&amp;L. Recognition of subsequent changes in fair value would adjust the amount of that recognised firm commitment. Assuming no further changes in fair value occurred since 31 March 20X6, the widgets would be recognised on the balance sheet on 30 June 20X6 at C600,000 (being the C500,000 purchase price plus the C100,000 basis adjustment).

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.83.1"></a>FAQ 46.83.1 – How does an entity assess whether a transaction is highly probable?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para 6.3.3  
 Reference to standing text: [46.83](74.md#s298-Para_46.83) 

  

**Question**

How does an entity assess whether a transaction is highly probable?

**Answer**

  
 In IFRS terminology, probable means ‘more likely than not’. Therefore, in the context of forecast transactions, the term ‘highly probable’ is taken to indicate a much greater likelihood of happening than the term ‘more likely than not’. This is consistent with the IASB’s use of ‘highly probable’ in IFRS 5, where the term is regarded as implying a significantly higher probability than ‘more likely than not’.

[IFRS 5 para BC81].

In assessing the likelihood that a transaction will occur, the following factors (where relevant) should be considered:

- The frequency of similar past transactions.

- The financial and operational ability of the entity to carry out the transaction.

- Substantial commitments of resources to a particular activity (for example, a manufacturing facility that can be used in the short run only to process a particular type of commodity).

- The extent of loss or disruption of operations that could result if the transaction does not occur.

- The likelihood that transactions with substantially different characteristics might be used to achieve the same business purpose (for example, an entity that intends to raise cash might have several ways of doing so, ranging from a short-term bank loan to an offering of ordinary shares).

- The entity’s business plan.

The length of time until a forecast transaction is projected to occur is also a factor in determining probability. Other factors being equal, the more distant a forecast transaction is, the less likely it is that the transaction would be regarded as highly probable, and the stronger the evidence that would be needed to support an assertion that it is highly probable. For example, a transaction forecast to occur in five years might be less likely to occur than a transaction forecast to occur in one year. However, forecast interest payments for the next 20 years, on a plain vanilla variable-rate debt, would typically be highly probable if supported by an existing contractual obligation and an expectation that the debt will not be paid early.

In addition, other factors being equal, the greater a forecast transaction’s physical quantity or future value in proportion to the entity’s transactions of the same nature, the less likely it is that the transaction would be regarded as highly probable, and the stronger the evidence that would be required to support an assertion that it is highly probable. For example, less evidence generally would be needed to support forecast sales of 100,000 units in the next month than 950,000 units in that month, where recent sales have averaged 800,000 units per month for the past three months.

Where the forecast transaction is a single, one-off transaction (such as the forecast issuance of debt), the assessment of whether the transaction is highly probable will need to address other factors, such as the availability of alternative sources of finance, the status of negotiations with the counterparty, the business purpose for which the finance is being raised, and the consequences for the business of not proceeding with the debt issuance.

A history of having designated hedges of forecast transactions, and then determining that the forecast transactions are no longer expected to occur, would call into question both an entity’s ability to predict forecast transactions accurately and the propriety of using hedge accounting in the future for similar forecast transactions.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.83.2"></a>FAQ 46.83.2 – Can a hedge of the foreign currency risk on a forecast business combination be accounted for as a cash flow hedge

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 paras 6.3.1, 6.3.3, 6.2.4(b)  
 Reference to standing text: [46.83](74.md#s298-Para_46.83)  Corporate Treasury

  

  
 **Question 1**

Can a hedge of the foreign currency risk on a forecast business combination be accounted for as a cash flow hedge?

**Illustration**

Entity A, whose functional currency is the Euro, on 1 March 20X1 started negotiations with entity B (an overseas entity) in order to take control of entity B and gain access to the overseas market. On 1 August 20X1, entity A and entity B agree the key terms of the transaction, including the purchase price which will be denominated in a fixed amount of the overseas currency. However, entity A has not yet received a required approval from the overseas authorities. On 29 March 20X2, entity A and entity B agree the full terms of the transaction and the overseas authorities have granted their approval. At this point, there are no significant uncertainties that could affect the completion of the business combination. Entity A’s risk management strategy is to hedge forecast transactions in a foreign currency as soon as they are highly probable. The entity intends to use a forward contract to hedge the payment in the overseas currency.

Can hedge accounting be achieved in this scenario?

**Answer 1**

Yes. However, the forecast business combination must be highly probable in order to be an eligible hedged item.

**Question 2**

Could entity A apply hedge accounting to the forecast transaction at 1 August 20X1?

**Answer 2**

It depends. In order to apply hedge accounting to a forecast transaction, this transaction must be highly probable. Whether this is the case will depend on the specific facts and circumstances and the significance of the outstanding contingencies affecting the completion of the transaction. In some territories, a regulatory process could be considered perfunctory or a ‘rubber stamping’ of the agreement and so the transaction can be considered highly probable before receiving the formal approval. However, in other cases the regulatory approval process will be more substantive and there will be significant uncertainty over whether it will be received, such that the transaction can be considered highly probable only once the formal approval is received.

**Question 3**

 

Can entity A designate only the spot component of the forward contract as the hedging instrument?

 

**Answer 3**

 

Yes. Once the hedge accounting criteria are met, the entity can choose either to designate the full fair value of the forward contract as a hedging instrument or to separate the forward points element and to designate only the spot component of the hedging instrument (on a discounted basis). Similarly, the entity can choose to separate the foreign currency basis spread and exclude it from the designated hedge. Changes in the value of the forward element/foreign currency basis spread are either taken to P&amp;L or to OCI if the cost of hedging approach is applied.

 

**Question 4**

 

If the acquirer elects to apply the cost of hedging approach to the hedging relationship, how would this impact the accounting for the acquisition?

 

**Answer 4**

 

[FAQ 46.125.1](77.md#s305-FAQ_46.125.1), [FAQ 46.128.1](77.md#s305-FAQ_46.128.1) and [FAQ 46.128.2](77.md#s305-FAQ_46.128.2) provide further detail on how to separate and account for forward points and currency basis. A hedge of a forecast business combination is a transaction-related hedge of the consideration to be paid for the acquisition, and not a time-related hedge. Therefore, when the business combination occurs, the forward points and currency basis deferred in equity under the cost of hedging approach would be transferred from equity (together with the amount accumulated in the cash flow hedge reserve for the effective portion of the hedge) and adjust the foreign currency purchase price. This will adjust the amount of goodwill recognised.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.83.3"></a>FAQ 46.83.3 – Can a forecast cash flow of an associate be designated as a hedged item in an entity’s separate financial statements?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 paras 6.3.1, 6.3.3, 6.5.2(c)  
 Reference to standing text: [46.83](74.md#s298-Para_46.83)  Corporate Treasury

  

**Question**

Can a forecast cash flow of an associate be designated as a hedged item in an entity’s separate financial statements?

**Illustration**

Company R has a 25% investment in a foreign entity over which it has significant influence. It therefore accounts for the foreign entity as an associate using the equity method (IAS 28). The associate’s functional currency, in which most of its sales and costs are denominated, differs from company R’s functional currency. In company R’s consolidated financial statements, its share of the associate’s net results will fluctuate with the changes in the exchange rate.

In its separate financial statements company R intends to designate a portion of the associate’s forecast cash flows as the hedged item in a hedge of foreign currency risk.

**Answer**

No. Under the functional currency concept in IAS 21, a cash flow that is denominated in the functional currency of an associate does not give rise to a foreign currency (transaction) exposure for the associate in its separate financial statements. The variability in company R’s share of its associate’s net results arises only in its consolidated financial statements and arises from the translation of the associate’s financial statements into the group’s presentation currency. This is a translation rather than a transaction exposure.

IFRS 9 permits an entity to apply hedge accounting to a hedge of the translation foreign currency risk arising on the existing net investment in a foreign operation; but this should not be applied, by analogy, to forecasts of future cash flows or profits of the investee.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.83.4"></a>FAQ 46.83.4 – Can an entity designate a hedging relationship when it is unlikely that the maturity of the hedging instrument and the hedged item will match?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para 6.3.1 and 6.3.3  
 Reference to standing text: [46.83](74.md#s298-Para_46.83)  Corporate Treasury

  

**Question**

Can an entity designate a hedging relationship when it is unlikely that the maturity of the hedging instrument and the hedged item will match?

  
 **Illustration**

Company J enters into cash flow hedges of highly probable forecast sales in a foreign currency (USD) in June 20X6. It is not possible to determine exactly when in June the individual sales will occur. Company J therefore decides to hedge the first x million of sales in USD in June 20X6. The hedging instrument, however, will mature on a specified day (for example, 15 June 20X6).

**Answer**

Yes. There is no requirement for the maturity date of the hedged item to match exactly the maturity of the hedging instrument. However, company J’s risk management strategy should recognise and accept that timing mismatches might give rise to ineffectiveness. In addition, if the derivative matures after the hedged item, it cannot be designated only for the change in value of the derivative attributable to the period until the hedged item occurs. This is because, under IFRS 9, it is not possible to designate only a portion of the time period for which a hedging instrument remains outstanding.

Refer to paragraph [46.76](74.md#s298-Para_46.76) and [FAQs 46.76.1](76.md#s304-FAQ_46.76.1)–46.76.3 for further information on partial term hedges.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.83.5"></a>FAQ 46.83.5 – Can a commercial paper roll-over program qualify as a hedged item?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para 6.3.1 and 6.3.3  
 Reference to standing text: [46.83](74.md#s298-Para_46.83)  Corporate Treasury

  

**Question**

Can a commercial paper roll-over program qualify as a hedged item?

**Illustration**

Entity A, in order to meet its current and future cash liquidity needs, enters into a US commercial paper issuance program where the company regularly issues short-term commercial paper. The commercial paper is issued either on a one-month, three-month or six-month maturity depending on the market situation and the entity’s liquidity forecast. These are periodically rolled over. The entity’s liquidity policy is to have a minimum of USD250 million and a maximum of USD500 million outstanding at all times under this program. This commercial paper program creates variability in the future interest cash flows of entity A.

The risk management strategy of entity A is to fix the interest rate on its debt for a proportion of 60% to 80% of its total debt on a three-year horizon. In line with this strategy, entity A contracts a pay fixed, receive the benchmark interest rate three-month swap for a nominal of USD200 million and a maturity of 3 years (that is, 75% of the minimum estimated future issuance of commercial paper).

**Answer**

Yes. The roll over program can qualify as a hedged item, provided that it is highly probable for the duration of the hedging relationship. The hedging relationship can be designated, provided it meets the qualifying criteria in IFRS 9 (for example, that an economic relationship exists between the one-, three- and six-month SONIA rates inherent in the commercial paper roll-overs and the three-month benchmark interest rate underlying the interest rate swap settlements).

Ineffectiveness will be measured using a hypothetical swap with characteristics that match the characteristics of the commercial paper issuance program (for example, with a variable leg based on an average rate with a relevant mix of one-, three- and six-month benchmark interest ).

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.83.6"></a>FAQ 46.83.6 – Are the illustrated cash flow hedge designations of future interest flows permitted under IFRS 9?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 paras 6.4.1, 6.6.3  
  Reference to standing text: [46.83](74.md#s298-Para_46.83)  

  

  
**Question**

Are the following cash flow hedge designations of future interest flows permitted under IFRS 9?

**Illustration**

Consider the following scenarios. Please note: In all scenarios both the swap and the hedged debt are denominated in Company A’s functional currency.

**Scenario 1**

Company A enters into a forward starting swap in which it pays a fixed rate and receives a floating interest rate to hedge a highly probable forecast debt issuance. The date of issuance is known, but it is not known whether the debt will be at fixed or floating rates. Company A designates the swap as a cash flow hedge of the variability in cash flows of the debt to be issued, due to changes in interest rates. As a result, the company considers the following:

- If the forecast issuance is at fixed rate, the swap will be terminated (or an opposing swap with the same residual maturity will be taken out to close the swap position) and hedge accounting will be discontinued.

- If the forecast issuance is at floating rate, the hedge relationship is maintained with the existing swap and therefore hedge accounting will continue to be applied.

**Scenario 2**

Company B enters into an interest rate swap in which it pays a fixed rate and receives a floating interest rate. Company B designates the swap as a cash flow hedge of the variability, due to changes in interest rates, of the cash flows resulting from a combination of current floating-rate debt (with a maturity shorter than that of the swap), followed by a highly probable forecast issuance of either fixed- or floating- rate debt for the remaining term of the swap (the latter is similar to scenario 1).

**Scenario 3**

Company C enters into a similar structure as in scenario 2 above. However, in this case the precise date when the existing floating debt will be rolled over into either floating- or fixed-rate debt is not known. The company can demonstrate that it has a highly probable funding requirement of at least CU1 million throughout the life of the swap, which will be satisfied by issuing either fixed- or variable-rate debt. The swap is designated as a cash flow hedge of the variability of future interest cash flows on the first CU1 million of debt in issue over the life of the swap.

**Scenario 4**

Company D enters into an interest rate swap in which it pays a fixed rate and receives a floating interest rate. Company D designates the swap as a cash flow hedge of the variability, due to changes in interest rates, of the cash flows resulting from a combination of current floating-rate debt (with a maturity shorter than that of the swap), followed by highly probable further issuances of either fixed- or floating-rate debt over the remaining term of the swap. If the floating-rate debt is rolled over into a fixed-rate debt at some point during the life of the swap, company D will enter into an offsetting interest rate swap on which it pays a floating interest rate and receives a fixed interest rate. The aggregated position of the offsetting pay floating rate / receive fixed-rate swap and the fixed-rate debt is equivalent to a floating-rate loan.

**Answer**

Yes, all of the above designations are allowed under IFRS 9, provided that all of the qualifying criteria in paragraph 6.4.1 of IFRS 9 are met, including for example that the intention to hedge changes in interest rates is in line with the entity’s risk management strategy. Entities might designate their hedge relationships in alternative ways depending on their facts and circumstances. Designation of the risks associated with forecast transactions is permitted, as long as they are highly probable.

In the case of scenario 3, in which a layer is designated as the hedged item, paragraph 6.6.3 of IFRS 9 requires among other aspects that the layer must be separately identifiable and reliably measurable. The forecast transaction must be identified and documented with sufficient specificity so that when the transaction occurs it is clear whether the transaction is or is not the hedged item. A drawback of designation of a layer is the complexity in proving that the designated level of funding is highly probable. For example, when entities specify the interest payment for a particular loan, then there is no need to prove that the cash flows are highly probable since those are contractually specified. When entities do not designate a specific contract then it is necessary to demonstrate that it is highly probable that there will be a need for a certain level of financing of a kind that meets the designated hedged item.

For scenario 4, in addition to the above analysis at the designation date, consideration needs to be given as to whether hedge accounting can be continued if the floating-rate debt is rolled over into a fixed-rate debt, and company D also enters into an interest rate swap on which it pays a floating interest rate and receives a fixed interest rate. Since paragraph 6.3.4 of IFRS 9 permits designating an aggregated exposure of a fixed-rate loan and a pay floating, receive fixed interest rate swap as the hedged item, and paragraph BC6.168 of IFRS 9 clarifies that the aggregate exposure can include a highly probable forecast transaction so long as it will be an eligible transaction once it occurs, company D can continue hedge accounting for the full remaining period, including future roll overs, provided that the aggregated exposure (current floating-rate debt or future fixed-rate debt plus swap) is consistent with the designated hedged item.

In all of the above scenarios, where the hedged item is issued floating-rate debt, ineffectiveness might arise (for example, if the reset dates or interest basis of the swap differ from those of the issued debt).

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.83.7"></a>FAQ 46.83.7 – Can cash flow hedges of future interest flows: continue if fixed-rate debt is issued subsequent to designation?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 paras 6.4.1, 6.6.3  
  Reference to standing text: [46.83](74.md#s298-Para_46.83)  

  

  
**Question**

Is the following cash flow hedge designation of future interest flows permitted under IFRS 9, once fixed-rate debt has been issued? Please note: In all scenarios both the swap and the hedged debt are denominated in company A’s functional currency.

**Illustration**

Company A enters into a five-year interest rate swap (swap 1: pay fixed and receive floating interest rate). The swap is designated as a cash flow hedge of the variability of future interest cash flows on the first CU1 million of debt in issue or to be issued – a highly probable funding – on which interest is not yet fixed over the life of the swap. During the first year, company A has floating-rate debt. After one year, the company issues a two-year fixed-rate debt. Contemporaneously the company enters into a new interest rate swap (swap 2: receive fixed and pay floating interest rate) with a maturity of two years, in order to offset the cash flows of swap 1 during years 2 and 3.

**Scenario 1**

The original hedge designation foresaw that company A might issue fixed-rate debt and enter into swap 2 (receive fixed and pay floating interest rate) during the period hedged by swap 1.

**Solution**

As the original hedge designation foresaw that the company might issue fixed-rate debt and enter into swap 2 during the period hedged by swap 1, the risk management objective of the original hedge has not changed. Therefore, company A can continue with the original hedge designation which should be consistent with the entity’s risk management strategy and objective. For example. in this scenario the entity’s risk management objective (and consequently the original hedge documentation) could have been:

- Hedge the variability of future interest cash flows. This risk management objective would be met by entering into a receive fixed, pay variable swap for those periods when fixed-rate debt is outstanding and including this new swap with the original swap in the hedging instrument, or

- Hedge the variability of future interest cash flows, where the hedged item is either (a) floating-rate debt or (b) the variability in cash flows arising from fixed-rate debt in conjunction with a swap (that is, an aggregated exposure in accordance with IFRS 9 para 6.3.4).

To the extent that the terms of swap(s) designated as the hedging instrument differ from those of the designated hedged item (for example due to different reset dates or interest bases), ineffectiveness might arise.

**Scenario 2**

The original hedge designation did not foresee the issuance of fixed-rate debt during the period hedged by swap 1.

**Solution**

As a result of the issuance of fixed-rate debt for years 2 and 3, the risk management objective of the initial hedge has changed. Therefore, this hedge relationship should be discontinued.

If company A designated as the hedged risk the variability of future interest cash flows on the first CU1 million of debt in issue or to be issued on which interest is not yet fixed over the life of swap 1 then even though company A issued fixed-rate debt, the cash flows designated under the initial hedge will still occur and the hedging reserve should be released to profit or loss over the term of swap 1.

If company A had designated the hedged risk as the variability of future interest cash flows on the first CU1 million of variable rate debt in issue over the life of swap 1, the hedging reserve corresponding to years 2 and 3 should be immediately released to profit or loss as the hedged cash flows would no longer occur.

After discontinuation of the original hedge, company A might want to designate a new hedge. The following new hedge designations would be allowed in scenario 2 under IFRS 9 provided all the qualifying criteria in paragraph 6.4.1 of IFRS 9 are met.

Alternative A

Company A can jointly designate swaps 1 and 2 in a new cash flow hedge of the variability of future interest cash flows on the first CU1 million of debt in issue or to be issued which has not yet fixed interest for years 4 and 5. Some ineffectiveness might arise because the fair value of the combination of swaps 1 and 2 is not nil on the new hedge designation date, and the fair value of the hypothetical derivative should be nil on that date.

Alternative B

Alternatively, company A could:

- Designate swap 2 as a fair value hedge of the interest rate risk of the fixed-rate debt, and

- Designate swap 1 as a cash flow hedge of the variability of future interest cash flows on the first CU1 million of debt in issue for years 2 to 5. Note that in this case during years 2 and 3, swap 1 hedges the aggregated exposure formed by the fixed-rate debt and swap 2, as permitted by paragraph 6.3.4 of IFRS 9. Some ineffectiveness would also arise in this case as the fair value of swap 1 is not nil on the designation date.

In both alternatives a layer is designated as the hedged item. Paragraph 6.6.3 of IFRS 9 requires among other aspects that the layer must be separately identifiable and reliably measurable. The forecast transaction must be identified and documented with sufficient specificity so that when the transaction occurs it is clear whether the transaction is or is not the hedged item. A drawback of designation of a layer is the complexity in proving that the designated level of funding is highly probable.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.83.8"></a>FAQ 46.83.8 – Does the replacement of a hedged item result in discontinuance of hedge accounting?

 

<table style="width:100%; border-collapse:collapse"><tr><td style="border:0.75pt solid #000000; padding-right:5.03pt; padding-left:5.03pt; vertical-align:top"><p style="background-color:transparent"><span style="font-style:italic">Reference to standard</span>: IFRS 9 paras 6.4.1(b), B3.3.6<br><span style="font-style:italic"> </span> <span style="font-style:italic">Reference to standing text</span>: <a href="74.md#s298-Para_46.83">46.83</a><br></p></td></tr><tr><td style="border:0.75pt solid #000000; padding-right:5.03pt; padding-left:5.03pt; vertical-align:top"><p style="background-color:transparent">For hedges impacted by IBOR reform, where the retrospective effectiveness assessment relief set out in Phase 1 Interest Rate Benchmark Reform Amendments to IFRS 9, IAS 39 and IFRS 7 was applied, refer to Appendix A3.29 for further information. Refer to Appendix A3.41 for the Phase 2 amendments issued in August 2020.</p></td></tr></table>

  
**Question**

Does the replacement of a hedged item result in discontinuance of hedge accounting, if the terms of the new hedged item are substantially different (under para B3.3.6 of IFRS 9) from the original instrument?

**Illustration**

Company A is exposed to interest rate risk on interest bearing debt. The company manages its exposure to interest rate risk through the proportion of fixed and variable rate net debt in its total net debt portfolio.

For the current period, Company A’s approved strategy in accordance with its risk management policies is to maintain a certain ratio of fixed:floating rate net debt. To meet this chosen ratio, management has decided to enter into interest rate swaps to swap the floating rate of certain debt to fixed. As a result of this risk management strategy the company has designated a cash flow hedge of variable rate debt. In order to manage liquidity risk, the company has a strategy of exchanging existing variable rate debt instruments some time before their maturity with new variable rate instruments (normally with a longer maturity). As the strategy is to replace existing variable rate debt with new variable rate debt the underlying ‘cash flow’ risk is not changing.

For example: Company A entered into a 10-year debt instrument with the following terms:

Notional: £50 m Interest rate: Benchmark interest rate + 25bp Start date: 30 June 2008 Maturity date: 30 June 2018 Interest settlement dates: Semi-annual – 1 January and 30 June

The interest rate cash flow risk was initially hedged using the following interest rate swap:

Notional: £50 m Receive leg: Benchmark interest rate Pay leg: GBP 5 % Start date: 30 June 2008 Maturity date: 30 June 2018 Interest settlement dates: Semi-annual – 1 January and 30 June

The terms of the interest rate swap match the terms of the hedged item.

At 30 June 2016 when the current debt only has 2 years remaining, management decides to extend the term of the funding arrangements for liquidity purposes. Management decides to replace the current debt with debt of a longer maturity. In this scenario, assume the new debt has a maturity of 8 years and has terms (that is, interest settlement dates, interest basis, currency) similar to that of the current debt. The underlying ‘cash flow risk’ will still be related to the benchmark interest rate (consistent with the cash flow risk of the ‘old debt’).

If the terms of the new hedged item are substantially different from the original instrument (that is, the present value of the new debt is more than 10% different than the present value of the remaining cash flows of the old debt), paragraph B3.3.6 of IFRS 9 requires de-recognition of the old debt. Does this:

- Immediately require discontinuance of the hedge relationship and release of the amount deferred in cash flow hedge reserve; or

- Can the amount in the cash flow hedge reserve remain in equity if the new debt has similar cash flow risk characteristics to the old debt (that is, also has cash flow risk associated with the benchmark interest rate) and merely continues to be a hedge of the cash flow risk associated with the first two years of the new 8-year variable rate debt?

**Answer**

It depends how the hedged risk has been identified in the original hedge documentation.

If the hedged risk has been defined as the cash flow variability from a ‘specific identified debt instrument’ then when the original debt instrument is de-recognised, hedge accounting has to be discontinued and the associated cash flow hedge reserve has to be reclassified to profit or loss.

If, however, the hedged risk has been defined as the “cash flow variability due to changes in the benchmark interest rate” and the hedged item as, for example, “group of forecast interest rate cash flows that are expected to occur with a high degree of probability in specific future periods either from the current debt instrument or any other debt instrument taken out to refinance or otherwise replace the current debt instrument prior to its maturity and that has similar risk characteristics”, the new debt instrument might still fall within the designated hedged item. In this case, replacement or restructuring of the current debt instrument would not require discontinuance of hedge accounting (or the associated reclassification to profit or loss of the cash flow hedge reserve), even when the original debt instrument is de-recognised. To ensure continuance of hedge accounting, the new underlying debt instrument should have similar cash flow variability risk as the previous debt instrument and meet all of the hedge accounting requirements (including the appropriate hedge effectiveness requirements).

A number of further issues require consideration to determine whether continuation of the current hedge relationship is appropriate:

- If the original hedged cash flows extended beyond management’s planning horizon for financing (for example, if management’s planning horizon for financing is 7 years, the variable rate debt was issued for a period of 10 years and management does not plan to replace the debt during the 7-year planning horizon) then the probability of the future cash flows being hedged is supported by the existence of the current loan. As it might be difficult to support the ‘highly probable’ requirement, the designation would have to be based on the specific loan. In this case, it might be difficult to define the hedged risk in a flexible manner that would not only include the ‘specific debt instrument’.

- If, however, the hedged cash flows are within management’s planning horizon for financing (for example, if management’s planning horizon for financing is 7 years, the original variable rate debt was issued for a period of 7 years or less and management plans to replace the debt within the 7-year planning horizon for financing), it might be possible to designate the ‘forecast highly probable variable interest rate payments’ as the hedged item and not necessarily the cash flows from a specific debt instrument. It is however important that the ‘highly probable’ requirement is sufficiently supported. The hedged cash flows need to be sufficiently designated to ensure they are clearly identifiable when they occur and all the hedge qualifying criteria are met.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.83.9"></a>FAQ 46.83.9 – Is hedge accounting available where highly probable forecast sales or purchases are hedged using a load following swap?

 

Reference to standard: IFRIC agenda decision March 2019, IFRS 9 paras 6.3.1 and 6.3.3  
 Reference to standing text: [46.83](74.md#s298-Para_46.83)  Corporate treasury

  

  
 

**Question**

Can a derivative whose notional amount varies with a non-financial underlying (for example, the actual output of a specified wind farm or solar farm) be designated as a cash flow hedge of that variable outcome (for example, as a hedge of the actual output of the wind farm or solar farm)?

**Illustration**

Company A owns and operates a wind farm. The amount of electricity produced by the farm varies with the strength and duration of the wind. Company A enters into a load following swap to pay the variable spot electricity price and receive a fixed price on a notional amount which equals the actual output/sales from the farm (and thus varies with the actual output/sales). Company A would like to designate the swap as a cash flow hedge of the actual output from the farm.

**Answer**

No. In March 2019, the IFRS IC issued an agenda decision ‘Application of the Highly Probable Requirement when a Specific Derivative is Designated as a Hedging Instrument’. The IC noted that, in order to designate a cash flow hedge of a forecast transaction, IFRS 9 requires the forecast hedged item to be ‘highly probable’ of occurring, which includes considering uncertainty over both the timing and amount of the hedged item. In addition, the entity must document the forecast energy sales with sufficient specificity in terms of timing and magnitude so that, when transactions occur, the entity can identify whether the transaction is the hedged transaction. Hence, company A cannot designate all (or a percentage) of the output/sales in a period as the hedged item, because that would lack the required specificity.

However, it might still be possible to use such hedging instruments as a hedge of a particular highly probable outcome of the forecast transaction (such as output/sales of a specified number of MWh). In this case, it is likely that some level of ineffectiveness would result from reflecting the other possible outcomes in the changes in the fair value of the derivative.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.86.1"></a>FAQ 46.86.1 – How can a parent, in its consolidated financial statements, hedge the foreign currency exposure of an intra-group receivable?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para 6.3.6  
 Reference to standing text: [46.86](74.md#s298-Para_46.86)  Corporate treasury

  

  
 **Question**

How can a parent, in its consolidated financial statements, hedge the foreign currency exposure of an intra-group receivable?

**Illustration**

Group X comprises a parent and two subsidiaries, Subsidiary A and Subsidiary B. Subsidiary A, whose functional currency is the euro, has an intra-group receivable from subsidiary B, whose functional currency is the Swiss franc (CHF). The receivable is denominated in Swiss francs, and subsidiary A enters into an EUR/CHF forward contract with an external party to hedge the resulting foreign currency risk.

In its separate financial statements, subsidiary A translates the receivable into euros, using the spot rate at the balance sheet date, and recognises a foreign currency gain or loss in accordance with IAS 21. Subsidiary B, in its separate financial statements, records the payable to subsidiary A in its own functional currency and does not recognise any gain or loss. On consolidation, the gain or loss recognised by subsidiary A is translated into the group’s presentation currency and is recognised in the group’s income statement. There is no offsetting loss or gain arising from subsidiary B.

Subsidiary A uses the EUR/CHF forward contract to hedge the foreign currency exchange risk on the receivable from subsidiary B in its separate financial statements. As the receivable gives rise to an exposure to foreign currency gains or losses that is not fully eliminated on consolidation, the foreign currency exposure on the intra-group receivable can be designated as the hedged item in the consolidated financial statements.

**Answer**

The group can designate the EUR/CHF forward contract in subsidiary A as the hedging instrument. The hedge accounting achieved by subsidiary A is reversed on consolidation and replaced with hedge accounting achieved by the group.

For group purposes, it is not necessary for the subsidiary to take out the forward contract for the foreign exchange exposure on the intra-group receivable to qualify as a hedged item on consolidation. The parent entity could have taken out the same forward contract hedging the EUR/CHF exchange risk instead.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.86.2"></a>FAQ 46.86.2 – Can a group, in its consolidated financial statements, hedge the foreign currency and interest rate exposure of an external borrowing that is on-lent to a subsidiary with a different functional currency?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para 6.2.3, IFRS 9 para 6.3.6  
 Reference to standing text: [46.86](74.md#s298-Para_46.86), [46.99](74.md#s299-Para_46.99)  Corporate treasury

  

  
 **Question**

Can a group, in its consolidated financial statements, hedge the foreign currency and interest rate exposure of an external borrowing that is on-lent to a subsidiary with a different functional currency?

**Background**

Group X has two financing entities, UK Financing Entity and Euro Financing Entity. UK Financing Entity’s functional currency is GBP, and it finances the UK operations of the group. Euro Financing Entity’s functional currency is the euro, and it is the main external financing entity of the group.

Euro Financing Entity issues a EUR fixed-rate bond that it immediately on-lends in fixed EUR to UK Financing Entity on the same terms (that is, in a back-to-back transaction). At the same time, Euro Financing Entity enters into an external receive fixed EUR to pay floating-rate GBP CCIRS, where the fixed EUR leg exactly matches the critical terms of the external bond.

Management proposes to designate the following hedge relationships to obtain hedge accounting in the group consolidated financial statements:

- A floating EUR leg is imputed in the CCIRS, to split the CCIRS into a EUR fixed to EUR floating IRS, and a EUR floating to GBP floating basis CCIRS.

- The imputed EUR fixed to floating IRS is designated in a fair value hedge relationship of the external fixed-rate EUR bond (at the level of Euro Financing Entity).

- The imputed EUR floating to GBP floating basis CCIRS is designated as a cash flow hedge of the spot revaluation of the intra-group EUR fixed-rate loan between Euro Financing Entity and UK Financing Entity at the level of UK Financing Entity (since the internal loan is not part of the net investment in UK Financing Entity, and so it will impact the consolidated income statement).

Is the proposed hedge designation permissible under IFRS 9?

**Answer**

Yes, the proposed designation is permissible. The risks being hedged impact the consolidated income statement of the group, and the entire hedging instrument is designated in a hedge relationship.

Note that the latter cash flow hedge relationship does not result in amounts deferred in OCI, but it is necessary so that the entire CCIRS is designated in a hedging relationship in accordance with paragraph 6.2.4 of IFRS 9.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.88.1"></a>FAQ 46.88.1 – Can hedge accounting be applied to all highly probable forecast inter-company transactions?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para 6.3.6  
 Reference to standing text: [46.88](74.md#s298-Para_46.88) 

  

**Question**

Can hedge accounting be applied to all highly probable forecast inter-company transactions?

  
 **Illustration**

Subsidiary A has a functional currency of euro (EUR); subsidiary B has a functional currency of Japanese Yen (JPY); both subsidiaries are part of a group with presentation currency of USD.

Subsidiary A sells goods to subsidiary B based on USD-denominated prices, and invoicing takes place in USD. USD is used due to the group’s global transfer pricing strategy, whereby it has been agreed internally and with relevant tax authorities that all intra-group transactions are priced and invoiced in USD. Subsidiary B will sell the goods in the Japanese market in JPY.

Subsidiary B enters into a USD/JPY derivative to hedge the USD risk on its purchases; subsidiary A does not enter into any hedge in relation to this transaction.

**Answer**

Hedge accounting can be applied for some, but not all, highly probable forecast inter-company transactions. It can only be applied where the exposure being hedged is fully reflected in consolidated profit and loss.

In the above illustration hedge accounting cannot be applied as only part of the foreign currency risk arising on the highly probable forecast intra-group transaction is reflected in consolidated profit and loss.

Since the overall profit and loss on this intra-group transaction is ultimately affected by the EUR/JPY exposure, rather than the USD/JPY exposure, hedge accounting cannot be applied.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.89.1"></a>FAQ 46.89.1 – How can an entity demonstrate linkage to an external transaction in order to hedge the foreign currency risk of an intra-group forecast transaction?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para 6.3.6  
 Reference to standing text: [46.89](74.md#s298-Para_46.89) 

  

  
 **Question**

In what situations will an entity be able to demonstrate linkage to an external transaction in order to hedge the foreign currency risk of an intra-group forecast transaction?

**Illustration**

One of the conditions for the foreign currency risk of a highly probable forecast intra-group transaction to be designated as a hedged item in the consolidated financial statements is that the foreign currency risk will affect the group’s consolidated P&amp;L, as explained in paragraph 6.3.6 of IFRS 9. This condition is met where the forecast intra-group transaction is related to an external transaction.

**Scenario 1 – Linkage to an external sale of the same item**

Group A (which uses pound sterling as its presentation currency) includes entity B, with euro functional currency, and entity C, with US dollar functional currency, in the consolidation. Entity B manufactures tyres and incurs production costs in euros. It sells most of the tyres to entity C, and those transactions are denominated in US dollars. Entity C markets and sells those tyres to external customers in the US, also in US dollars.

In June 20X6, entity B forecasts that it will sell tyres to entity C in October 20X6 amounting to US$10m. These sales are highly probable, and all the other conditions in IFRS 9 for hedge accounting are met. Entity C expects to sell this inventory to external customers in early 20X7. At the same time in June 20X6, entity B enters into a euro/US$ derivative (buy EUR/sell US$) to hedge the foreign currency risk of the forecast sale of US$10m to entity C in October 20X6.

Group A intends to designate the forward contract as hedging the foreign currency risk of the forecast intra-group sales of US$10m by entity B in a cash flow hedging relationship in the consolidated financial statements. In this situation, the group can achieve hedge accounting, because all of the following conditions are met:

- The intra-group sales are highly probable, and all of the other conditions for using hedge accounting are met.

- The intra-group sales are denominated in a currency (US$) other than entity B’s functional currency (euro).

- The existence of the expected onwards sale of the inventory in US dollars to third parties outside the group results in the hedged exposure affecting the pound sterling consolidated P&amp;L. This is because the intra-group profit on sale recognised in entity B is denominated in euros that is fixed according to the EUR/US$ rate when the sale takes place in October 20X6. This profit is eliminated on consolidation against the carrying value of tyre inventory in entity C and released to consolidated P&amp;L when the onward sale of inventory to third parties takes place in 20X7.

Gains/losses on the EUR/US$ derivative are recognised initially in other comprehensive income and consolidated equity to the extent that the hedge is effective. These amounts are reclassified to consolidated P&amp;L in 20X7 when the external sales occur.

**Scenario 2 – Linkage to an external sale of a different item**

A parent company with a Swedish Krona (SEK) functional currency has incurred costs of SEK10m in developing the manufacturing process necessary to make product A. The technical know-how is transferred to its US subsidiary. Management estimates that 10,000 units of product A will be produced and sold at a market price of USD500 per unit over the three-year period during which product A is marketed.

With the transfer of the manufacturing process, the US subsidiary agrees to pay the parent company a royalty of SEK1,200 per unit sold of product A. According to the initial calculations, the cost of goods sold will be USD200 + USD150 for the royalty payment to the parent company based on the exchange rate at the date of the agreement (SEK8 : USD1). The agreement is for a period of three years and is capped at 10,000 units. At the end of three years or after 10,000 units have been sold, the US subsidiary can use the technology without paying any further royalties.

At the date of the agreement, the US subsidiary enters into foreign currency forward contracts to hedge the foreign currency risk of the forecast intra-group payments of royalties on the highly probable external sales transactions. It is possible to designate the foreign currency risk in the intra-group royalty payment as the hedged item in a cash flow hedge in the group accounts; this is because the royalty payments are a necessary cost of the goods being sold and are therefore directly related to the external sale.

The standard notes the difficulty in demonstrating that there is a related external transaction for intra-group royalty and interest payments, but does not preclude the use of hedge accounting for such transactions where a clear link to an external transaction can be demonstrated.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.91.1"></a>FAQ 46.91.1 – Are forecast inter-company dividends an eligible hedged item in consolidated financial statements?

 

Reference to standard:  IFRS 9 para 6.3.6  
 Reference to standing text: [46.91](74.md#s298-Para_46.91) 

  

  
 **Question**

Are forecast inter-company dividends, which are undeclared but expected to be paid and received in a foreign currency, an eligible hedged item in consolidated financial statements?

**Illustration**

Company Q, whose functional currency is pound sterling, has a subsidiary in the US, whose functional currency is US dollars. On 1 January 20X6, company Q’s management forecasts that it will receive a US$100m dividend from its US subsidiary in six months. The inter-company dividend was declared and approved on 30 April 20X6, at which time both company Q and its subsidiary recognised the dividend as a receivable and payable respectively.

The foreign currency dividend receivable in company Q’s balance sheet was retranslated at the reporting period end, 31 May 20X6, resulting in a foreign currency loss.

Company Q’s management designated the highly probable inter-company dividend as the hedged item in a cash flow hedge from 1 January 200X to 30 June 200X, in order to hedge the exposure to changes in the GBP/USD exchange rate.

**Answer**

No. IFRS 9 does not permit hedge accounting to be applied to hedges of inter-company transactions in consolidated accounts except in certain specified circumstances, such as where the foreign currency risk on intra-group monetary items is not eliminated on consolidation.

Undeclared inter-company dividends are not foreign currency transactions that are eligible hedged items under IFRS 9, because they do not affect the consolidated income statement; they are distributions of earnings.

On the other hand, the foreign currency exposure arising from the dividend receivable in US dollars recognised on 30 April 200X could be designated as a hedged item, because it gives rise to foreign currency gains and losses that do not fully eliminate on consolidation and therefore affect the consolidated income statement. Company Q’s management can therefore only apply hedge accounting from 30 April 200X, when the dividend is declared, until 30 June 200X when the cash is received.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.91.2"></a>FAQ 46.91.2 – Can a forecast transaction in an entity’s own shares be designated as the hedged item?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 paras 6.3.1, 6.3.3, 6.5.3  
 Reference to standing text: [46.91](74.md#s298-Para_46.91)  Corporate Treasury

  

**Question**

Can a forecast transaction in an entity’s own shares be designated as the hedged item?

  
 **Illustration**

Company P has repurchased some of its own shares (treasury shares). It plans to resell the shares in the market in six months’ time to finance a new investment. The entity purchases a cash-settled put option on its own shares, to protect itself against the risk that the proceeds from the future sale of the treasury shares will decrease because of a fall in the share price.

**Answer**

No. Under IFRS 9, a recognised asset or liability or a forecast transaction qualifies as a hedged item only if it exposes the entity to a particular risk that can affect profit or loss (or other comprehensive income (‘OCI’) where the entity hedges an equity investment for which it elects to present fair value changes in OCI).

A purchase, sale, issue or cancellation of an entity’s own equity instruments is recorded in equity and hence does not affect either profit or loss or OCI.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.92.1"></a>FAQ 46.92.1 – Can a parent’s equity investment in a subsidiary, associate or joint venture be designated as a hedged item for foreign currency risk in its separate financial statements?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B6.3.2  
 Reference to standing text: [46.92](74.md#s298-Para_46.92)  Corporate Treasury

  

**Question**

Can a parent’s equity investment in a subsidiary, associate or joint venture be designated as a hedged item for foreign currency risk in its separate financial statements?

  
 **Illustration**

Company S, the parent company of a group, has entered into a foreign currency forward contract to hedge the net investment in one of its foreign subsidiaries. It applies net investment hedge accounting in its consolidated financial statements. Management would also like to designate its equity investment in the subsidiary as the hedged item in company S’s separate financial statements.

**Answer**

Yes. Although net investment hedge accounting can only be applied in the consolidated financial statements, company S can designate its equity investment in the foreign subsidiary as the hedged item in a fair value hedge of the foreign currency risk associated with the shares, provided that all of the conditions for hedge accounting are met. These conditions include the need to designate a clear and identifiable exposure to changes in foreign exchange rates in the shares held, which is most likely to exist if the foreign subsidiary has the same functional currency as the currency being hedged.

Hedging a non-monetary item for foreign currency risk is explicitly acknowledged by IAS 39 Implementation Guidance (IG) E3.4 and such guidance has been carried forward, along with certain other IGs, into the IFRS 9 Implementation Guidance.

Equity investments in associates and joint ventures can similarly be designated as hedged items in fair value hedges in the separate financial statements.

### <a id="s304-EX46.93.1"></a>EX 46.93.1 – Forecast foreign currency transaction undertaken by a foreign subsidiary

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B6.3.2  
 Reference to standing text: [46.93](74.md#s298-Para_46.93) 

  

  
 Entity A’s functional currency is the euro. It has a US subsidiary, subsidiary B, whose functional currency is the US dollar. Subsidiary B has highly probable forecast sales denominated in Japanese yen.

Entity A wishes to hedge subsidiary B’s forecast Japanese yen inflows back into euros (entity A’s functional currency) using external foreign currency forward contracts (JPY/EUR). Entity A’s management intends to designate, in the consolidated financial statements, the forward contracts as hedging instruments in a cash flow hedge of the forecast transactions denominated in Japanese yen.

The JPY/EUR forward contracts taken out by entity A do not qualify for cash flow hedge accounting on consolidation. This is because there is no JPY/EUR cash flow exposure that affects consolidated P&amp;L. The consolidated income statement will be exposed to JPY/USD movements, as subsidiary B will translate its Japanese yen sales into its own functional currency (USD). The exposure to movements in USD/EUR constitutes a translation risk rather than a cash flow exposure, and so it cannot be the subject of a cash flow hedge.

However, it is possible for subsidiary B to use a JPY/USD forward contract to designate a cash flow hedge of its JPY/USD transaction exposure. Alternatively, entity A could use a JPY/USD forward contract to hedge the exposure, since IFRS 9 does not require the operating unit that is exposed to the risk being hedged to be a party to the hedging instrument.

Entity A could also hedge its net investment in subsidiary B using a EUR/USD forward (see [para 46.22](74.md#s296-Para_46.22)). This would, however, not include the forecast transaction.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.93.2"></a>FAQ 46.93.2 – Can a parent hedge forecast future revenues denominated in the functional currency of a subsidiary which is different from the functional currency of the parent

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B6.3.2  
 Reference to standing text: [46.93](74.md#s298-Para_46.93) 

  

  
 **Question**

Can a parent hedge forecast future revenues denominated in the functional currency of a subsidiary which is different from the functional currency of the parent?

**Illustration**

Entity A, based in Germany, whose functional currency is the euro, has a subsidiary in the UK, whose functional currency is GBP. The group’s presentation currency is also the euro. The UK subsidiary sells gas within the UK for GBP to British customers.

At the group level, the group’s treasury department enters into an external GBP/euro forward contract to hedge against movements in GBP versus euro on behalf of the group.

Can the group apply cash flow hedge accounting?

**Answer**

No. The group cannot obtain cash flow hedge accounting for the UK subsidiary sales. The sales of the UK subsidiary are made in its functional currency, so it has no foreign currency exposure. The consolidated group has a foreign currency exposure, but it will not affect the group’s reported net P&amp;L. At the consolidated level, the foreign currency exposure will be deferred as part of cumulative translation adjustment in equity when the UK subsidiary’s financial statements are translated into the group’s presentation currency for consolidation. [IAS 21 para 39(c)]. Group management might, therefore, consider the possibility of net investment hedge accounting under paragraph 6.5.2 of IFRS 9. However, the group will not be able to obtain hedge accounting for hedges of forecast sales – see last sentence of example presented in [EX 46.93.1](76.md#s304-EX46.93.1).

 

<a id="s304-chap_46_navPoint_152"></a>
### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.93.3"></a>FAQ 46.93.3 – Can a parent hedge foreign currency sales of a foreign subsidiary denominated in the group’s presentation currency in the consolidated accounts?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B6.3.2  
 Reference to standing text: [46.93](74.md#s298-Para_46.93) 

  

**Question**

  
 A European parent entity, whose functional currency is the euro, has a Chinese subsidiary whose functional currency is RMB. The presentation currency of the group is the euro. The Chinese subsidiary has highly probable forecast sales in euros that its management has not hedged. The parent’s management enters into EUR/RMB forward contracts and designates these as cash flow hedges of the Chinese subsidiary’s forecast sales. Can the European parent entity hedge the Chinese subsidiary’s foreign currency sales in its consolidated accounts?

**Answer**

The forecast euro sales give rise to a currency risk that will affect the subsidiary’s P&amp;L in its separate financial statements. This is because the RMB amount at which the sales are reported in the subsidiary’s separate financial statements will be affected by the EUR/RMB exchange rate at the time the sales occur.

However, in the consolidated financial statements the sales will be translated into euros using the exchange rates at the dates of the transactions (or an average rate, where this approximates to the rates at the dates of the transactions) in accordance with paragraph 39(b) of IAS 21. Hence, the amount at which the sales are reported in the group’s consolidated financial statements will not be affected by the EUR/RMB exchange rate at the time the sales occur. Even though the exchange rate might not have an impact on the sales expressed in euros, the consolidated P&amp;L is affected by the realised margin in the Chinese subsidiary.

The forecast transaction meets the requirements of paragraph 6.5.2(b) of IFRS 9, because it is a foreign currency transaction exposure for the subsidiary that affects P&amp;L. This is consistent with the functional currency framework in IAS 21, under which a foreign exchange exposure arises whenever a transaction is denominated in a currency other than the functional currency of the entity entering into the transaction. IFRS 9 also permits the hedging instrument to be entered into by the parent, and states that it need not be entered into by the subsidiary that has the exposure. Hence, provided that all the other criteria in paragraph 6.4.1 of IFRS 9 are met, the group can apply cash flow hedge accounting (see [para 46.14](74.md#s296-Para_46.14) onwards) in its consolidated financial statements.

 

<a id="s304-chap_46_navPoint_153"></a>
### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.99.1"></a>FAQ 46.99.1 – Why might a group use internal hedging transactions and how should these be reflected in the consolidated financial statements?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B6.3.2  
 Reference to standing text: [46.99](74.md#s299-Para_46.99) 

  

**Question**

Why might a group use internal hedging transactions and how should these be reflected in the consolidated financial statements?

**Answer**

  
 Entities with sophisticated central treasury functions often use internal hedging transactions to ‘transfer’ interest rate and currency risk to the group treasury. For instance, central treasury might enter into internal derivative contracts (such as forward contracts and swaps) with subsidiaries and various divisions of a consolidated group, with the objective of ‘converting’ all financial assets and liabilities of those operating units to variable-rate instruments in the reporting currency. Central treasury will assess its exposure to various currencies and to interest rate risk and enter into external forward contracts and swaps to manage those risks on a centralised basis, thereby generating economies of scale and pricing efficiency.

Internal derivative contracts used to transfer risk exposures between different entities within a group, or divisions within a single legal entity, cannot be designated as hedging instruments if the derivative contracts are internal to the entity being reported on. It follows that internal derivative contracts cannot be designated as hedging instruments in the consolidated financial statements. Nor can they be designated as hedging instruments in the individual or separate financial statements of a legal entity for hedging transactions between divisions in the entity. IFRS 9 makes it clear that only instruments that involve a party external to the reporting entity (that is, a group or an individual entity that is being reported on) can qualify as designated hedging instruments.

[IFRS 9 para 6.2.3]. [IFRS 9 para BC6.142]. [IFRS 9 para BC6.147].

However, if an internal contract is offset with an external party, the external contract could be regarded as the hedging instrument, and the hedging relationship might qualify for hedge accounting. In such situations, the hedging relationship consists of the external instrument and the item that was the subject of the internal hedge. The internal derivative is often used as a tracking mechanism to relate the external derivative to the hedged item. Indeed, many entities take advantage of this to net risk through a central treasury function and, thereafter, to hedge the net exposure by entering into external contracts with third parties. This avoids the cost of each subsidiary entering into contracts with third parties, some of which might duplicate each other.

Paragraph IG.F.1.4 of IAS 39 (the predecessor standard to IFRS 9) provided a very useful summary of its application to internal hedging transactions; this is reproduced below for information, as it is consistent with our view of internal derivatives:

- “The standard does not preclude an entity from using internal derivative contracts for risk management purposes and it does not preclude internal derivatives from being accumulated at the treasury level or some other central location so that risk can be managed on an entity-wide basis or at some higher level than the separate legal entity or division.

- Internal derivative contracts between two separate entities within a consolidated group can qualify for hedge accounting by those entities in their individual or separate financial statements, even though the internal contracts are not offset by derivative contracts with a party external to the consolidated group.

- Internal derivative contracts between two separate divisions within the same legal entity can qualify for hedge accounting in that legal entity’s individual or separate financial statements only if those contracts are offset by derivative contracts with a party external to the legal entity.

- Internal derivative contracts between separate divisions within the same legal entity and between separate entities within the consolidated group can qualify for hedge accounting in the consolidated financial statements only if the internal contracts are offset by derivative contracts with a party external to the consolidated group.

- If the internal derivative contracts are not offset by derivative contracts with external parties, the use of hedge accounting by group entities and divisions using internal contracts must be reversed on consolidation”.

For segment reporting purposes, entities might present financial information including the effects of hedging as reported to management; for that purpose, entities do not have to meet the IFRS 9 criteria for hedge accounting. Hence, internal derivatives between segments might be accounted for as hedges for the purpose of segment reporting, if this is the information presented to the chief operating decision maker. Disclosure of differences between the measurements of the reportable segments’ profits or losses and the entity’s P&amp;L due to intra-group hedging activity and policies might be required. [IFRS 8 para 27(b)].

Refer to [FAQ 46.99.2](76.md#s304-FAQ_46.99.2) for an example of hedging with internal derivative contracts for risk management purposes.

 

<a id="s304-chap_46_navPoint_154"></a>
### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.99.2"></a>FAQ 46.99.2 – Are internal derivative contracts eligible hedging instruments?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B6.3.2  
 Reference to standing text: [46.99](74.md#s299-Para_46.99) 

  

**Question**

  
 The banking division of entity A enters into an internal interest rate swap with the trading division of the same entity. The purpose is to hedge the interest rate risk exposure of a loan (or group of similar loans) in the loan portfolio. Under the swap, the banking division pays fixed interest payments to the trading division and receives variable interest rate payments in return.

Is the internal interest rate swap an eligible hedging instrument in the separate and consolidated financial statement of entity A?

**Answer**

No. If a hedging instrument is not acquired from an external party, IFRS 9 does not allow hedge accounting treatment for the hedging transaction undertaken by the banking and trading divisions. This is because only derivatives that involve a party external to the entity can be designated as hedging instruments. [IFRS 9 para 6.2.3]. Furthermore, any gains or losses on intra-group or intra-entity transactions are eliminated on consolidation. Therefore, transactions between different divisions within entity A do not qualify for hedge accounting treatment in entity A’s financial statements. Similarly, transactions between different entities within a group do not qualify for hedge accounting treatment in entity A’s consolidated financial statements.

However, if, in addition to the internal swap mentioned above, the trading division enters into an interest rate swap or other contract with an external party that offsets the exposure hedged in the internal swap, hedge accounting is permitted under IFRS 9. For the purposes of IFRS 9, the hedged item is the loan (or group of similar loans) in the banking division, and the hedging instrument is the external interest rate swap or other contract.

The trading division might aggregate several internal swaps, or portions of them that are not offsetting each other, and enter into a single third-party derivative contract that offsets the aggregate exposure. Under IFRS 9, such external hedging transactions might qualify for hedge accounting treatment, provided that the hedged items in the banking division are identified and the other conditions for hedge accounting are met.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.99.3"></a>FAQ 46.99.3 – How should a group present the impact of inter-company derivatives?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B6.3.2  
 Reference to standing text: [46.99](74.md#s299-Para_46.99)  Corporate Treasury

  

**Question**

How should a group present the impact of inter-company derivatives?

  
 **Illustration**

Group E hedges forecast purchases and sales denominated in foreign currencies arising from some of its subsidiaries whose functional currency is the same as that of the parent. Group E hedges on a net basis in its consolidated accounts using external derivatives (foreign currency forward contracts) entered into centrally by group E’s treasury.

Internal derivative agreements are contracted between each subsidiary and group E’s treasury function to provide a hedge on a gross basis at a subsidiary level.

**Answer**

Although, hedge relationships with internal derivatives do not qualify for hedge accounting under IFRS in the group’s financial statements, hedge accounting could be applied in the separate financial statements of the subsidiaries.

Disclosure on segmental reporting under IFRS 8 is through the eyes of management. Therefore, provided the effects of hedge accounting associated with internal derivatives are included in the internal financial information reported to the Chief Operating Decision Maker, group E reflects the effects of the underlying hedges in its segmental reporting disclosures.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.99.4"></a>FAQ 46.99.4 – Can a foreign currency inter-company loan be designated as a hedging instrument in consolidated financial statements?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B6.2.3  
 Reference to standing text: [46.99](74.md#s299-Para_46.99)   

  

  
 **Question**

Can a group designate an inter-company borrowing denominated in USD as a hedging instrument for the group’s net investment in USD functional currency subsidiaries?

**Illustration**

A Swiss group, whose parent is a holding company with functional currency of CHF, has substantial investments in subsidiaries with USD functional currency. The CHF-functional currency parent company also has an inter-company borrowing in USD from one of its US subsidiaries.

Although the inter-company borrowing will be eliminated on consolidation, the foreign currency (FX) gain or loss from the translation of USD into CHF will affect consolidated profit or loss, consistent with the functional currency concept under IAS 21. The parent company wishes to obtain hedge accounting in its consolidated financial statements by designating the inter-company borrowing as a hedging instrument in a hedge of its net investment in USD subsidiaries (and so recognise the FX gain or loss on the inter-company borrowing in other comprehensive income and the hedging reserve).

**Solution**

No, an inter-company borrowing cannot be used as a hedging instrument. Paragraph 6.2.3 of IFRS 9 stipulates that only instruments that involve a party external to the reporting entity can be designated as hedging instruments. Paragraph 6.2.3 of IFRS 9 applies irrespective of whether a hedging instrument, such as an inter-company borrowing, will affect the consolidated profit or loss.

### <a id="s304-FAQ_46.100.1"></a>FAQ 46.100.1 – Can a single external derivative which offsets several internal derivatives qualify as a hedging instrument?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B6.3.2  
 Reference to standing text: [46.100](74.md#s299-Para_46.100) 

  

  
 **Question**

In the consolidated financial statements of a group, can a single external derivative which offsets several internal derivatives qualify as a hedging instrument?

**Illustration**

Entity A has a number of subsidiaries. All treasury activities of the group are undertaken by entity A. Individual subsidiaries intending to hedge their exposure to interest rate risk are required to enter into separate derivative contracts with entity A.

Entity A aggregates the internal derivative contracts and enters into a single external derivative contract that offsets the internal derivative contracts on a net basis. For instance, entity A might enter into three internal receive fixed, pay variable interest rate swaps (total notional amount of C100m) that lay off the exposure to variable interest cash flows on variable-rate liabilities in the three subsidiaries, and one internal receive variable, pay fixed interest rate swap (notional amount of C80m) that lays off the exposure to variable interest cash flows on variable-rate assets in another subsidiary. It then enters into a receive variable, pay fixed interest rate swap (notional amount of C20m) with an external counterparty that exactly offsets the four internal swaps. It is assumed that the hedge accounting criteria are met.

**Answer**

In entity A’s consolidated financial statements, the single offsetting external derivative would not qualify as a hedging instrument in a hedge of an overall net position – that is, it cannot be used to hedge all of the items that the four internal derivatives are hedging, because paragraph 6.6.1 of IFRS 9 only allows a net position to be designated as the hedged item in a cash flow hedge if the hedged risk is foreign currency risk.

However, as explained in [paragraph 46.63.1](74.md#s298-Para_46.63.1), designating a part of the underlying items as the hedged position on a gross basis is permitted (that is, the external derivative can hedge C20m of variable-rate liabilities totalling C100m). Therefore, even though the purpose of entering into the external derivative was to offset internal derivative contracts on a net basis, hedge accounting is permitted if the hedging relationship is defined and documented as a hedge of a part of the underlying cash inflows or cash outflows on a gross basis and this is consistent with the entity’s risk management strategy.

 * External derivative

For the consolidated financial statements, TC’s external forward contract on FC300 could be designated, at the beginning of month 1, as a hedge of the net position in TC, because paragraph 6.6.1(c) of IFRS 9 permits cash flow hedges of net positions for foreign exchange risk if the entity hedges on a net basis for risk management purposes. [IFRS 9 paras 6.6.1]. [IFRS 9 App B para B6.6.1]. The hedged item must, however, be designated as the group of items that make up the net position (in this case, FC200 revenue and FC500 expenses). [IFRS 9 App B para B6.6.4]. IFRS 9 requires that, in the consolidated financial statements at the end of month 1, the accounting effects of the internal derivative transactions should be eliminated. However, the net balances before and after elimination of the accounting entries relating to the internal derivatives are the same, as set out below. Accordingly, there is no need to make any further accounting entries in order for the requirements of IFRS 9 to be met.

Note that the revenue and cost of sales lines will still be reported at the currency rate ruling on the date of the respective transactions. The impact of hedge accounting (the loss on the external derivative) will be presented in P&amp;L on a separate line from those affected by the hedged items at the time when the first of the hedged items within the net position affects P&amp;L.

[IFRS 9 para 6.6.4]. [IFRS 9 App B para B6.6.9].

Alternatively, for the consolidated accounts, the external forward could have been designated as a hedging instrument of the first FC300 of subsidiary B’s highly probable future expenses. That is not a hedge of a net position and so, in that case, the FC300 in costs that were designated in hedge relationships would effectively be reported at the hedged foreign currency rate in P&amp;L. The revenue and cost of sales that were not designated in hedge relationships would continue to be reported at the currency rate ruling on the date of the respective transactions.
