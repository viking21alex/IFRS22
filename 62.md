## Introduction (paras 41.1 - 41.9)

 

<a id="s255-Para_41.1"></a>**41.1**A derivative instrument that falls within the scope of IFRS 9 need not be free-standing. Terms and conditions might be embedded in a financial instrument or non-financial contract (the ‘host’ contract) that behave like a free-standing derivative. These are referred to as embedded derivatives. The combination of the host contract and the embedded derivative is a ‘hybrid instrument’.

<a id="s255-Para_41.2"></a>**41.2**Analysing non-derivative financial instruments and executory contracts for potential embedded derivatives might be challenging. A derivative identified in a host contract needs further evaluation, to determine whether it should be accounted for separately as a stand-alone derivative at fair value. Not all identified embedded derivatives need to be accounted for separately from the host contract.

 

<a id="s255-chap_41_navPoint_2"></a>
### Definition and key characteristics

 

<a id="s255-Para_41.3"></a>**41.3** An embedded derivative is a component of a hybrid instrument that also includes a non-derivative host contract. Some of the cash flows of the hybrid instrument vary in a way similar to a stand-alone derivative. An embedded derivative causes some or all of the cash flows of the contract to be modified according to a specified interest rate, financial instrument price, commodity price, foreign exchange rate, index of prices or rates, credit rating or credit index, or other variable. Variation of the cash flows over the contract’s term is a critical indicator of the presence of one or more embedded derivatives.

[IFRS 9 para 4.3.1].

[FAQ 41.3.1 – What is an example of a hybrid instrument, a host contract and an embedded derivative?](62.md#s262-FAQ_41.3.1)

<a id="s255-Para_41.4"></a>**41.4** A derivative that is attached to a financial instrument but is contractually transferable independently of that instrument, or has a different counterparty from that instrument, is not an embedded derivative, but a separate financial instrument.

[IFRS 9 para 4.3.1].

[FAQ 41.4.1 – What is an example of a separate derivative?](62.md#s262-FAQ_41.4.1)

<a id="s255-Para_41.5"></a>**41.5** A financial asset host that is within the scope of IFRS 9 is not assessed for embedded derivatives, because the solely payments of principal and interest (SPPI) criterion is applied to the entire hybrid contract to determine the appropriate measurement category.

[IFRS 9 para 4.3.2].

[FAQ 41.5.1 – Do embedded derivatives as identified under IAS 39 in financial assets prevent the SPPI criterion from being met under IFRS 9?](62.md#s262-FAQ_41.5.1)

 

<a id="s255-chap_41_navPoint_3"></a>
### Conditions for separation

 

<a id="s255-Para_41.6"></a>**41.6** An embedded derivative should be separated from the host contract and accounted for as a derivative if all of the following three conditions are met:

- the economic characteristics and risks of the embedded derivative are not closely related to the economic characteristics and risks of the host contract;

- a separate instrument with the same terms as the embedded derivative would meet the definition of a derivative (see [chapter 40 para 30](61.md#s251-Para_40.30)); and

- the hybrid instrument is not measured at fair value, with changes in fair value recognised in profit or loss.

[IFRS 9 para 4.3.3].

<a id="s255-Para_41.7"></a>**41.7**The requirements are summarised in the following diagram.

![](assets/150.png)

  
 * If embedded derivative is not separately measurable, the entire instrument is recorded at fair value through profit or loss.

<a id="s255-Para_41.8"></a>**41.8** An entity could avoid separating an embedded derivative from a host contract by electing the fair value option. If a contract contains one or more embedded derivatives and the host is not an asset within the scope of this standard, an entity could designate the entire hybrid contract at FVTPL unless:

- the embedded derivative(s) does not significantly modify the cash flows that otherwise would be required by the contract; or

- it is clear, with little or no analysis, when a similar hybrid instrument is first considered, that separation of the embedded derivative(s) is prohibited; for example, a pre-payment option embedded in a loan that permits the holder to pre-pay the loan for approximately its amortised cost.

Examples of eligible host contracts are financial liability host contracts within the scope of IFRS 9, as well as non-financial host contracts such as executory contracts for the purchase of goods or services that are outside the scope of IFRS 9 (for example, a host contract for the future purchase of renewable energy credits).

[IFRS 9 para 4.3.5].

<a id="s255-Para_41.9"></a>**41.9**The requirement to separate embedded derivatives from host contracts applies to both the issuer and the holder of a hybrid instrument. However, the two parties to the contract might reach different accounting treatments. An interest payment dependent on the FTSE 100 on a debt instrument will lead to the instrument failing the SPPI test and, as such, it is carried at fair value by the holder of such instrument. However, from the issuer’s perspective, the equity-indexed interest is an embedded derivative which is bifurcated, unless the issuer elects to value the whole contract at fair value.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.6].

## Interpretation of ‘closely related’ (paras 41.10 - 41.21)

 

<a id="s256-Para_41.10"></a>**41.10** Once an embedded derivative is identified, it is necessary to consider whether its economic characteristics and risks (that is, the factors that cause the derivative to fluctuate in value) are closely related to the economic characteristics and risks of the host contract. IFRS 9 provides specific examples of embedded derivatives that are, or are not, closely related to the host contract, rather than a definition. The examples focus on whether the underlying economic characteristics and risks of the embedded derivative behave in a manner similar to the host contract’s economic characteristics and risks.

[IFRS 9 para 4.3.3].

<a id="s256-Para_41.11"></a>**41.11** A key determinant in the ‘closely related’ assessment is risk. Risk in a derivative is determined by the underlying (such as interest rate, foreign exchange, prices etc), and by any leverage in the formula for determining settlement. An embedded derivative such as a cap or floor on the interest rate (interest rate risk), that bears a close economic relationship to its host debt contract, would be considered closely related. Conversely, where a derivative that is embedded in a debt instrument embodies an equity instrument’s economic characteristics, the economic characteristics of the derivative (equity price risk) and host contract (interest rate risk) are different. The embedded derivative would then not be considered closely related to the host contract.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.5].

[FAQ 41.11.1 – Should an entity consider both quantitative and qualitative factors to assess the closely related criteria?](62.md#s262-FAQ_41.11.1)

 

<a id="s256-chap_41_navPoint_5"></a>
### Embedded leverage features in host contracts

 

<a id="s256-Para_41.12"></a>**41.12** Embedded leverage features can significantly modify some or all of the cash flows that otherwise would be required by the host contract. IFRS 9 does not define the term ‘leverage’. A hybrid instrument contains embedded leverage features if some or all of its contractually required cash flows, determined by reference to changes in one or more underlying, are modified in a manner that multiply or otherwise exacerbate the effect of those changes. The effect of leverage is only relevant for those embedded derivatives that would otherwise be ‘closely related’ to the non-derivative host contract. Embedded derivatives that are not ‘closely related’ are separated from the host contract in any event, irrespective of whether they are considered to be leveraged.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.8(b)].

<a id="s256-Para_41.13"></a>**41.13**There is no quantification of how much leverage is acceptable, for an embedded derivative to still be considered closely related. Any embedded feature that leverages the exposure of the host contract to more than an insignificant amount would require separation. However, the standard does not provide guidance as to what would constitute an acceptable threshold and, as such, not all leveraged contracts require separation. A threshold frequently used in practice is the notion of at least doubling the holder’s interest rate of return in relation to embedded derivatives in which the underlying is an interest rate or an interest rate index ([see para 41.24](62.md#s257-Para_41.24)).

[IFRS 9 Appendix B para B4.3.8(b)].

[FAQ 41.13.1 – What is an example of a leverage feature that does not require separation?](62.md#s262-FAQ_41.13.1)

 

<a id="s256-chap_41_navPoint_6"></a>
### Identifying the terms of embedded derivatives and host contract

 

<a id="s256-Para_41.14"></a>**41.14** An assessment of whether an embedded derivative is ‘closely related’ to the host contract requires an understanding of the economic characteristics and risks of both the host contract and the derivative. The nature of the embedded derivative and the type of host contract must be determined.

[IFRS 9 para 4.3.3].

<a id="s256-Para_41.15"></a>**41.15** The economic characteristics and risks of the host contract are generally readily apparent, and determining the nature of the contract is most often not difficult in practice. Common host contracts for embedded derivatives are:

- Debt instruments.

- Equity instruments.

- Leases.

- Executory contracts, including purchase and sales contracts.

- Insurance contracts.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.5].

<a id="s256-Para_41.16"></a>**41.16** A thorough evaluation of the terms must be performed to determine whether the contract contains an embedded derivative. Certain terms and phrases might indicate the presence of an embedded derivative, including:

- Pricing based on a formula.

- The right to purchase/sell additional units.

- Exchange/exchangeable into.

- Indexed to/adjusted by/referenced to.

- Premium/strike/limits.

- The right to cancel/extend/repurchase/return.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.5].

<a id="s256-Para_41.17"></a>**41.17**Another method of determining whether a contract has an embedded derivative is to compare the terms of a contract (such as interest rate, maturity date(s), cancellation provisions, etc) with the corresponding terms of a similar, non-complex contract. Any differences between typical market terms and the terms of the contract that is being evaluated can indicate an embedded derivative.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.5].

[FAQ 41.17.1 – What are common examples of host contracts and embedded derivatives?](62.md#s262-FAQ_41.17.1)

<a id="s256-Para_41.18"></a>**41.18**The entity makes its own judgement of the terms of a contract, in the absence of stated terms. However, in exercising such judgement, the entity should not seek to create a component that is not specified in the contract. Nor should it establish the terms of the host debt instrument in a manner that would separate an embedded derivative that is not clearly present in the hybrid instrument. It cannot create a cash flow that does not exist.

[IFRS 9 para IG C1].

[FAQ 41.18.1 – What is an example of determining a cash flow that does not exist?](62.md#s262-FAQ_41.18.1)

 

<a id="s256-chap_41_navPoint_7"></a>
### Assessment at inception or contract modification

 

<a id="s256-Para_41.19"></a>**41.19**An entity should assess whether an embedded derivative is required to be separated from the host contract and accounted for as a derivative when the entity first becomes a party to the contract. An entity would become party to the contract when the debt is issued at inception, bought in the secondary market or on the date of a business combination in which the debt was acquired. This initial assessment is not revised, unless the contractual terms change and the change significantly modifies the expected future cash flows associated with the embedded derivative, the host contract or both, relative to the previously expected cash flows on the contract. A change in market conditions does not give rise to a need for re-assessment.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.11].

<a id="s256-Para_41.20"></a>**41.20** The acquirer in a business combination is required to re-assess all contracts of the acquired entity for embedded derivatives at the acquisition date. The acquirer becomes a party to the contract at the acquisition date. Re-assessment might result in more embedded derivatives being separated from host contracts in the new group’s consolidated financial statements. Some of the criteria for separating embedded derivatives are driven by market conditions existing at the time when the company becomes a party to the contract. The assessment performed at acquisition date uses market conditions that might be different from those at the inception of the contract. These separated embedded derivatives are accounted for at fair value through profit or loss in the consolidated financial statements after the acquisition, unless they are designated in a valid hedge relationship.

[IFRS 3 para 16].

[FAQ 41.20.1 – Should an embedded derivative be separated following a business combination?](62.md#s262-FAQ_41.20.1)

 

<a id="s256-chap_41_navPoint_8"></a>
### Application of ‘closely related’ criterion to different types of host contracts

 

<a id="s256-Para_41.21"></a>**41.21**The application guidance to IFRS 9 provides examples of situations where the embedded derivative is, or is not, closely related to the host contract. This guidance is not an exhaustive list of contract features or embedded derivatives, but it does contain many of the common features found in typical host contracts. Judgement will be required to analyse situations that are not included in these examples. We have included additional relevant examples to assist in making this determination.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.5].

<a id="s257-chap_41_navPoint_9"></a>
## Embedded derivatives in debt host (paras 41.22 - 41.46)

 

<a id="s257-Para_41.22"></a>**41.22**A contract which meets the definition of a financial instrument but is not an equity instrument is a debt instrument. An identified embedded derivative would need to have debt characteristics to be regarded as closely related.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.2].

<a id="s257-Para_41.23"></a>**41.23**The value of a debt instrument is determined by the interest rate that is associated with the contract. The interest rate stipulated in the debt instrument is a function of the following factors:

- Risk-free interest rate.

- Credit risk.

- Expected maturity.

- Liquidity risk.

Embedded derivatives that affect the yield on debt instruments because of any of the above factors would be considered to be closely related, unless they are leveraged ([see para 41.12](62.md#s256-Para_41.12)) or do not change in the same direction as interest rates ([see para 41.24](62.md#s257-Para_41.24)). Embedded derivatives that have economic characteristics and risks such as equity or commodity features that are unrelated to interest rates are not closely related to the debt host.

[FAQ 41.26.3 – Should an inverse SONIA feature in a leveraged inverse floater be separated?](62.md#s262-FAQ_41.26.3)

 

<a id="s257-chap_41_navPoint_10"></a>
### Index-linked interest payments

 

<a id="s257-Para_41.24"></a>**41.24**Floating rate loans are commonly priced off a benchmark interest rate such as SONIA. More complex debt instruments contain embedded interest rate indices that can change the amount of interest that would otherwise be paid or received, such as leveraged inverse floaters or range floaters. An embedded derivative in which the underlying is an interest rate or interest rate index, that can change the amount of interest that would otherwise be paid or received on an interest-bearing host debt contract (or insurance contract), is usually closely related to the host contract. However, if either of the following situations exists, the embedded derivative is not closely related:

- the combined instrument can be settled in such a way that the holder would not recover substantially all of its recognised investment; or

- the embedded derivative could at least double the holder’s initial rate of return on the host contract and could result in a rate of return that is at least twice what the market return would be for a contract with the same terms as the host contract.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.8(a)].

<a id="s257-Para_41.25"></a>**41.25** The condition that “the holder would not recover substantially all of its recognised investment” applies to situations in which the holder can be forced to accept settlement at an amount that causes the holder not to recover substantially all of its recognised investment. The term ‘substantially all’ is not defined in IFRS 9, and so judgement should be exercised after considering all of the relevant facts and circumstances.

[IFRS 9 para IG C10].

[FAQ 41.25.1 – What is an example of an instrument where the holder would not recover ‘substantially all’ of a recognised investment?](62.md#s262-FAQ_41.25.1)

<a id="s257-Para_41.26"></a>**41.26** The second condition has two parts. An embedded derivative should be able to at least double the initial rate of return and, at the same time, it should be able to result in a return which is at least twice that of the market rate at the time of the change. Both of these parts should be met for the embedded derivative not to be considered closely related. So, for example, if the embedded derivative feature results in doubling the initial return under a possible future interest rate scenario (no matter how remote) but, at the same time, that interest rate scenario would not result in a rate of return that is at least twice what the then-current market return would be for a contract that has the same terms as the host, then only one part of the condition would have been met and the embedded derivative would be regarded as closely related to the host.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.8(a)].

### Inflation-linked interest and principal payments

 

<a id="s257-Para_41.27"></a>**41.27**The guidance on inflation adjustment features in lease host contracts is usually applied to assess inflation adjustments in debt contracts, because IFRS 9 is silent. An embedded inflation-related index is closely related to the lease host contract if the index is not leveraged and it relates to inflation in the entity’s own economic environment under that guidance. Similar considerations are applied to debt instruments in practice. See specific discussion of lease contracts at [paragraph 41.67](62.md#s260-Para_41.67) onwards.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.8(f)].

<a id="s257-Para_41.28"></a>**41.28**An embedded inflation adjustment in a debt instrument would not be closely related if it is considered to be leveraged. If the leverage is greater than one, the indexation is not simply compensating for the time value of money, and so it is not closely related.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.8(f)].

<a id="s257-Para_41.29"></a>**41.29**The inflation index should be a recognised measure of general inflation in the relevant economic environment. If the inflation index relates to a different economic environment from that of the debt, or it is not a commonly used measure of general inflation, the underlying inflation index will not be regarded as closely related to the debt host.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.8(f)].

### Equity- and commodity-linked interest and principal payments

 

<a id="s257-Para_41.30"></a>**41.30** Interest and principal payments in a debt instrument that are linked to an equity index, a commodity index or any other non-financial index are not closely related to the debt instrument host contract and must be accounted for separately unless the index is a non-financial variable specific to the entity. The risks inherent in equity-indexed interest or principal payments embedded in a host debt instrument or insurance contract are not closely related to the host instrument, because the risks inherent in the host and the embedded derivative are dissimilar. Similarly, commodity-indexed interest or principal payments embedded in a host debt instrument or insurance contract – by which the amount of interest or principal is indexed to the price of a commodity – are not closely related to the host instrument, because the risks inherent in the host and the embedded derivative are dissimilar.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.5].

<a id="s257-Para_41.31"></a>**41.31** The holder of a debt instrument might have the right to put the debt instrument back to the issuer in exchange for an amount of cash or other financial assets that varies on the basis of the change in an equity or commodity index. These instruments are another example of an equity- or commodity-linked payment. The embedded derivative must be separately accounted for, unless the issuer on initial recognition designates the instrument as a financial liability at fair value through profit or loss.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.6].

<a id="s257-chap_41_navPoint_13"></a>
### Interest rate caps, floors and collars

 

<a id="s257-Para_41.32"></a>**41.32** An embedded floor or cap on the interest rate on a debt contract is closely related to the host contract, provided that, when the instrument is issued:

- the floor is at or below the market rate of interest (out of the money); or

- the cap is at or above the market rate of interest (out of the money); and

- the cap or floor is not leveraged in relation to the host contract.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.8(b)].

<a id="s257-Para_41.33"></a>**41.33** In January 2016 the IFRS Interpretations Committee (IC) discussed the application of this guidance and in particular how to determine the floor or cap rate in the contract and the market rate of interest. The IC clarified that an entity should compare:

- the overall interest rate floor or cap for the hybrid contract; to

- the market rate of interest for a similar contract without the interest rate floor or cap (the host contract).

The IC observed that the overall interest rate floor or cap for the hybrid contract is the benchmark interest rate referenced in the contract plus contractual spreads and if applicable any premiums, discounts or other elements that would be relevant to the calculation of the effective interest rate. When determining the appropriate market rate of interest for the host contract an entity is required to consider all specific terms of the host contract and the relevant spreads (including credit spreads) appropriate for the transaction.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.8(b)]. [IFRIC Update January 2016].

<a id="s257-Para_41.34"></a>**41.34** The term ‘market rate of interest’ is linked to the concept of fair value as defined in IFRS 13, ‘Fair value measurement’, and is described as the rate of interest “for a similar instrument (similar as to currency, term, type of interest rate and other factors) with a similar credit rating”. As the market rate of the host instrument typically includes a receipt of premium for the floor, it is expected the IFRIC guidance will lead to floors being closely related to host contracts unless they are leveraged.

[IFRS 9 App B para B5.1.1].

<a id="s257-Para_41.35"></a>**41.35**The IC also observed that the guidance in paragraph AG33(b) of IAS 39 (equivalent to para B4.3.8(b) of IFRS 9) is applied in a negative interest rate environment in the same way as it would be applied in a positive interest rate environment.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.8(b)]. [IAS 39 para AG33(b)].

<a id="s257-Para_41.36"></a>**41.36**An interest rate cap or floor embedded in a variable-rate debt instrument, that was out of the money when the debt instrument was initially recognised, would not be separated from the host debt, even if interest rates change so that the cap or the floor is in the money. Similar considerations apply to caps ([see para 41.19](62.md#s256-Para_41.19) above).

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.11].

<a id="s257-chap_41_navPoint_14"></a>
### Calls, puts and pre-payment options

 

<a id="s257-Para_41.37"></a>**41.37**Embedded calls, puts and pre-payment options are not closely related to the host debt contract, unless:

- the option’s exercise price is approximately equal, on each exercise date, to the host debt instrument’s amortised cost, or

- the exercise price of the pre-payment option reimburses the lender for an amount up to the approximate present value of lost interest for the host contract’s remaining term. Lost interest is the product of the outstanding principal amount multiplied by the interest differential. The interest differential is the difference between the effective interest rate on the host contract and the effective interest rate that could be obtained by the lender if it invested the principal at the repayment date for the host contract’s remaining term in a similar contract.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.5(e)].

<a id="s257-Para_41.38"></a>**41.38**An interest or principal cash flow stream that has been separated and payable at different dates is an interest-only or principal-only strip. An embedded pre-payment option in an interest-only or principal-only strip is closely related to the host contract, provided that the host contract:

- initially resulted from separating the right to receive contractual cash flows of a financial instrument that did not itself contain an embedded derivative; and

- does not contain any terms that are not present in the original host debt contract.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.8(e)].

 

<a id="s257-chap_41_navPoint_15"></a>
### Term-extending options

 

<a id="s257-Para_41.39"></a>**41.39**A debt instrument might include clauses that allow the issuer to extend the debt’s term beyond its original maturity (option to extend). This feature might be considered either an option to extend the maturity of the instrument or a loan commitment from the holder of the instrument. The accounting for the option to extend will depend on how the entity views the option. If the entity considers the extension an option, it is an embedded derivative in a debt instrument. If the entity considers the extension a loan commitment, it might not be a derivative within IFRS 9’s scope, and it does not need to be separately accounted for. This judgement is an accounting policy choice that should be applied consistently by the entity.

[IFRS 9 App B para 2.3].

<a id="s257-Para_41.40"></a>**41.40** An option or automatic provision to extend the remaining term to maturity of a debt instrument is not closely related to the host debt instrument, unless there is a concurrent adjustment to the approximate current market rate of interest at the time of the extension.

[IFRS 9 para App B B4.3.5(b)].

<a id="s257-Para_41.41"></a>**41.41**Certain loan commitments fall within the scope of IFRS 9 and might need to be considered for separation. Only the following loan commitments are within the scope of IFRS 9:

- Instruments that the entity designates as financial liabilities at fair value through profit or loss.

- Loan commitments that can be settled net in cash or by delivering or issuing another financial instrument.

- Commitments to provide a loan at a below-market interest rate.

Loan commitments that are not within the scope of IFRS 9 do not meet the definition of a derivative, because all derivatives must be financial instruments or other contracts within the scope of IFRS 9. An option to extend the term of a debt instrument that is considered to be a loan commitment could not be an embedded derivative that needs to be assessed for separation (see [para 41.6](62.md#s255-Para_41.6)).

[IFRS 9 para 2.3]. [IFRS 9 App A].

The issuer regards the call option as extending the term to maturity of the debt instrument, provided that the issuer can be required to participate in or facilitate the re-marketing of the debt instrument as a result of the call option being exercised. As such, the issuer needs to determine whether the extension option needs to be bifurcated according to the guidance above on extension options.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.5(b)].

### Credit-sensitive payments and credit derivatives

 

<a id="s257-Para_41.43"></a>**41.43** The creditworthiness of the issuer is a key factor in setting the level of interest rate on the debt instrument, although IFRS 9 does not deal specifically with payments based on the creditworthiness of the issuer. A debt instrument that provides an interest rate reset if there is a change in the issuer’s published credit rating or creditworthiness contains an embedded derivative. However, the embedded derivative is closely related to the debt host, because the risks are similar, and so it would not be separated from the host contract.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.5(f)].

### Equity conversion features

 

<a id="s257-Para_41.45"></a>**41.45**An equity conversion option is found in debt securities that are convertible into the issuer’s equity shares at the investor’s option. The written equity conversion option is an equity instrument for the issuer and is excluded from IFRS 9’s scope, provided that it meets the conditions for equity classification under IAS 32 (see [chapter 43 para 59](67.md#s279-Para_43.59)). However, if a debt instrument is convertible or exchangeable into shares of another entity, the embedded derivative is not closely related and the issuer would have to separate the embedded derivative from the host contract. The holder of such instruments would account for such instruments at fair value, because the instrument fails the SPPI test.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.2].

### Foreign currency features

 

<a id="s257-Para_41.46"></a>**41.46**An entity might issue debt in a currency other than its functional currency. Such a foreign currency loan is accounted for under IAS 21, which requires foreign currency gains and losses on monetary items to be recognised in profit or loss. The foreign currency gains or losses are already recognised, so a foreign currency derivative embedded in such a host debt instrument is considered closely related and is not separated. This also applies to an embedded foreign currency derivative that provides a stream of interest payments that are denominated in a different foreign currency and are embedded in a host debt instrument, such as a dual currency bond. If the debt instrument contains foreign currency features which are not accounted for under IAS 21, it might lead to embedded derivatives which need to be separated.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.8(c)].

## Embedded derivatives in equity host (paras 41.47 - 41.51)

 

<a id="s258-Para_41.47"></a>**41.47** A host contract that encompasses a residual interest and that involves the rights of ownership is usually an equity host. A host contract that has a stated or pre-determined maturity might not represent a residual interest in the entity’s net assets (see [chapter 43 para 35](67.md#s277-Para_43.35)). The value of an equity instrument is a function of the underlying equity price or index. An embedded derivative in an equity host would need to possess equity characteristics related to the same entity in order to be regarded as closely related.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.2].

<a id="s258-Para_41.48"></a>**41.48**Preference shares must first be analysed to establish if they are more akin to an equity instrument or a debt instrument, in order to determine which guidance on embedded derivatives is applicable. Following this analysis, any put or call options embedded in the preference shares are considered for separation as embedded derivatives.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.2].

 

<a id="s258-chap_41_navPoint_20"></a>
### Calls and puts

 

<a id="s258-Para_41.49"></a>**41.49**A call option embedded in an equity instrument, that enables the issuer to re-acquire that equity instrument at a specified price, is an equity instrument, provided that it meets the conditions for that classification under IAS 32, in which case it is excluded from IFRS 9’s scope. The holder of such instruments would account for such instruments at fair value, because the instrument fails the SPPI test.

[IAS 32 para 16].

<a id="s258-Para_41.50"></a>**41.50**A put option that requires the issuer to re-acquire an equity instrument at a specified price is a written option that gives the counterparty the right to sell the issuer’s own equity instrument to the entity for a fixed price. The issuer recognises a financial liability equal to the present value of the redemption amount, being the present value of the option’s exercise price. See further [chapter 43](67.md#s274-chap_43). The holder of such instruments accounts for them at fair value, because the instrument fails the SPPI test.

[IAS 32 para 23].

<a id="s258-Para_41.51"></a>**41.51**In the case of a puttable instrument that can be put back at any time for cash equal to a proportionate share of the entity’s net asset value (such as units of an open-ended mutual fund or some unit-linked investment products), the effect of separating an embedded derivative and accounting for each component is to measure the combined instrument at the redemption amount that is payable at the balance sheet date if the holder exercised its right to put the instrument back to the issuer.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.7].

### Inflation-indexed rentals

 

<a id="s259-Para_41.53"></a>**41.53**An embedded derivative in a host lease contract is closely related to the host contract if the embedded derivative is an inflation-related index, such as an index of lease payments to a consumer price index, provided that:

- the lease is not leveraged; and

- the index relates to inflation in the entity’s own economic environment.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.8(f)].

<a id="s259-Para_41.54"></a>**41.54**An embedded inflation adjustment in the lease contract would not be closely related if it is considered to be leveraged. An inflationary adjustment of greater than one would be considered sufficiently leveraged for the inflation feature to be accounted for separately. The market expects the prices of goods and services to move in line with inflation, all other factors being equal. Therefore, any inflationary adjustments in a lease contract that increase the indexed cash flows by more than the normal rate of inflation are considered to be leveraged.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.8(f)].

<a id="s259-Para_41.55"></a>**41.55**IFRS 9 is silent as to whether this means inflation in the lessee’s or the lessor’s economic environment. It is reasonable to assume that the economic environment that is most relevant to the lease contract is the one in which the leased asset is located. The inflation index of that economic environment is the one that directly affects the lease rentals and, hence, the leased property’s value. In addition, the economic environment could also refer to the currency in which the lease payments are denominated, since an inflation-linked lease liability could be analogised to an inflation-linked debt instrument (refer to [para 41.29](62.md#s257-Para_41.29) and [FAQ 41.29.3](62.md#s262-FAQ_41.29.3) for considerations regarding inflation-linked debt instruments). Therefore, if the lease payments are indexed to a recognised measure of general inflation in the economic environment of the currency in which the lease payments are denominated, the inflation index is regarded as closely related to the host lease liability from the lessee’s perspective.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.8(f)].

<a id="s259-chap_41_navPoint_25"></a>
### Lease payments in foreign currencies

 

<a id="s259-Para_41.58"></a>**41.58**Lease contracts that give rise to financial assets in lessors, and financial liabilities in lessees (most leases under IFRS 16), are financial instruments. Therefore, since a lease asset / liability denominated in a foreign currency is similar in nature to a foreign currency loan, no separation of the embedded foreign currency derivative is required, because the foreign currency lease receivable and payable are monetary items that are accounted for in accordance with IAS 21. The guidance that applies to foreign currency loans is also applicable to foreign currency leases that give rise to financial assets or financial liabilities, and the embedded derivative is not separated (see para 41.46).

<a id="s259-Para_41.59"></a>**41.59**For a lessor, an operating lease is not regarded as a financial instrument. Therefore, an operating lease agreement that provides for payments in a foreign currency might contain embedded foreign currency derivatives that require separation. ## Embedded derivatives in executory contracts and service contracts (paras 41.60 - 41.73)

 

<a id="s260-Para_41.60"></a>**41.60**Contracts to buy or sell a non-financial item that can be settled net in cash or another financial instrument or by exchanging financial instruments, but that are entered into to meet the entity’s expected purchase, sale or usage requirements (‘own use contracts’) are accounted for as executory contracts and not as if they were financial instruments. This might include some commitments and take or pay contracts. Although these contracts can be settled net in cash or another financial instrument or by exchanging financial instruments, they are outside the scope of IFRS 9 under the own use purchase or sale exemption. However, they might contain embedded derivatives that need to be accounted for separately from the host executory contract.

[IFRS 9 para 2.4].

<a id="s260-Para_41.61"></a>**41.61** There is some specific guidance in IFRS 9 for foreign currency features relating to derivatives embedded in such contracts. The general guidance on embedded derivatives is applied to such contracts, and so it is necessary to consider carefully the economic characteristics and risks of such contracts. Both quantitative and qualitative factors should be considered. Relevant guidance for other types of host contract should be considered.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.8(d)].

<a id="s260-Para_41.62"></a>**41.62**Normal purchase and sale contracts outside the scope of IFRS 9 might contain price clauses that modify the contract’s cash flows. It is necessary to establish whether the underlying in a price adjustment feature is related or unrelated to the cost or fair value of the goods or services being sold or purchased in assessing the closely related criterion.

An entity might designate an own use contract at fair value through profit and loss if it would eliminate an accounting mismatch or if the own use contract contains one or more embedded derivatives, where that fulfils the requirement in paragraph 4.3.5 of IFRS 9. When the entire derivative contract is accounted for at fair value through profit or loss, any derivatives embedded in such contracts might no longer be accounted for separately.

[IFRS 9 para 4.3.5].

<a id="s260-chap_41_navPoint_28"></a>
### Volume adjustment features

 

<a id="s260-Para_41.64"></a>**41.64**Many contracts for the supply of goods give the buyer the right to take either a minimum quantity of goods or any amount based on the buyer’s requirements. From the buyer’s perspective such a feature will need to be included in the analysis of whether the contract as a whole is entered into and continued to be held for the purpose of the receipt of a non-financial item in accordance with the entity’s expected purchase, sale or usage requirements (see [chapter 40 para 81](61.md#s252-Para_40.81)).

<a id="s260-Para_41.65"></a>**41.65**From the supplier’s perspective volume flexibility in the contract can be viewed in two ways:

- The whole contract is viewed as one instrument containing a written option. Written options have to be accounted for in accordance with IFRS 9 if they can be ‘net settled’(see [chapter 40 para 85](61.md#s252-Para_40.85)). ‘Net settled’, in this context, means either that the contract allows net settlement in cash or another financial instrument or that the item that is the subject of the contract is readily convertible into cash. The supplier would be prevented from classifying a contract containing a written option as ‘own use’.

- The contract is viewed as two components: an ‘own use’ fixed volume host contract outside IFRS 9’s scope for any contractually fixed volume element; and an embedded written option potentially within IFRS 9’s scope for the volume flexibility element. The latter would be within IFRS 9’s scope if the option can be net settled, as explained under the first view.

[IFRS 9 para 2.7].

 

<a id="s260-chap_41_navPoint_idp1688675576"></a>
#### Inflation-related features

 

<a id="s260-Para_41.66"></a>**41.66**Inflation escalator clauses are included in executory contracts to protect the seller’s margin in real terms. Although IFRS 9 is silent on the treatment of inflationary adjustments in executory contracts, there is no reason, in principle, why the guidance on inflation adjustment features in lease host contracts would not be appropriate for such contracts. An embedded inflation-related index is closely related to the lease host contract if the index is not leveraged and it relates to inflation in the entity’s own economic environment (see [para 41.53](62.md#s259-Para_41.53)).

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.8(f)].

[FAQ 41.66.1 – Should an inflation feature in purchase contract be separated?](62.md#s262-FAQ_41.66.1)

<a id="s260-Para_41.66.1"></a>**41.66.1** The economic environment that is most relevant to an executory contract might be the one in which either of the parties to the contract is located or where the underlying goods or services are produced, procured or sold. In addition, the currency in which the contract is priced will often be relevant when determining the economic environment. Therefore, if the contract payments are indexed to a recognised measure of general inflation in the economic environment of the currency in which the contract is priced, the inflation index might be deemed closely related to the host contract.

<a id="s260-Para_41.66.2"></a>**41.66.2** The nature of the underlying non-financial item subject to the contract should also be considered, however, since the application of an inflation index might not be acceptable if market participants would not use it as an input or assumption in pricing or transacting in the underlying non-financial item, regardless of the currency in which the contract is denominated. [IFRS 13 paras 11, 22 and 69]. For example, it is unlikely that an inflationary adjustment applied in a contract to purchase or sell an actively traded commodity would be considered closely related to the host contract, since prices for such commodities are typically subject to observable market fluctuations and are not driven by inflationary measures.

<a id="s260-Para_41.66.3"></a>**41.66.3** However, where a contract extends beyond the period for which observable prices are available, an inflationary adjustment for the period beyond the observable prices might be acceptable where market participants would reasonably use this in establishing the price beyond the traded forward curve (that is, beyond the observable pricing period). For example, a 20-year contract to purchase a commodity for which there is a five-year active forward market might have prices in the first five years based on the observed forward prices and, after year 5, have prices indexed to the 5th year price plus an actual measure of inflation. Where market participants would use an inflation index to consider escalation of forward prices beyond the forward curve, it might be reasonable to assume that such indexation is closely related.

<a id="s260-Para_41.66.4"></a>**41.66.4** Care should be taken, however, where a hybrid contract includes an inflation-linked feature and is also denominated in a currency that would require the bifurcation of a foreign currency embedded derivative (see paras 41.68–41.71). Generally, multiple embedded derivatives in a single hybrid contract are treated as a single compound embedded derivative, unless those derivatives relate to different risk exposures and are readily separable and independent of each other. [IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.4]. Therefore, where the foreign currency derivative embedded in the hybrid contract is not considered closely related and requires bifurcation, the embedded inflation-linked derivative would also require bifurcation, since the inflationary adjustment impacts the extent of the foreign currency exposure (that is, the notional amount of the foreign currency embedded derivative). The foreign currency and inflation-linked embedded derivatives would therefore be treated as a single compound embedded derivative.

 

<a id="s260-chap_41_navPoint_29"></a>
### Caps, floors and collars

 

<a id="s260-Para_41.67"></a>**41.67** Contracts to buy or sell a non-financial asset might contain provisions that provide for payments to be made at the market price at the time of payment, but set an upper and/or lower limit on the final price to be paid or received. Such caps, floors or collars included in contracts are embedded derivatives. Provisions included in a contract to purchase or sell an asset (for example, a commodity) that establish a cap and a floor on the price to be paid or received for the asset are closely related to the host contract if both the cap and floor were out of the money on inception of the contract and are not leveraged. This guidance can be extended to a collar that would be closely related if it was out of the money at inception ([see para 41.44](62.md#s257-Para_41.44)).

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.8(b)].

### Foreign currency features

 

<a id="s260-Para_41.68"></a>**41.68**An embedded foreign currency derivative is closely related to the host contract, provided that it is not leveraged, does not contain an option feature and requires payments denominated in one of the following currencies:

- The functional currency of any substantial party to that contract.

- The currency in which the price of the related goods or service that is acquired or delivered is routinely denominated in commercial transactions around the world (such as the US dollar for crude oil transactions).

- A currency that is commonly used in contracts to purchase or sell non-financial items in the economic environment in which the transaction takes place (for example, a relatively stable and liquid currency that is commonly used in local business transactions or external trade).

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.8(d)].

<a id="s260-Para_41.69"></a>**41.69** The currency in which the price of the related goods or services is routinely denominated in commercial transactions around the world is a currency used for similar transactions all around the world, and not just in one local area. For example, if cross-border transactions in natural gas in North America are routinely denominated in US dollars, and such transactions are routinely denominated in euros in Europe, neither the US dollar nor the euro is a currency in which natural gas is routinely denominated in commercial transactions around the world. Very few items, if any, are likely to meet this requirement, apart from crude oil and some metals which are routinely denominated in US dollars in international commerce.

[IFRS 9 para IG C9].

[FAQ 41.69.1 – Should a leveraged foreign currency feature in an ‘own use’ contract be separated?](62.md#s262-FAQ_41.69.1)

<a id="s260-Para_41.70"></a>**41.70**A currency is commonly used in local business transactions where monetary amounts are viewed by the general population not in terms of the local currency, but in terms of a relatively stable foreign currency, and prices could be quoted in that foreign currency. ‘Commonly used’ is limited to those countries with small or developing economies that do not have a currency that is freely convertible and able to be used in commercial transactions. Alternatively, it can be viewed to be applicable to any currency which meets the ‘commonly used’ criteria. For example, economic data supports the contention that the US dollar is used in a wide variety of contracts by Canadian companies. It can be argued that the US dollar is ‘commonly used’ in Canada, even though it is not a small developing country. We see the merits of both points of view and, therefore, will accept either interpretation as an accounting policy election to be applied on a consistent basis.

[IFRS 9 para BCZ4.95].

<a id="s260-Para_41.71"></a>**41.71**The currency must be commonly used within the country by the general public in local business transactions, and not just within a particular industry or particular market. Many countries use more than one stable or liquid currency in undertaking local business transactions or external trade. Therefore, where such countries undertake transactions in those stable or liquid currencies rather than in their local currencies, the foreign currency derivative would be viewed as closely related to the host contract and would not be accounted for separately. The conclusion that a currency is commonly used should be supported by an appropriate analysis on a country-by-country basis.

[IFRS 9 para BCZ4.95].

## Accounting for embedded derivatives (paras 41.77 - 41.85)

 

<a id="s261-Para_41.77"></a>**41.77**A financial instrument containing an embedded derivative is recognised at the fair value of the entire instrument. A day 1 gain or loss might arise if the consideration paid or transaction price is not equal to the fair value of the instrument as a whole. A day 1 gain or loss is recognised only where the fair value of the entire instrument is evidenced either by other observable current market transactions in the same instrument (without modification or packaging) or by a valuation technique whose variables include only data from observable markets (see [chapter 42 para 100](63.md#s267-Para_42.100)).

[IFRS 9 App B para B5.1.2A].

<a id="s261-Para_41.78"></a>**41.78**An embedded derivative that is required to be separated from a host contract is measured at fair value on the balance sheet, with changes in fair value being accounted for through profit or loss. This is consistent with the accounting for a free-standing derivative. The host contract’s carrying value at initial recognition is the difference between the fair value of the entire instrument as a whole and the embedded derivative’s fair value. A day 1 gain or loss will not arise as a result of separating the embedded derivative from the host contract.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.3].

<a id="s261-Para_41.79"></a>**41.79**It might not be possible to determine reliably the fair value of an embedded derivative on the basis of its terms and conditions. The fair value of the embedded derivative, in that situation, is the difference between the fair value of the entire instrument and the fair value of the host contract. If the entity cannot determine the fair value of the embedded derivative (either at initial recognition or subsequently), the entire instrument is fair valued and designated as at fair value through profit or loss.

[IFRS 9 para 4.3.7].

<a id="s261-Para_41.80"></a>**41.80**The host contract is accounted for in accordance with IFRS 9, if it is a financial instrument, and in accordance with other appropriate standards if it is not a financial instrument, following separation of an embedded derivative. Alternatively, an entity can elect to measure the entire instrument at fair value if it meets certain criteria ([see para 41.8](62.md#s255-Para_41.8)).

[IFRS 9 para 4.3.4].

 

<a id="s261-chap_41_navPoint_34"></a>
### Separating a non-option-based embedded derivative

 

<a id="s261-Para_41.81"></a>**41.81**A non-option-based embedded derivative, such as a forward or swap, is separated from its host contract on the basis of its stated or implied substantive terms. The result should be a fair value of zero at initial recognition.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.3].

[FAQ 41.81.1 – How should a non-option-based derivative be separated?](62.md#s262-FAQ_41.81.1)  
[FAQ 41.81.2 – How should a non-option-based derivative be separated to produce zero fair value at inception?](62.md#s262-FAQ_41.81.2)

 

<a id="s261-chap_41_navPoint_35"></a>
### Separating an option-based embedded derivative

 

<a id="s261-Para_41.82"></a>**41.82**An embedded option-based derivative (such as an embedded put, call, cap, floor or ‘swaption’) is separated from its host contract on the basis of the option feature’s stated terms. The host instrument’s carrying amount at initial recognition is the residual amount after separating the embedded derivative.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.3].

<a id="s261-Para_41.83"></a>**41.83**The economic behaviour of a hybrid contract with an option-based embedded derivative depends critically on the strike price of the embedded derivative. Therefore, the separated option should be based on the specified strike price in the contract and not so as to obtain a zero fair value or zero intrinsic value at inception:

- If an entity were required to identify the terms of an embedded option-based derivative so as to achieve a fair value of the embedded derivative of zero, the strike price (or strike rate) generally would have to be determined so as to result in the option being infinitely out of the money. This would imply a zero probability of the option feature being exercised. However, since the probability of the option feature in a hybrid instrument being exercised is generally not zero, it would be inconsistent with the likely economic behaviour of the hybrid instrument to assume an initial fair value of zero for the embedded derivative.

- Similarly, if an entity were required to identify the terms of an embedded option-based derivative so as to achieve an intrinsic value of zero for the embedded derivative, the strike price (or strike rate) would have to be assumed to equal the price (or rate) of the underlying variable at the initial recognition of the hybrid instrument. In this case, the option’s fair value would consist only of time value.

The separated embedded derivative could have a zero intrinsic value if the agreed strike price was equal to the price (or rate) of the underlying variable at the initial recognition of the hybrid instrument.

[IFRS 9 para IG C2].

 

<a id="s261-chap_41_navPoint_36"></a>
### Multiple embedded derivatives

 

<a id="s261-Para_41.84"></a>**41.84**A host contract might contain more than one embedded derivative. Each derivative must be identified and assessed to see whether they warrant separate accounting from the host contract. Generally multiple embedded derivatives in a single hybrid contract are treated as a single compound embedded derivative when bifurcated and valued (see [para 41.77](62.md#s261-Para_41.77)). If the embedded derivatives relate to different risk exposures and are readily separable and independent of each other, they are accounted for separately from each other.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.4].

### Embedded derivatives as hedging instruments

<a id="s261-Para_41.85"></a>**41.85** Embedded derivatives that are accounted for separately can be designated as hedging instruments, like any free-standing derivatives, as long as the hedge accounting criteria are met. IFRS 9 does not restrict the circumstances in which a derivative can be designated as a hedging instrument, provided that the hedge accounting criteria are met, subject to some limitations on written options (see [chapter 46 para 33](74.md#s297-Para_46.33)).

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_41"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.5.1"></a>FAQ 41.5.1 – Do embedded derivatives as identified under IAS 39 in financial assets prevent the SPPI criterion from being met under IFRS 9?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para 4.3.2  
 Reference to standing text: [41.5](62.md#s255-Para_41.5)  
 

  

  
**Question**

Do embedded derivatives as identified under IAS 39 in financial assets prevent the SPPI criterion from being met under IFRS 9?

**Answer**

It depends. Many embedded derivatives introduce variability to cash flows, which is not consistent with the notion that the instrument’s contractual cash flows represent SPPI. If an embedded derivative is not considered to be ‘closely related’ under the requirements of IAS 39, this does not automatically mean that the instrument will not qualify for amortised cost treatment in IFRS 9. But the number of circumstances where such instruments will qualify for amortised cost is limited. There are some embedded derivatives that might require separation but might pass the SPPI condition. Conversely, some hybrid contracts, in which the embedded derivative would be regarded as closely related under IAS 39, might fail the SPPI condition and be measured at fair value through profit or loss.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_42"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.11.1"></a>FAQ 41.11.1 – Using both quantitative and qualitative factors to assess the closely related criteria

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.5  
Reference to standing text: [41.11](62.md#s256-Para_41.11)  

  

  
**Question**

Entity A, an electricity provider, operates in a country where electricity is not traded on a market, and it has a number of electricity contracts that have prices linked to coal prices. Management believes that, from a qualitative perspective, the electricity price is linked to the price of coal, because coal is a major input to the electricity generation process. A quantitative analysis reveals that electricity prices are not directly correlated with coal prices. Should the entity consider both the quantitative as qualitative factors in determining whether the coal element should be bifurcated?

**Answer**

Whilst coal is an important input in the production of electricity, the price of electricity is driven by many other factors and, therefore, it is important to assess the extent to which the economic characteristics and risks of coal are, in practice, closely related to those of electricity. A quantitative assessment of correlation might be relevant to this determination and might lead to the conclusion that the coal price is not closely related to the electricity price. The embedded derivative would, therefore, need to be separated.

It is appropriate to take into account quantitative as well as qualitative factors in determining whether the economic characteristics and risks of an embedded derivative are closely related to those of the host contract.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_43"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.13.1"></a>FAQ 41.13.1 – What is an example of leverage features that does not require separation

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(b)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.13](62.md#s256-Para_41.13)  
 

  

  
**Question**

An entity has a contract for delivery of paper, where the price is linked to a pulp index, a transportation index and a relevant inflation index; the indices are multiplied by percentages that reflect the relative weighting of the cost factor in the production of paper. Do the leverage features over the three indexes require those repricing features to be separated from the host contract?

**Answer**

Such percentages would normally add up to 100% of the total cost of paper. To support the closely related assertion, it has to be demonstrated that this was the normal way to price commercial contracts in the market for paper at the time when the contract was entered into. That could be done, for example, by reference to websites of relevant industry associations, commodity or goods exchanges, etc. Therefore, judgement should be exercised after considering all relevant facts and circumstances that are specific to the situation. These features do not require separation if it was the normal way of pricing such contracts when the contracts were entered into.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.17.1"></a>FAQ 41.17.1 – What are common examples of host contracts and embedded derivatives?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.5

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.20.1"></a>FAQ 41.20.1 – Should an embedded derivative be separated following a business combination?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 13 para 16  
 Reference to standing text: [41.20](62.md#s256-Para_41.20)  
 

  

  
**Question**

A UK entity entered into an ‘own use’ long-term electricity purchase contract in the early 1990s. At that time, there was no market for electricity, but prices for gas and electricity were linked and there was high correlation between the two prices. Hence, the price of the electricity contract was linked to gas prices. Following deregulation of the energy market in the UK in the mid-1990s, the prices of gas and electricity are no longer correlated. The UK entity is acquired many years later, when the contract has a remaining maturity of two years. Should the price adjustment feature be separated as an embedded derivative?

**Answer**

In the UK entity’s financial statements

When considered at the date that the contract was entered into, the price adjustment feature would have been regarded as closely related, reflecting market conditions prevailing at that date. Given that there were no amendments to the terms of the contract, in accordance with [paragraph 41.19](62.md#s256-Para_41.19), the initial assessment is not updated, even though, over time, market conditions have changed, and the prices of gas and electricity are no longer correlated at the acquisition date many years later.

In the acquirer’s consolidated financial statements

The acquirer becomes a party to the contract at the acquisition date. At the acquisition date, the price adjustment feature (that is, the electricity price in the contract is linked to gas prices and not to the market price of electricity) would not be regarded as closely related. Therefore, the embedded derivative would have to be separated and accounted for as a derivative in accordance with [paragraph 41.20](62.md#s256-Para_41.20).

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.25.1"></a>FAQ 41.25.1 – What is an example of an instrument where the holder would not recover ‘substantially all’ of a recognised investment?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para IG C10  
 Reference to standing text: [41.25](62.md#s257-Para_41.25) 

  

  
 An example of an instrument where the holder would not recover substantially all of its recognised investment would be a bond callable by the issuer at an amount significantly lower than its issue price. If the issuer decided to exercise the call option (that is, repurchase the bond), the holder would be required to sell it and, therefore, would not recover substantially all of its recognised investment. The first condition that “the holder would not recover substantially all of its recognised investment” is not satisfied if the terms of the combined instrument permit, but do not require, the investor to settle the combined instrument in a manner that causes it not to recover substantially all of its recognised investment and the issuer has no such right (for example, a puttable debt instrument). An embedded interest rate derivative with such terms is regarded as closely related to the interest-bearing host contract.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.26.1"></a>FAQ 41.26.1 – Should a plain vanilla SONIA feature in floating-rate debt be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(a)  
Reference to standing text: [41.26](62.md#s257-Para_41.26)   

  

  
**Question**

Entity A takes out a floating-rate loan with a bank. The contractually determined interest rate on the debt is six-month SONIA plus two percentage points (assuming credit spread is two percentage points). Should the SONIA interest reset be separated?

**Answer**

A plain vanilla floating-rate instrument with a normal credit spread, whose risk-free interest rate component (for example, SONIA) is periodically reset to market interest rate, cannot contain an embedded derivative that is to be separated.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_49"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.26.2"></a>FAQ 41.26.2 – Should a complex SONIA feature in floating-rate debt be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(a)  
Reference to standing text: [41.26](62.md#s257-Para_41.26)  

  

  
**Question**

Entity A issues a floating-rate debt instrument with a face value of C50 million. The contractually determined interest rate on the debt is six-month SONIA. However, there is a provision that, if six-month SONIA increases by 200 basis points, the investor will pay C2 million to entity A. Should this provision be separated?

**Answer**

The embedded derivative consists of a contingent payment provision that depends on SONIA increasing by 200 basis points. The embedded derivative is not separated, because the investor will receive C50 million, and the C2 million payment is not considered to reduce its initial investment substantially. So the first condition in [paragraph 41.24](62.md#s257-Para_41.24) is not met. Furthermore, there is no provision in the contract that causes the investor’s yield to increase to at least twice the initial rate. Therefore, the second condition is not met. The embedded derivative is closely related to the debt host and no separation is necessary. The fact that SONIA might increase by 200 basis points in a year is simply a condition that triggers the payment, and it is not relevant to determining whether the embedded derivative is closely related.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_50"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.26.3"></a>FAQ 41.26.3 – Should an inverse SONIA feature in a leveraged inverse floater be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(a)  
Reference to standing text: [41.26](62.md#s257-Para_41.26), [41.23](62.md#s257-Para_41.23)   

  

  
**Question**

Entity A takes out a loan with a bank. The contractually determined interest rate on the debt is calculated as 14.5% − 2.5 × three-month SONIA (sometimes referred to as a leveraged inverse floater).

Three-month SONIA at inception = 4%, giving an initial yield of 4.5%. Should this feature be separated?

**Answer**

The embedded derivative has a highly leveraged underlying, so either a negative yield or a significantly higher than market yield is possible. This is because, if, under a future interest rate scenario, three-month SONIA increases to 8%, the yield would be negative (that is, −9.5% (14.5% − 24%)) and the investor would not recover substantially all of its recognised investment. Since the first condition in [paragraph 41.24](62.md#s257-Para_41.24) is satisfied, the embedded derivative is not closely related to the host contract and will be accounted for separately. In practice, sometimes the yield will not be allowed to go negative. To prevent this from happening, a floor is often imposed on the coupon rate.

Note that there is no need to evaluate the second condition in [paragraph 41.24](62.md#s257-Para_41.24). But, if it were evaluated, the result would be the same (that is, the embedded derivative would not be considered closely related to the host debt contract). This is because, for example, if, under a future interest rate scenario, three-month SONIA falls to 2%, the yield would be 9.5% (14.5% − 5%) which is more than twice the initial yield of 4.5%. In addition, the yield of 9.5% is also likely to be twice the then market yield for a contract with the same terms as the host.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.26.4"></a>FAQ 41.26.4 – Should an interest rate option in fixed-rate debt be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(a)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.26](62.md#s257-Para_41.26)  
 

  

  
**Question**

Entity A issues an 8% fixed-rate debt instrument that matures at the end of year 5. The holder has an option to convert the loan into a variable rate debt at SONIA + 3% after two years. SONIA at inception is 5%. Does the option need to be separated?

**Answer**

The embedded derivative in the debt host is an option on a forward starting interest rate swap that changes the fixed-rate interest payments to floating-rate after two years. In order to determine whether the option on the forward starting swap should be accounted for as a separate derivative, it is necessary to apply the second condition in [paragraph 41.24](62.md#s257-Para_41.24).

If, under a future interest rate scenario (however remote), SONIA increases to 14%, the increase would more than double the initial return on the investment from 8% to 17% (14% + 3%). However, that interest rate scenario of 14% does not result in a market rate of interest that is twice the current market rate for a contract with the same terms as the host contract, which is only 17% (assuming no change in credit rating). Because only one part of the second condition in [paragraph 41.24](62.md#s257-Para_41.24) is satisfied, the embedded option on the forward starting interest rate swap is closely related to the debt host and no separation is permitted.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.29.3"></a>FAQ 41.29.3 – Should the inflation feature in an inflation-linked bond be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(f)  
Reference to standing text: [41.29](62.md#s257-Para_41.29)  

  

  
**Question**

A UK entity issues an inflation-linked sterling bond that pays a fixed interest of 5% per annum. The return of the principal amount is linked to the UK retail price index (RPI), which is a recognised headline measure of inflation in the economic environment of the currency in which the bond is denominated. Should the inflation feature be separated?

**Answer**

The coupon on the bond is 5%, and the underlying principal of the bond is £100, so the bond pays £5 interest per annum. If the inflation index increases to 10%, the principal of the bond would increase to £110 and the interest payment would increase to £5.50 (5% × C110).

The principal payment is not leveraged and it is indexed to the headline measure of inflation in the economic environment of the currency in which the bond is denominated, and so the inflation index is regarded as closely related to the host debt instrument.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.31.1"></a>FAQ 41.31.1 – Should a cash-settled put option and a conversion option in a convertible bond be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.6  
 Reference to standing text: [41.31](62.md#s257-Para_41.31)  
 

  

  
**Question**

Entity A issues convertible bonds to entity H. The term of the bonds is three years and the par value is C2 million. Each bond pays fixed interest annually at 6% a year and it is convertible by entity H, at any time until maturity, into a fixed number of entity A’s ordinary shares. Entity H also has the option to put the convertible bond back to entity A for cash at par at any time.

Entity A has determined that the market rate for a loan with comparable credit status and providing substantially the same cash flows on the same terms, but without the conversion option, is 7%. Should the embedded features be accounted for separately?

**Answer**

Entity A’s management should separate the convertible bond’s equity and liability elements. [IAS 32 para 28]. The conversion option is an equity instrument of entity A, provided that it can only be settled by physical delivery of a fixed number of shares for a fixed amount of cash or other financial assets. It is not accounted for as a derivative. [IFRS 9 para 2.1(d)].

The fair value of a liability with a demand feature is not less than the amount payable on demand, discounted from the first date that the amount can be required to be paid. Hence, entity A would record the liability at its nominal value, with any residual consideration received being attributed to the equity component. [IFRS 13 para 47].

The option to put the convertible bond back to entity A for cash at par is an embedded derivative. This option is closely related to the host debt instrument, because the exercise price (par) is approximately equal to the bond’s amortised cost before separating the equity element under IAS 32.

[IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.5(e)].

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.31.2"></a>FAQ 41.31.2 – Should a call option linked to an equity index in a debt instrument be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.6  
 Reference to standing text: [41.31](62.md#s257-Para_41.31)  
 

  

  
**Question**

An entity issues a debt instrument at par with a term of five years. The debt is callable by the issuer three years after issue. If called, the holder will receive a sum that is the greater of the par value of the debt and the par value as adjusted by the percentage increase in the FTSE 100 index. Is the call feature closely related?

**Answer**

The embedded call option, exercisable three years after the issue of the debt instrument, is not closely related to the debt host, because the pay-off is indexed to an equity index. The equity-linked feature provides an upside to the investor, linked to an index representing different risks from those of the ordinary debt instrument. Therefore, the option should be separated from the host debt instrument and accounted for at fair value through profit and loss.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.31.3"></a>FAQ 41.31.3 – Should linkage to an unrelated entity’s share price in a debt instrument be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.6  
 Reference to standing text: [41.31](62.md#s257-Para_41.31)  
 

  

  
**Question**

An entity issues a debt instrument at par with a term of five years. The debt is redeemable at par at any time. The loan agreement provides that, during the term of the loan, the entity will either receive or pay an amount based on the changes in the share price of an unrelated listed company, A plc; the reference point is the market price of A plc at the date of issue of the debt instrument. Is the equity feature closely related?

**Answer**

The interest payments are based on changes in the price of an equity instrument (equity risk), so they are not closely related to the host debt instrument (characterised mainly by interest rate and credit risk). Therefore, the embedded equity-linked amounts should be separated from the host debt instrument and accounted for at fair value through profit and loss.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.31.4"></a>FAQ 41.31.4 – How should an equity-linked embedded call option be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.6  
 Reference to standing text: [41.31](62.md#s257-Para_41.31)  
 

  

  
**Question**

Entity A purchases a one-year debt instrument, issued by bank B for its principal amount of C1,000 on the issue date. Bank B will make no interest payments during the life of the instrument. At maturity, entity A will receive the principal amount plus any increase in the S&amp;P 500 index since the issue date. The fair value of an at-the-money European option maturing in one year is C48 at issuance date.

How should the embedded call option be separated by bank B?

**Answer**

Bank B should recognise the hybrid instrument as a combination of a call option and a zero-coupon debt instrument, having first determined the option contract’s fair value and strike price. [IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.3].

Since bank B only pays increases in the value of the index since the issue date, the embedded option is at the money at the date of issue, and its fair value consists only of its time value and no intrinsic value. So its fair value at the issue date is C48, and the balance of the consideration given (C1,000 − C48 = C952) is attributed to the carrying amount of the zero-coupon debt instrument.

The zero-coupon host contract should be recognised at amortised cost, with interest accreted at the original effective interest rate of 5% to reach C1,000 at maturity. The option should be separated from the host debt instrument and accounted for at fair value through profit and loss.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.31.5"></a>FAQ 41.31.5 – Should contingent payments based on the gold price in a commodity-linked bond be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.6  
 Reference to standing text: [41.31](62.md#s257-Para_41.31)  
 

  

  
**Question**

A gold mining company issues a debt instrument with a face value of C10 million and contingent interest payments in addition to a guaranteed minimum interest payment of 4% per annum. The contingent payments are linked to the price of gold, such that an additional interest payment of 0.5% would be paid for every US$100 increase in the gold price above US$1,000. The price of gold at issue date was US$1,100 per ounce. The market rate for a fixed-interest loan without such additional features is 5%. Should the gold features be separated?

**Answer**

A commodity-linked bond, such as the one described, enables the issuer to reduce its financing cost by offering contingent payments to investors, whilst the investor is assured of a guaranteed minimum but is able to participate in any increase in the price of gold.

In this situation, the issuer would be viewed as having (a) issued a host debt instrument at a market rate of interest of 5%, and (b) sold a call option contract under which the issuer receives a premium of 1% fixed interest and pays a variable amount that varies with the movement in the gold price above US$1,000. The option contract is not closely related to the debt instrument, because the factors that cause the option to change in value (commodity price risk) are not the same as the factors that cause the debt host to change in value (interest rate risk).

Therefore, the embedded option contract should be separated from the host debt instrument by the issuer and accounted for at fair value through profit and loss.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.31.6"></a>FAQ 41.31.6 – Should an equity kicker in a subordinated loan be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.6  
 Reference to standing text: [41.31](62.md#s257-Para_41.31)  
 

  

  
**Question**

A venture capital entity provides a subordinated loan that, in addition to interest and repayment of principal, contains terms that entitle it to receive shares of the borrower, free of charge or at a very low price (an ‘equity kicker’), if the borrower undergoes an initial public offering (IPO). As a result of this feature, the interest on the subordinated loan is lower than it would otherwise be. Should the borrower separate this feature?

**Answer**

The ‘equity kicker’ meets the definition of a derivative, even though the right to receive shares is contingent on the future listing of the borrower. Paragraph BA.1 of IFRS 9 states that a derivative could require a payment as a result of some future event that is unrelated to a notional amount. An ‘equity kicker’ feature is similar to such a derivative, except that it does not give a right to a fixed payment, but an option right, if the future event occurs. Because the economic characteristics and risks of an equity return are not closely related to the economic characteristics and risks of a host debt instrument, the borrower would account separately for the embedded derivative.

[IFRS 9 para IG C4].

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.36.1"></a>FAQ 41.36.1 – Should an interest rate collar in debt be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.11  
 Reference to standing text: [41.36](62.md#s257-Para_41.36)  
 

  

  
**Question**

Entity A has borrowed cash from a bank. The debt is interest-bearing at a variable rate, but within a collar. The variable rate at the date of inception of the loan was 7%, the floor was 5% and the cap was 9%. Should the collar be separated?

**Answer**

In this situation, the embedded derivative is a collar that modifies the cash flows of variable-rate debt if the variable rate moves outside the range of the collar. IFRS 9 does not specifically deal with collars in this context, although the guidance relating to caps and floors can be extended to collars (being a combination of a cap and a floor). At the time when the variable rate debt instrument is issued, the interest rate is 7% for a similar contract without the interest rate floor or cap (the host contract). As such, the floor is below the market rate of interest (out of the money), and the cap is above the market rate of interest (out of the money). Also, the collar is not leveraged. Therefore, based on the guidance in [paragraphs 41.32](62.md#s257-Para_41.32) and [41.33](62.md#s257-Para_41.33), the embedded collar is regarded as closely related, and separate accounting for the embedded derivative is prohibited.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.37.1"></a>FAQ 41.37.1 – Should a call put in a debt instruments be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.5(e)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.37](62.md#s257-Para_41.37)  
 

  

  
**Question**

An entity issues the following debt instruments:

- five-year zero-coupon debt for proceeds of C7 million with a face value of C10 million; issue costs are insignificant. The debt is callable by the issuer at its amortised cost, calculated on the basis of the effective interest rate method in the event of a change in tax legislation adversely affecting the tax deductions available to the issuer.

- five-year zero-coupon debt for proceeds of C7 million with a face value of C10 million; issue costs are insignificant. The debt is puttable at its face value in the event of a change of control of the issuer.

Both instruments will be recorded initially at a fair value of C7 million, which is the consideration received. The debt will accrete to its final value of C10 million at maturity in year 5. Therefore, between inception and maturity the debt’s amortised cost will not be the same as its face value, except in the period close to maturity. Should the pre-payment options be separated?

**Answer**

For the first instrument, if the debt is called by the issuer at its amortised cost, the call option does not accelerate the repayment of principal, because the option’s exercise price is the same as the debt’s amortised cost (the exercise price is not fixed but variable), even though the debt was initially issued at a substantial discount. Therefore, the call option is closely related to the debt host and would not be accounted for separately. [IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.5(e)].

For the second instrument, if the debt is put back by the holder at its face value before maturity, the put option’s exercise price of C10 million (fixed at the outset) would not be the same as the debt’s amortised cost at exercise date. Therefore, the put option is not closely related to the debt host. [IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.5(e)]. This means that the put option must be separately accounted for. This will result in the initial fair value of C7 million being disaggregated into a written put option liability and a host debt contract. The initial carrying amount of the host debt contract will be less than C7 million and its effective interest rate will be higher than in the first example.

Both debt instruments contain terms that allow the debts to be called or put back on the occurrence of a contingent event (adverse change in tax legislation, or change in control). These terms (relating to factors other than interest rate risk and credit risk of the issuer) are not closely related to the debt hosts. However, determining whether the call or the put option is closely related to the host contract is based solely on the difference between the option’s exercise price and the debt’s amortised cost.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_63"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.37.2"></a>FAQ 41.37.2 – Should a pre-payment option in a debt instruments be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.5(e)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.37](62.md#s257-Para_41.37)  
 

  

  
**Question**

Entity A takes out a fixed rate loan with a bank for C1 million. The loan is repayable in quarterly instalments. The debt contains a pre-payment option that can be exercised by entity A on the first day of each quarter. The exercise price is the remaining capital amount outstanding on the debt plus a penalty of C100,000.

At inception, entity A would record the financial liability at its fair value of C1 million. Because entity A makes repayments of capital to the bank, the amortised cost of the debt will change. Should the pre-payment option be separated?

**Answer**

The exercise price of the pre-payment option at inception is C1 million plus the penalty of C100,000. Whether the entity will exercise its option to pre-pay the loan early might depend on a number of reasons, but the level of interest rates is a critical variable. If there is a significant decline in interest rates, any potential gain from early pre-payment might well be more than the cost of the pre-payment (the penalty payable).

Given that the penalty payable is fixed, the option’s exercise price (outstanding principal plus penalty) will always exceed the debt’s amortised cost at each option exercise date, because the loan is paid off in instalments. Hence, the pre-payment option is not closely related to the debt host and should be separately accounted for. The fair value of the option would need to be calculated, and this will be a positive value to the entity, because the value of the pre-payable bond equals the value of the straight bond minus the value of the pre-payment feature. If interest rates decline, the option’s value will increase, making it more attractive to the entity to repay the debt early.

Sometimes, the pre-payment option’s exercise price is a ‘market adjusted value’. A market adjusted value is calculated by discounting the contractual guaranteed amount payable at the end of the specified term to present value, using the current market rate that would be offered on a new loan having a maturity period equal to the remaining maturity period of the current loan. As a result, the adjustment necessary to arrive at the market adjusted value might be positive or negative, depending on market interest rates at each option exercise date. In that situation, the pre-payment option enables the holder simply to cash out of the instrument at fair value at the date of pre-payment. Since the holder receives only the market adjusted value, which is equal to the fair value of the loan at the date of pre-payment, the pre-payment option has a fair value of zero at all times. In fact, on a stand-alone basis, a pre-payment option with a strike price equal to market value would not meet the definition of a derivative, so it cannot be an embedded derivative.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_64"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.37.3"></a>FAQ 41.37.3 – Should embedded puts and calls be taken into account when determining the expected life of convertible debt?

 

Reference to standard: IAS 32 paras 31, IE37, IE38  
 Reference to standing text: [41.37](62.md#s257-Para_41.37), [43.58](67.md#s279-Para_43.58) 

  

  
 **Question**

A company issues a convertible debt instrument that contains both an equity conversion option and embedded written puts and purchased calls. The debt component is initially measured at the fair value of a vanilla debt, with the same contractual maturity plus the value of the written puts less value of the purchased calls, in accordance with paragraph 31 of IAS 32 and as illustrated in paragraphs IE37 and IE38 (example 10) of IAS 32. If the puts and calls are not required to be separated from the debt host, should they be taken into account when determining the expected life of the debt component and hence it's effective interest rate?

**Answer**

Yes. The debt component is measured at amortised cost using the effective interest rate method as defined in Appendix A of IFRS 9. The puts and calls are considered in determining the expected future life of the instrument and, therefore, the expected interest rate.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_65"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.40.1"></a>FAQ 41.40.1 – Should a term-extending option with a reset to market rates be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.5(b)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.40](62.md#s257-Para_41.40)  
 

  

  
**Question**

An entity issues a 6% fixed rate debt that has a fixed term of three years. It can extend the debt, before its maturity, for an additional two-year period, but the rate for the period of extension is the market rate at the time of the extension. Should this extension feature be separated?

**Answer**

The option to extend the term causes the interest rate to reset to current market rates, so the option is regarded as closely related to the host debt. Common sense would also suggest that such an option has no real value to the entity, other than providing liquidity. This is because, if the market interest rate at the time of extension is 8%, the entity cannot extend the term without paying an additional 2% interest anyway. On the other hand, if the market interest rate drops to 4%, the extension is equivalent to taking out a new loan at 4%.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_66"></a>
### <a id="s262-EX41.40.2"></a>EX 41.40.2 – Comparison of put and extension options

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.5(b)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.40](62.md#s257-Para_41.40)  
   

  

**Example**

A bond that has a put option is economically no different from one that has a term-extending option, yet IFRS 9 prescribes a different treatment for the derivatives embedded in them. Consider the following example:

An entity purchases two bonds, A and B, with the following terms:

- Bond A has a stated maturity of 10 years, but the entity can put it back to the issuer at par after three years.

- Bond B has a stated maturity of three years but, after three years, the entity can extend the maturity to 10 years (seven more years) at the same initial rate (that is, the interest rate is not reset to the interest rate at the date of extension).

Both bonds are issued by the same issuer at par and have a coupon rate of 6%.

Assume also that the following two scenarios exist at the end of year 3:

Scenario 1: for the issuer, the interest rate for seven-year debt is at 8%

- The holder will put bond A back to the issuer and reinvest the par amount of the bond at 8%.

- The holder will not extend the maturity of bond B and, instead, will reinvest the principal at 8%.

Scenario 2: for the issuer, the interest rate for seven-year debt is at 4%

- The holder will not put bond A back to the issuer and, instead, will continue to receive 6% for the next seven years.

- The holder will extend the term of bond B and continue to receive 6% for the next seven years.

**Analysis**

As can be seen from the above, the entity is in the same position with respect to either bond A or bond B. However, IFRS 9 prescribes a different treatment for the put and the term-extending options embedded in bond A and bond B respectively.

As discussed in [paragraph 41.37](62.md#s257-Para_41.37), the embedded put option derivative in bond A is regarded as closely related to the bond, since the bond’s amortised cost based on the effective interest rate at the end of three years is the same as par, which is also the put option’s exercise price. This is because the expected cash flows would take into account the possibility of the debt being pre-paid early for the purposes of calculating the effective interest rate. Therefore, the embedded put option would not be recognised separately and fair valued.

On the other hand, the embedded term-extending option is not regarded as closely related to the host debt because, under bond B’s terms, the interest rates are not reset when the option is exercised. Therefore, the option would be separated and fair valued. Alternatively, if the embedded term-extending option was considered to be a loan commitment outside the scope of IFRS 9, the option would not be separated, because it would not meet the definition of a derivative.

The example illustrates that two instruments that are economically similar, but different in form by virtue of the way in which the terms of the embedded options are expressed, can lead to different accounting treatments for the embedded options.

To be able to rationalise the apparent conflict in accounting treatment between these two economically identical situations, it is necessary to consider the issuer’s expectation of the instrument’s life for the purposes of the effective interest rate calculation. So the issuer will not necessarily follow the legal form of the terms, but the economics, in determining whether the instrument has a put option or a term-extending option, and thus the appropriate accounting treatment.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_67"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.41.1"></a>FAQ 41.41.1 – If an issuer treats a term-extending option as loan commitment, how should it account for the change in interest rate when it extends the loan?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B2.3  
 Reference to standing text: [41.41](62.md#s257-Para_41.41)  
 

  

  
**Question**

If an issuer has chosen to treat the term-extending option as a loan commitment, and subsequently chooses to extend the loan, how should it account for the change in interest rate?

**Answer**

One approach would be to adjust the previous carrying value of the liability and to recognise the difference immediately in profit or loss under paragraph B5.4.6 of IFRS 9. That is, the issuer should recalculate the present value of the revised cash flows at the original effective interest rate and recognise the resulting change in carrying amount as a gain or loss in profit or loss.

Another approach would be to treat the extension as a new loan drawn down under the loan commitment. The approach adopted (that is, whether to treat the change in interest rates as a change to the carrying value of the instrument or as a new loan) is an accounting policy choice that should be applied consistently.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_68"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.41.2"></a>FAQ 41.41.2 – Should a term-extending option without a reset to market rates be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B2.3  
 Reference to standing text: [41.41](62.md#s257-Para_41.41)  
 

  

  
**Question**

An entity issues 6% fixed-rate debt that has a fixed term of three years. The entity can extend the debt, before its maturity, for an additional two-year period at the same 6% interest. Should this extension feature be separated?

**Answer**

Because the entity can extend the debt’s term at the same interest rate and there is no reset to current market rates, the term-extending option is not considered closely related to the debt host.

Such a term-extending option could be valuable to an entity, because it allows the issuer to refinance debt at the same interest rate when market rates are rising. Conversely, if market rates are falling, the entity would not exercise its option to extend. Therefore, the option is a derivative that would need to be accounted for separately at fair value through profit or loss, even though its value is closely related to interest rates that also affect the value of the underlying debt host.

The treatment regarding term-extending options is justified, because otherwise such options could be used to avoid the requirement to bifurcate a derivative in circumstances where the investor might not recover substantially all of its initial recorded investment (see further [para 41.24](62.md#s257-Para_41.24)). Term-extending options in host debts typically involve postponement of the repayment of the principal and, even though such postponement does not cause the failure of the investor to recover substantially all of its initial recorded investment, it can significantly reduce the fair value of the recovery of that investment.

Alternatively, if the term-extending option was considered to be a loan commitment outside IFRS 9’s scope, it would not be separated from the host debt instrument.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_69"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.43.2"></a>FAQ 41.43.2 – Should ‘green variability’ features be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para 4.3.3  
 Reference to standing text: [41.43](62.md#s257-Para_41.43), [42.121](63.md#s268-Para_42.121) 

  

  
 **Question**

Entities might issue bonds or obtain loans where the interest rate is linked to certain sustainability performance objectives (sometimes called environmental, social and governance or ‘ESG’ metrics). The contractual terms of these products provide that cash flows vary depending on these sustainability-linked measures (referred to as ‘green variability’). Examples of green variability are provided in [FAQ 42.41.1](64.md#s271-FAQ_42.41.1).

In the case of a financial liability, should such green variability be considered an embedded derivative? If so, should this embedded derivative be accounted for separately?

**Answer**

For green variability to meet the definition of a derivative set out in Appendix A of IFRS 9, among other criteria, the value of the feature must fluctuate in response to an underlying that is not a non-financial variable that is specific to a party to the contract. In determining whether this underlying criterion is met, the type of the sustainability-linked measure is important.

Where the sustainability-linked measure is a non-financial variable specific to the issuing entity or the holder, no embedded derivative would exist as the green variability as a separate stand-alone instrument would not meet the definition of a derivative. Though the specific facts and circumstances would need to be assessed, this would be expected to be the case for financial liabilities that contain measures such as the issuer’s CO2 emissions over a defined period, the issuer’s compliance with emissions and waste regulation standards, or with energy consumption standards.

Where the sustainability-linked measure is not a non-financial variable specific to the issuer or the holder, given it will be settled at a future date, the feature would then meet the IFRS 9 definition of a derivative if it required no initial net investment or an initial net investment smaller than would be required for other types of contracts that would be expected to have a similar response to changes in market factors.

Where the sustainability-linked feature meets the definition of a derivative, it will need to be considered whether it shall be accounted for separately as a derivative under paragraph 4.3.3 of IFRS 9. Where the measure is not specific to the issuing entity or the holder, the economic characteristics and risks of the sustainability-linked feature would not be expected to be closely related to the economic characteristics and risks of the host contract. In this case, the sustainability-linked feature would be separately accounted for if all other conditions are met.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.44.1"></a>FAQ 41.44.1 – Should the embedded credit derivative in a credit-linked note be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.5(f)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.44](62.md#s257-Para_41.44)  
 

  

  
**Question**

An investment bank issues a credit-linked note to another party (the investor) in return for consideration equal to the par value of the note. The coupon on the note is linked to the credit risk of a portfolio of third-party bonds (the reference assets). In economic terms, the credit-linked note comprises a fixed income instrument with an embedded credit derivative. Should this derivative be separated?

**Answer**

The embedded credit derivative must be accounted for separately, because it is linked to credit risks of debt instruments issued by third parties and not to the credit risk of the host debt instrument issued by the investment bank. The notion of an embedded derivative in a hybrid instrument refers to provisions incorporated into a single contract and not to provisions in separate contracts between different counterparties.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_71"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.44.2"></a>FAQ 41.44.2 – Should non-viability clauses be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.5(f)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.44](62.md#s257-Para_41.44)  
 

  

  
**Question**

The terms of a debt instrument might include clauses that stipulate that, if the issuer suffers severe financial distress, the entire loan is written down (that is, forgiven). These types of clause are known as non-viability clauses and, in practice, are often included in debt instruments issued by banks, where the triggering event is normally the breach of a specified capital ratio or the bank’s regulator judging the bank to be in severe financial distress. Should these clauses be separated?

**Answer**

There is no specific guidance in IFRS on how to account for these clauses. The non-viability clause will cause some or all of the cash flows under the debt to be modified; this is because, on occurrence, the entire loan will be forgiven by the holder. The above-mentioned term does not meet the definition of an embedded derivative (see Appendix A to IFRS 9), since the modification is linked only to changes in a non-financial variable specific to a party to the contract (capital ratio).

The issuer would adjust the gross carrying amount (see [chapter 42 para 45](63.md#s265-Para_42.45)) to reflect changes in the loan’s estimated payments resulting from the non-viability clause.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_72"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.44.3"></a>FAQ 41.44.3 – Do cash CDOs contain embedded derivatives that must be separated from the host financial liability by the issuer?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 paras B4.3.5(f)  
Reference to standing text: [41.44](62.md#s257-Para_41.44)  
 Banking and capital markets

  

 

**Background**

 

IFRS 9 requires embedded derivatives in hybrid contracts that are not a financial asset to be separated if they are not closely related to their host contract. Credit derivatives that are embedded in a host financial liability and allow one party to transfer the credit risk of a particular reference asset, which it might not own, to another party (the ‘guarantor’) are not closely related to the host financial liability. Such credit derivatives allow the guarantor to assume the credit risk associated with the reference asset without directly owning it. [IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.5(f)].

 

In cash (or cash flow) collateralised debt obligations (‘CDOs’), financial assets such as loans or bonds are transferred from a sponsor to a special purpose entity (‘SPE’) in return for cash. To fund the acquired assets, the SPE issues tranches of notes with different levels of credit risk.

 

The most common way of creating tranches with different credit risk characteristics that are serviced by the same pool of assets is through subordination:

 

- If an asset held by the SPE defaults, the default affects the most junior tranche first (that is, the notional amount of the equity tranche is reduced by the amount of the loss), without bankruptcy of the SPE.

- When cumulative losses exceed the notional amount of the lowest tranche, the notional of the next tranche is reduced to the extent of the excess loss, through the same process.

 

The higher credit risk borne by the lower tranches is remunerated through a higher coupon.

 

**Question**

 

Do cash CDOs contain embedded derivatives that must be separated from the host financial liability by the issuer?

 

**Answer**

 

No. An embedded derivative is not required to be separated, because the SPE owns the reference asset and therefore assumes the credit risk directly. The junior (for example, equity) tranches of debt are reduced only if an actual default occurs, and not on the basis of a change in credit rating or otherwise. Although the risk in the notes is ultimately linked to the performance of the SPE’s assets, it is not linked to an individual asset or group of assets. Since the adjustment is made on the basis of the performance of all of the SPE’s assets as well as the degree of subordination of the tranches, the economic characteristics of the embedded derivative can be considered closely related to those of the host financial liability.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_73"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.46.1"></a>FAQ 41.46.1 – In a dual currency bond, should the embedded swap be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.5(c)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.46](62.md#s257-Para_41.46)  
 

  

  
**Question**

An entity with pound sterling as its functional currency issues a £10 million debt instrument that provides for the annual payment of interest in euros and the repayment of principal in pound sterling. Should euro interest payments be separated from the pound sterling host contract?

**Answer**

This dual currency bond can be viewed as containing a foreign currency swap that converts the pound sterling interest payments to euro interest payments. This embedded swap is not accounted for separately because, under IAS 21, any exchange gains and losses arising on the annual euro interest payments due to exchange rate changes are themselves reported in profit or loss. [IAS 21 para 28]. See also the example of the measurement of a dual currency bond in [chapter 42 para 138](63.md#s269-Para_42.138).

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_74"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.46.2"></a>FAQ 41.46.2 – Should a foreign currency option in a loan be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.5(c)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.46](62.md#s257-Para_41.46) 

  

  
**Question**

An entity issues a C10 million loan at an above market rate. The entity has the option to repay the loan at par for C10 million or a fixed amount in a foreign currency, say €15 million. Should a derivative be separated?

**Answer**

The debt instrument can be viewed as combining a loan at prevailing market interest rates and a foreign currency option. In effect, the issuer has purchased a foreign currency option that allows it to take advantage of changes in foreign currency exchange rates during the outstanding period of the loan. The premium for the option is paid as part of the higher interest cost. Similarly, the lender has written an option that exposes it to foreign currency risk. Because the borrower has the option of repaying the loan in its functional currency or in a foreign currency, the option is not closely related to the debt instrument (that is, it is not directly related to the currency of the loan or the interest rate that applies to that currency). The principle discussed in [paragraph 41.36](62.md#s257-Para_41.36) does not apply, since application of the IAS 21 rules for revaluing monetary items would not lead to revaluation of the foreign currency option. So the embedded foreign currency option should be separated from the host contract and accounted for separately by both parties to the contract. In contrast, if both the principal and the interest payments were made in a foreign currency (that is, if no optionality was involved), there would be no embedded derivative.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_75"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.46.3"></a>FAQ 41.46.3 – Should a feature linking interest payments to foreign currency exchange rates be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.5(c)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.46](62.md#s257-Para_41.46) 

  

  
**Question**

An entity issues a £10 million debt security at par. Quarterly interest payments, which are payable in pound sterling, are computed based on a formula that is linked to the £/€ exchange rate. Should a derivative be separated?

**Answer**

The quarterly interest is not denominated in foreign currency and, therefore, the principle discussed in [paragraph 41.46](62.md#s257-Para_41.46) does not apply. Since the formula for computing the interest payable on a sterling bond is linked to the £/€ exchange rate, and is not based on an interest rate or an index based on interest rates, an inflation index or the creditworthiness of the debtor, it is an embedded derivative that is not closely related to the sterling bond. Hence, it should be accounted for separately. The embedded derivative is a forward foreign exchange contract.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.51.1"></a>FAQ 41.51.1 – Should a put option in puttable preference shares be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.7  
 Reference to standing text: [41.51](62.md#s258-Para_41.51) 

  

  
**Question**

An entity issues C50 million of mandatorily redeemable preference shares at par with a fixed dividend of 8% per annum. The preference shares are puttable to the company for cash at par if the market interest rate exceeds 12%. Would the issuer need to separate the option?

**Answer**

The mandatorily redeemable preference shares (including fixed dividend) are a financial liability of the issuer and similar to a debt instrument. Furthermore, the embedded put option’s exercise price, which is par, is approximately equal to the preference shares’ amortised cost. Hence, the put option is considered closely related to the debt host.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_77"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.51.2"></a>FAQ 41.51.2 – Should a conversion option in convertible preference shares be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.7  
 Reference to standing text: [41.51](62.md#s258-Para_41.51)  
 

  

  
**Question**

An entity issues C50 million of irredeemable preference shares that give the holders a preferential right to return of capital in a winding up, but which are also convertible into a fixed number of ordinary shares at the holder’s option. Any dividends paid in the year are at the discretion of the issuer. Would the issuer need to separate the option?

**Answer**

The preference shares are irredeemable and there is no obligation on the issuer to pay dividends, so the shares are equity in nature. The conversion feature represents an embedded written call option on the company’s ordinary shares which, on a free-standing basis, would be an equity instrument of the entity. Since both the embedded call option and the host are equity instruments, the entity does not account for the embedded option separately. See also [FAQ 43.41.1](67.md#s281-FAQ_43.41.1).

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_78"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.52.1"></a>FAQ 41.52.1 – Are extension, termination and purchase options in lease agreements embedded derivatives?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(f), IFRS 9 para BA.5  
 Reference to standing text: [41.52](62.md#s259-Para_41.52), [40.35](61.md#s251-Para_40.35)  

  

  
Often, lease contracts include extension, termination or purchase options. Such options would not qualify as embedded derivatives where their only underlying is the price of renting or purchasing the leased asset. Such an underlying is a non-financial variable which is specific to a party to the contract and, accordingly, the option fails the definition of a derivative. Although the price of renting or purchasing the leased asset will be affected by market prices for such assets, the price is for the specific asset that is the subject of the lease and also reflects the condition of that asset.

The accounting for these options is considered in the chapter on leases (see [chapter 15](33.md#s106-chap_15)).

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_79"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.52.3"></a>FAQ 41.52.3 – Are residual value guarantees in lease agreements embedded derivatives?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(f)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.52](62.md#s259-Para_41.52) 

  

  
Where a lease includes a residual value guarantee, the lessee undertakes to make a payment if the residual value of the asset at the end of the lease falls below a pre-determined amount. A residual value guarantee does not meet the definition of a derivative, because its underlying (the price of the leased asset) is a non-financial variable that is specific to a party to the contract. In addition, under IFRS 16 the amount expected to be payable under a residual value guarantee is treated as part of the lease payments and is accounted for as such. Therefore, it does not need to be accounted for under IFRS 9.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_80"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.55.1"></a>FAQ 41.55.1 – Should inflation features in lease rentals be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(f)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.29](62.md#s257-Para_41.29), [41.55](62.md#s259-Para_41.55)  
 

  

  
 **Illustration 1**

**Question**

A UK entity leases a property in France. The rentals are paid in euros and increase each year in line with the increase in the rate of inflation in France. The lessor is located in France. Should the inflation feature be separated?

**Answer**

The future cash flows (the rental payments) will change in response to changes in the inflation index in France. The embedded inflation-indexed payments are not leveraged, and they relate to the economic environment in which the leased asset is located. Therefore, the inflation adjustment is closely related to the lease contract and separation is not required.

Note that this is a lease liability in a currency that is not the functional currency of the UK lessee.

**Illustration 2**

**Question**

A South African entity leases a property in Mauritius. The rentals are denominated in USD and increase each year in line with the increase in a recognised measure of general inflation in the US. Should the inflation feature be separated from the host lease liability?

**Answer**

The future cash flows (the lease payments) will change in response to changes in the inflation index in the US. The embedded inflation-indexed payments are not leveraged, and although they do not relate to the economic environment in which the leased asset is located, they are indexed to a recognised measure of general inflation in the economic environment of the currency in which the lease payments (and the resulting lease liability) are denominated (that is, USD). Based on the guidance in [paragraph 41.29](62.md#s257-Para_41.29), the inflation adjustment is closely related to the host lease liability and separation is not required.

Note that in both illustrations above, the lease liabilities are denominated in currencies that are not the functional currencies of the lessees. Foreign currency features in lease contracts are discussed further at [paragraph 41.58](62.md#s259-Para_41.58) onwards.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_81"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.55.2"></a>FAQ 41.55.2 – How are leveraged inflation features in lease rentals separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para IG C.1/IFRS 9.B4.3.8(f)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.55](62.md#s259-Para_41.55)  
  
 

  

  
 **Question**

A UK entity enters into a 10-year property lease in the UK as lessee. Rental payments in pound sterling are contractually fixed for the first year, but increase thereafter at a rate of 1.5 times the change in the UK retail price index (RPI). Since the cash flows change by an amount in excess of the change in RPI, such cash flows are leveraged and hence the leveraged RPI feature is an embedded derivative that should be accounted for separately in accordance with paragraph B4.3.8(f) of IFRS 9.

How are the terms of the embedded derivative and the host instrument identified? In particular: (1) Should the embedded derivative be defined as 1.5 times the change in RPI (that is, the embedded derivative is the entire effect of the RPI indexation) or as 0.5 times the change in RPI (that is, the embedded derivative is limited to only the excess amount constituting leverage)? and (2) Is the host instrument a fixed or floating rate lease contract?

**Answer**

Paragraph IG C.1 of IFRS 9 requires the terms of the host instrument to reflect the stated or implied substantive terms of the hybrid contract. In the absence of implied or stated terms, the entity makes its own judgement of the terms. However, an entity cannot identify a component that is not specified or cannot establish terms that would result in the separation of an embedded derivative that is not already clearly present in the hybrid contract (that is, it cannot create a cash flow that does not exist). Furthermore, the terms of an embedded non-option derivative must result in the embedded derivative having a fair value of zero at initial recognition.

It would not be appropriate to identify the host contract as a lease with a fixed rate escalated at 1 times RPI and the embedded derivative with a pay leg equal to 0.5 times RPI (that is, only the portion above the change in RPI) for the following reasons [IFRS 9 App B para B4.3.4]:

- Separating the leveraged and non-leveraged portions of RPI identifies cash flows that are not clearly present in the lease contract, which is not in accordance with the guidance in paragraph IG C.1.

- The leveraged and non-leveraged portions relate to the same risk and, since the inflation is leveraged overall, the entire link to RPI is no longer considered closely related. This is consistent with the guidance in paragraph B4.3.4 of IFRS 9.

For the scenario above, the stated terms of the lease are not clearly fixed or floating. Hence, the entity could identify the host contract as either:

- A fixed rate lease contract. The embedded derivative would then have a fixed receive leg (equal to the estimated payments under a non-inflationary host contract) and a pay leg equal to 1.5 times RPI.

- A floating rate lease contract in which the rental resets periodically to reflect changes in the market rate of rentals. The embedded derivative would have a floating receive leg (equal to the market rate rentals) and a pay leg equal to 1.5 times RPI. However, this approach is acceptable only if there is sufficient observable pricing available to determine the floating/market rate of the host, such as a periodic spot market rental.

However, in an environment in which market rentals are strongly correlated with changes in RPI, approach b) above might give a similar result to defining the embedded derivative as 0.5 times RPI.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.55.3"></a>FAQ 41.55.3 – Should inflation features in lease rentals which relate to a different economic environment be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(f)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.55](62.md#s259-Para_41.55)  
 

  

  
**Question**

The facts are the same as in [ Illustration 1 of FAQ 41.55.1](62.md#s262-FAQ_41.55.1), except that the inflation adjustment relates to a specified US annual inflation rate (for example, retail price index or consumer price index). The landlord is a US entity. Should the inflation feature be separated?

**Answer**

Yes. The future cash flows (the rental payments) will change in response to changes in the US inflation index. The embedded inflation-indexed payments, although not leveraged, relate to a different economic environment from that in which the leased asset is located, the environment in which the UK entity operates and the currency in which the lease payments are denominated. So the inflation-indexed payment is not closely related to the lease host contract and would be accounted for separately.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.55.4"></a>FAQ 41.55.4 – Should upward-only inflation increases in lease rentals be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(f)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.55](62.md#s259-Para_41.55)  
 

  

  
**Question**

Entity A is a tenant in a 10-year UK property lease agreement. The rent for the first year is contractually determined at C100,000. The rental payments will change in line with an index of UK prices; so, if, at the end of year 1, the index had increased by 3%, the rent for year 2 would be C103,000. However, the lease provides that the rent cannot be decreased. So, if, during year 2, the index fell by 1%, the rent for year 3 would remain at C103,000. The index shows that UK prices are generally increasing at the inception of the lease (that is, there is UK inflation). Should the inflation feature be separated?

**Answer**

The rental payments are linked to an index. They follow that index while it is increasing, but do not follow it if it decreases. This feature is often known as ‘upward only’. This lease contains a floor in the rental payments, because of the upward-only feature, so it is necessary to identify whether the floor is in or out of the money at the inception of the lease. Assuming that the initial rent is at market rates then, even in a deflationary environment, the floor will be at the money at inception. Therefore the derivative is closely related.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.56.1"></a>FAQ 41.56.1 – Should contingent lease rentals be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(f)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.56](62.md#s259-Para_41.56)  
 

  

  
**Question**

A UK entity leases a property located in the UK. The rentals consist of a base rental of C10,000 plus 5% of the lessee’s sales each month. Should the sales-related payments be separated?

**Answer**

The rental payments will vary according to an underlying, being the entity’s sales.

However, the portion of the contingent rentals based on related sales is considered to be closely related to the host lease contract. Therefore, the sales-related payments should not be separated.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.57.1"></a>FAQ 41.57.1 – Should SONIA indexation of lease rentals be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(f)  
Reference to standing text: [41.57](62.md#s259-Para_41.57)  
  

  

  
**Question**

A UK entity leases a property located in the UK. The lease rentals are denominated in GBP and indexed to the SONIA rate. The contract does not contain any leverage feature. Does the embedded derivative need to be separated?

**Answer**

The embedded derivative does not need to be separated, because the rentals are based on a variable interest rate index of the UK economy.

On the other hand, if the rentals were indexed to a variable interest rate of an economic environment that is different from the economic environment in which the leased asset is located, the related embedded derivative would not be regarded as closely related, in the same way that a similarly indexed inflation payment would not be regarded as closely related (see [para 41.53](62.md#s259-Para_41.53)).

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.59.1"></a>FAQ 41.59.1 – Should foreign currency exposure be separated from lease payments? (1)

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(d)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.59](62.md#s259-Para_41.59)  
 

  

  
 **Question**

A UK company enters into a lease, denominated in pound sterling, for a property in France with a European lessor. The functional currency of the lessee and the lessor are the pound sterling and the euro respectively. The lessor classifies the lease as an operating lease. Should the lessor separate the pound sterling currency element from the host lease contract?

**Answer**

Paragraph B4.3.8(d) of IFRS 9 provides that contracts, other than financial instruments, that specify payments denominated in the functional currency of any substantial party to the contract are closely related to the host contract.

The lease payments are denominated in the functional currency of the lessee, which is a substantial party to the contract. Therefore, the embedded derivative is closely related to the host contract and is not separated. It is not appropriate to argue that, because the payments are in a foreign currency and the European lessor is exposed to currency risk, there is an embedded foreign currency forward converting euro payments to pound sterling.

On the other hand, if the lease payments are denominated in a currency that is unrelated to either party’s functional currency (for example, US dollars), the embedded foreign currency forward should be separated from the host lease contract (lease contract denominated in euros or pound sterling) and accounted for separately as a derivative.

For lessees under IFRS 16, if the lease payments are denominated in the lessor’s functional currency then the foreign currency element will be included in the lease liability recognised on the balance sheet and will be translated under IAS 21, instead of separating an embedded foreign currency derivative. If the lessee is applying the short-term or low-value lease exemption, see [para 41.59](62.md#s259-Para_41.59).

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_87"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.59.2"></a>FAQ 41.59.2 – Should foreign currency exposure be separated from lease payments? (2)

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(d)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.59](62.md#s259-Para_41.59)  
 

  

  
 **Question**

On 1 June 20X2, entity A, a Russian oil refinery, entered into an arrangement with entity S to lease a building in Moscow for a 10-year period. Entity A’s management has determined its functional currency to be the US dollar.

Entity S is a property management and development entity located in Russia. Its management has determined its functional currency to be the Russian rouble. Entity S classifies the lease as an operating lease.

Entity S set the monthly lease repayments in Swiss francs, to avoid exposure to any devaluation in the Russian rouble and to obtain a natural hedge for the repayment of its Swiss franc-denominated bonds. Should the Swiss franc currency element be separated from the host lease contract?

**Answer**

The Swiss franc is neither the functional currency of one of the substantial parties to the lease contract, nor a currency commonly used in Russia, nor the currency in which leases are routinely denominated in the world, so the lease contract contains an embedded derivative that is not closely related to the host contract.

From entity S’s perspective, the lease contract contains a series of embedded forward contracts to buy Swiss francs against Russian roubles. These embedded derivatives are not closely related to the host contract (lease contract denominated in Russian roubles) and should be accounted for separately. Entity S might, however, be able to use the separated forward contracts in a formal hedge of the cash flows on its Swiss franc bonds – see chapter 6.8A on hedging under IAS 39 or [chapter 46](74.md#s294-chap_46) if applying IFRS 9 for more details of when this could be permissible.

From entity A’s perspective, under IFRS 16 the foreign currency element will be included in the lease liability recognised on the balance sheet and will be translated under IAS 21, instead of separating an embedded foreign currency derivative, assuming the lease has been recognized on balance sheet. If the lessee applied the short-term or low-value exemption, and so did not recognise a lease liability, it would account for the lease contract as containing a series of embedded forward contracts to sell Swiss francs against US dollars. These embedded derivatives are not closely related to the host contract (lease contract denominated in US dollars), and so they should be accounted for separately.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.59.3"></a>FAQ 41.59.3 – If a parent guarantees a foreign subsidiary’s lease payments, is the parent’s functional currency considered?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(d)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.59](62.md#s259-Para_41.59)  
 

  

  
**Question**

A major French operating subsidiary of a US parent enters into a lease with a Swiss company that requires lease payments in US dollars. The lease payments are guaranteed by the US parent. The functional currencies of the respective entities are their local currencies. The lessor classifies the lease as an operating lease. Should the US dollar foreign currency element be separated?

**Answer**

The substantial parties to the lease contract are the French lessee and the Swiss lessor. The guarantor is not a substantial party to the contract and, therefore, its functional currency is of no relevance to the analysis (see further [para 41.68](62.md#s260-Para_41.68)). Since the lease payments are made in US dollars, which is not the functional currency of the lessee or the lessor, the embedded foreign currency swap would need to be accounted for separately by the lessor.

For the lessee, the foreign currency element will be included in the lease liability recognised on the balance sheet and will be translated under IAS 21, instead of separating an embedded foreign currency derivative, with the exception of short-term leases and leases of low-value assets (see [para 41.59](62.md#s259-Para_41.59)).

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.62.1"></a>FAQ 41.62.1 – Should a market price adjustment be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.5(d)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.62](62.md#s260-Para_41.62)  
 

  

  
**Question**

An entity (whose functional currency is pound sterling) contracts to purchase 200 tonnes of aluminium from a UK supplier in 12 months’ time. The aluminium is intended for use in the course of business. The purchase price will be the market price at the contract date plus an amount determined by a specified index of aluminium prices. The contract does not contain any leverage feature. Should the price adjustment feature be separated?

**Answer**

The future cash outflows are linked to movements in the market price for aluminium. The purpose of the embedded derivative is to ensure that the price paid for the aluminium is the market price at the date of purchase/supply, rather than the date when the two parties entered into the supply contract. This ensures that the seller passes any price risk to the entity. Because the underlying is related to the price of the aluminium purchased, the derivative is closely related to the aluminium supply contract (host) and would not be accounted for separately.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_90"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.62.2"></a>FAQ 41.62.2 – Should an electricity market price adjustment be separated from a coal purchase contract?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.5(d)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.62](62.md#s260-Para_41.62)  
 

  

  
**Question**

An entity enters into a coal purchase contract that includes a clause that links the price of coal to a pricing formula based on the prevailing electricity price at the date of delivery. The entity purchases the coal for its own use, and there are no provisions to settle the contract net. Should the price adjustment feature be separated?

**Answer**

The coal purchase contract is the host contract. The pricing formula that changes the price risk from coal price to electricity price is the embedded derivative. Although coal might be used for the production of electricity, the underlying based on electricity prices is not pertinent to both the changes in the cost and the changes in the fair value of coal. Therefore, the embedded derivative (the electricity price adjustment) is not closely related to the host contract and should be accounted for separately.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_91"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.62.3"></a>FAQ 41.62.3 – Should multiple market price adjustments be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.5(d)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.62](62.md#s260-Para_41.62)  
 

  

  
**Question**

An entity has entered into a contract with a customer to sell 10,000 wooden chairs for a period of five years. The price per chair is contractually determined at C100 per chair for the first year, and it increases thereafter in line with changes in certain indices: 50% of the price of each chair is linked to an index of timber prices, and 30% is linked to the UK wage inflation index. So, if the timber index rises by 10% and the wage inflation index increases by 5%, the price for each chair will be C106.50 ((C50 × 1.1) + (C30 × 1.05) + C20). The sales contract in its entirety does not meet the definition of a derivative, because it will be settled by physical delivery in the normal course of business. Should the price adjustment features be separated?

**Answer**

There are two embedded derivatives contained in this contract, because the price of chairs over the contract term is linked to the two indices (timber and wage inflation). The purpose of these embedded derivatives is to protect the entity’s profit margin over the contract term, by ensuring that changes in the prices of inputs are passed on to the end customer.

Although both indices are considered to be relevant and pertinent to the cost or fair value of the chairs being sold, they would be considered to be closely related to the host contract if the entity can reliably demonstrate that the standard mix of direct material and direct labour for each chair produced is maintained.

If the entity is unable to demonstrate reliably the clear linkage, the embedded derivatives might be leveraged (because the percentage of the price linked to each index might not accurately reflect the cost structure). In other words, the magnitude of the price adjustment might cause the price of the host contract to increase by more than an insignificant amount (see [para 41.13](62.md#s256-Para_41.13)). In that situation, the index-linked price adjustments would not be considered to be closely related.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_92"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.62.4"></a>FAQ 41.62.4 – Should proxy/formula pricing mechanisms be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.5(d), IFRS 13   
 Reference to standing text: [41.62](62.md#s260-Para_41.62) 

  

  
**Question 1**

An active market for a commodity does not exist for all or part of the duration of a supply contract. The price of the contract was set using an identifiable industry norm that is regularly used to price contracts in that commodity. Does this price mechanism represent an embedded derivative which needs to be separately accounted for?

**Answer**

No, contracts can be priced by reference to a market price or to a proxy/formula. A proxy/formula is normally used where an active market for the commodity does not exist for the duration of the contract.

An active market is one in which transactions for the asset or liability take place with sufficient frequency and volume to provide pricing information on an ongoing basis. [IFRS 13 App A].

If an active market exists, no embedded derivative would be separated from a contract that is priced at the variable market price of the same commodity or at a fixed price. However, any other pricing formula or proxy pricing mechanism would result in an embedded derivative which would have to be separated.

If an active market does not exist for a particular commodity in a similar location to the one specified in the contract, management should consider how other contracts for that particular commodity are normally priced, in the absence of a readily available market price. That pricing mechanism would not be considered an embedded derivative if it can be demonstrated that it is an identifiable industry ‘norm’ and that contracts are regularly priced according to that norm. This is because such a pricing mechanism does not modify the cash flows under the contract. [IFRS 9 para 4.3.1].

In circumstances where management has a long-term contract to purchase a commodity and there is a short-term market for the commodity for the first few years and no readily available market prices after that, the commodity could be priced based on liquid market curves for the initial years, and then the contract could be linked to an identifiable industry norm at which contracts are regularly priced. In such a contract, there will be no separated embedded derivatives. In such circumstances, if the commodity is priced based on a proxy formula for the whole duration of the contract (including the period when there are readily available market prices), the contract would contain an embedded derivative that would have to be separated and fair valued through profit or loss, unless it is designated as an effective hedging instrument.

Each contract should be reviewed on an individual basis, with appropriate evidence to support the conclusion that contracts are priced according to an identifiable industry ‘norm’ and that contracts are regularly priced according to that norm. It will be a matter of judgement to determine whether that supporting evidence is satisfactory.

**Question 2**

Management has a contract to purchase commodity A. Because there is no active market for commodity A, there are no available market prices, and hence the contract price is linked to the price of commodity B, for which there are an active market and available market prices. Commodity B is used as a normal proxy price mechanism for commodity A in the industry. Does this price mechanism represent an embedded derivative which needs to be separately accounted for?

**Answer**

No, the price linkage to commodity B does not constitute an embedded derivative; this is because linking to the price of commodity B is the normal proxy pricing mechanism for commodity A.

**Question 3**

Management has a contract to purchase commodity C. There are available market prices for commodity C. The contract price for commodity C is linked to the price of commodity D. Does this price mechanism represent an embedded derivative which needs to be separately accounted for?

**Answer**

Yes. There are available market prices for commodity C. The price linkage to commodity D therefore constitutes an embedded derivative. Management must assess the linkage to commodity D under IFRS 9, to determine whether the economic risks and characteristics of the embedded derivative are closely related to the economic risks and characteristics of the host contract, being commodity C.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.63.1"></a>FAQ 41.63.1 – Should an embedded derivative be separated from a contract that does not meet the ‘own use’ exemption?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.5  
 Reference to standing text: [41.63](62.md#s260-Para_41.63)  
 

  

  
**Question**

An entity enters into a forward contract to purchase 10,000 tonnes of coal. The contract does not qualify for the ‘normal’ purchase or sale exception, because there is a provision in the contract for net settlement, and the entity’s past practice indicates that it will settle the contract net. Settlement is based on changes in the fair value of coal during the contract’s term, plus a payment provision based on a formula linked to prevailing electricity prices. Should the entity account for the electricity price adjustment feature separately from the coal purchase contract?

**Answer**

Given that the own use exception does not apply, the entire forward contract to purchase coal will be accounted for as a derivative in accordance with IFRS 9, with gains and losses on the entire contract reported in the profit and loss account. Therefore, there is no need to identify separately the leverage payment provision as an embedded derivative. Because the host contract is measured at fair value through profit or loss, an embedded derivative cannot be separated from the host contract.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_94"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.66.1"></a>FAQ 41.66.1 – Should an inflation feature in a purchase contract be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(f)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.66](62.md#s260-Para_41.66)  
 

  

  
**Question**

A UK entity contracts to purchase a fixed quantity of raw materials from a UK supplier in 12 months’ time. The raw materials are intended for use in the entity’s business. The purchase price will be the market price at the contract date plus an adjustment for UK inflation from the beginning of the contract. The contract does not contain any leverage feature. Should the inflation element be separated?

**Answer**

The future cash flows are linked to an inflation index that is not leveraged and the index is in the local economic environment. Therefore, the embedded derivative is closely related to the host purchase contract.

If, on the other hand, the entity is unable to demonstrate that these goods are to be used in the course of its business (that is, the own use exception does not apply), the entire contract would be treated as a forward purchase contract that would fall to be settled net. In that situation, the forward purchase contract meets the definition of a derivative and is accounted for at fair value through profit or loss. So there is no need to account separately for any derivatives that might be embedded in it.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_95"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.67.1"></a>FAQ 41.67.1 – Should a price cap and floor in a purchase contract be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(b)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.67](62.md#s260-Para_41.67)  
 

  

  
**Question**

A manufacturer enters into a long-term contract to purchase a specified quantity of certain raw materials from a supplier. Under the contract, the supplier will provide the raw materials at the list price prevailing at the delivery date, but within a specified range (for example, the purchase price cannot exceed C20 per kg or fall below C15 per kg). The current list price at inception of the contract is C18 per kg. Would the cap or floor need to be separated from the host contract?

**Answer**

From the manufacturer’s perspective, the embedded derivatives contained in the purchase contracts are two options: a purchased call option with a strike price of C20 per kg; and a written put with a strike price of C15 per kg. These options would each meet the definition of a derivative if they were free-standing, because they have a notional amount (the fixed quantity to be purchased) and an underlying (the price per kg), they require no initial net investment, and they will be settled in the future.

The economic characteristics and risks of the two options are closely related to the purchase contract, because the options are indexed to the asset’s purchase price that is the subject of the contract, and both the embedded cap (cap price of C20 is greater than current list price of C18) and the floor (floor price of C15 is lower than current list price of C18) are out of the money at inception of the contract. Hence, the embedded derivatives are closely related and the host contract can be considered to be a purchase contract that requires delivery of the specified quantity of raw materials at a price equal to the current list price.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_96"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.68.1"></a>FAQ 41.68.1 – What is a ‘substantial party’?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(d)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.68](62.md#s260-Para_41.68)  
 

  

  
The term ‘substantial party’ to the contract is not explained in IFRS 9. Generally, it is taken to mean a party that is acting as principal to the contract (that is, the buyer or seller). A bank that provides a guarantee, on behalf of a local importer to a foreign supplier, that the buyer will meet its payment obligations in foreign currency under the contract’s terms is not a substantial party to the contract. Its functional currency is of no relevance in determining whether the payments denominated in the foreign currency are closely related to the contract.

There would be a rebuttable presumption that the local currencies of the economic environment in which the counterparties operate would be their functional currencies. In addition, the local currency is always presumed to be commonly used. If payments are denominated in a currency that is not the local currency of either party to the contract, the contracting parties would need to determine whether that currency is the functional currency of their counterparty. All available information concerning the indicators for determining functional currency in IAS 21 would need to be considered in determining the functional currency. An entity should not rely on a single indicator, such as the currency in which the counterparty’s sales are denominated.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_97"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.68.2"></a>FAQ 41.68.2 – Should payments denominated in the functional currency of a party to the contract be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(d)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.68](62.md#s260-Para_41.68)  
 

  

  
**Question**

A UK entity (whose functional currency is pound sterling) contracts to sell goods to a French purchaser (whose functional currency is the euro). The contract will be fulfilled by the physical delivery of goods, and payments by the French buyer would be made in euros. Is there a need to separate a derivative?

**Answer**

The payments in euros expose the UK entity to currency risk, because the cash flows under the contract will vary with the £/€ exchange rate. The contract can be viewed as a host contract that is denominated in sterling, containing an embedded foreign currency swap that converts payments in pound sterling to euros, or an embedded foreign currency forward contract to sell pound sterling and buy euros. However, the embedded swap or the forward contract is not separated, because the payments are denominated in the functional currency of the French buyer, who is a substantial party to the contract.

 

<a id="s262-chap_41_navPoint_98"></a>
### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.68.3"></a>FAQ 41.68.3 – What is the maturity date of a foreign currency embedded derivative separated from an executory contract?

 The UK seller separates the firm commitment to sell goods into two contracts: a host contract to sell goods in pound sterling; and an embedded forward contract to buy euros and sell pounds sterling with an inception date of 1 March 20X6. But there is some debate regarding the deemed date of settlement/maturity of the embedded derivative.

Some take the view that the embedded derivative’s settlement/maturity date is 30 June 20X6 (that is, when the goods are delivered), on the basis that the derivative embedded in the host firm commitment can no longer exist after that date, because there is no host contract (the seller having fulfilled its commitment by delivering the goods to the customer). Following performance by the seller, the seller has an unconditional right to receive consideration from the customer. In other words, the embedded derivative is in the sales contract and not in the receivable – the derivative has effectively been settled by creation of a financial asset (receivable). ### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.68.4"></a>FAQ 41.68.4 – Should foreign currency features be separated if the functional currency of a party to the contract changes?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(d)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.68](62.md#s260-Para_41.68)  
 

  

  
**Question**

A UK entity (whose functional currency is pound sterling) contracts to sell goods to a Japanese purchaser (whose functional currency is the yen). The contract will be fulfilled by the physical delivery of goods, and payments by the Japanese buyer would be made in yen. Subsequently, because of changes in economic circumstances, the functional currency of the Japanese buyer changes to US dollars. Is there a need to separate a derivative?

**Answer**

The UK entity must assess whether the embedded derivative should be separated from the host sales contract and accounted for as a derivative when it first becomes a party to the contract. At that time, the functional currency of the Japanese buyer was the yen. So, no separation of the embedded derivative is required, because the yen is the functional currency of a substantial party to the contract. The issue is whether this assessment should be revisited following the change in functional currency of the Japanese buyer.

As explained in [paragraph 41.19](62.md#s256-Para_41.19), re-assessment is only required if there is a change in the contract’s terms that meet certain criteria. In this example, the contract terms are unchanged. The UK entity will still make payments in yen. Therefore, the embedded derivative continues to be treated as closely related, even though the factors that led to the initial assessment have changed. However, any yen-denominated contracts entered into with the Japanese buyer after it changed its functional currency to US dollar would fail the closely related criterion, as they would not be in the functional currency of either party to the contract.

This treatment also applies to the Japanese buyer, even though it will be exposed to yen/dollar currency risk when it makes payments under the contract. Such exchange gains and losses will fall to be accounted for in accordance with IAS 21. In other words, as long as the contract terms remain unchanged, the Japanese buyer cannot create an embedded currency exposure that did not exist at the date when it became a party to the contract.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.68.5"></a>FAQ 41.68.5 – Should an option to make payments in alternative currencies be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(d)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.68](62.md#s260-Para_41.68)  
 

  

  
**Question**

The facts are the same as in [FAQ 41.68.1](62.md#s262-FAQ_41.68.1), except that the contract contains an option to make payment either in euros or in Swiss francs. Is there a need to separate a derivative?

**Answer**

The UK seller has written an option to receive payments in a currency that is not its functional currency, and so it is exposed to pound sterling/euro or pound sterling/Swiss franc currency risk. Although the euro is the functional currency of the other substantial party to the contract, there is no certainty at inception of the contract that settlement will be in euros. Hence, the foreign currency option is not closely related to the host.

Similarly, the French buyer has effectively purchased an option to make payments in its own functional currency (euros) or in a foreign currency (Swiss francs) that exposes it to euro/Swiss franc currency risk. Although the French buyer has the option to settle the contract in its own currency and eliminate any exchange risk, there is no certainty at inception of the contract that it would eventually exercise that option. Since the contract could also be settled in Swiss francs, the settlement could either be potentially favourable or unfavourable, and it is not closely related to the purchase contract.

Accordingly, the embedded option should be separated from the host contract and accounted for separately by both parties to the contract.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.68.6"></a>FAQ 41.68.6 – Should embedded foreign currency derivatives with option features be separated?

 

Reference to standard:IFRS 9 para B4.3.8(d)  
 Reference to standing text: [41.68](62.md#s260-Para_41.68)  
 

  

**Question**

Foreign currency derivatives embedded in executory contracts sometimes contain option features. So, for example, there might be contracts in which changes in foreign exchange rates affect the cash flows resulting from the contract only if the spot exchange rate at the date of settlement exceeds a certain level. Other foreign currency derivatives contain a cap and/or a floor.

Take the following examples:

**Example 1**

- Entity A [functional currency RUB] enters into a contract to purchase equipment from a US supplier [functional currency USD].

- The contract contains the following pricing mechanism:

- The price of the equipment is denominated in USD while the actual payment (settlement) will be made in RUB.

- On the order date, the parties agree to a fixed foreign currency exchange rate (‘contract rate’) and an applicable range of 10% above or below that rate.

- If at the settlement date the prevailing spot exchange rate is within that range, the ‘contract rate’ will apply.

- If at the settlement date the prevailing spot exchange rate is outside that range, entity A and its supplier agree to share the difference between the actual spot exchange rate at the settlement date and the ‘contract rate’.

**Example 2**

- Entity A [functional currency RUB] enters into a contract to purchase equipment from a US supplier [functional currency USD].

- The contract contains the following pricing mechanism:

- The price of the equipment is denominated in USD while the actual payment (settlement) will be made in RUB.

- Payments will be calculated based on the prevailing spot exchange rate at the date of settlement subject however to a pre-defined cap and floor.

- If at the settlement date the prevailing spot exchange rate is below the cap but above the floor, that rate will apply.

- If at the settlement date the prevailing spot exchange rate is above the cap or below the floor, the pre-defined cap or floor applies (that is, the payments are calculated based on a foreign currency exchange rate set in the contract).

Does entity A have to separate the embedded foreign currency derivative?

**Answer**

Yes. In terms of the three conditions for separating an embedded derivative in paragraph 4.3.3 of IFRS 9, conditions (b) and (c) are met. This is because the foreign currency derivative that is embedded in the contract meets the definition of a derivative and the executory contract is not measured at fair value through profit or loss. Hence the key question is whether the derivative is closely related to the host contract (condition (a)).

Paragraph B4.3.8(d) of IFRS 9 states that an embedded foreign currency derivative in a host contract that is not a financial instrument (such as a contract for the purchase or sale of a non-financial item where the price is denominated in a foreign currency) is closely related to the host contract provided inter alia, it does not contain an option feature.

An embedded foreign currency derivative that contains one or more option features is not closely related to the host contract even if all the other criteria in paragraph B4.3.8(d) of IFRS 9 are met. In both the examples above, the embedded derivative contains option features. In example 1, the option feature arises from the sharing of the difference between the actual spot exchange rate at the settlement date and the ‘contract rate’; in example 2 it arises from the cap and the floor.

Accordingly, in both examples the embedded foreign currency derivative are not closely related and hence have to be separated from the host contract. Since they relate to the same risk exposure and are not readily separable and independent of each other, they are accounted for together as a single compound embedded derivative in accordance with paragraph B4.3.4 of IFRS 9.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.69.1"></a>FAQ 41.69.1 – Should a leveraged foreign currency feature in an ‘own use’ contract be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para IG C9  
 Reference to standing text: [41.69](62.md#s260-Para_41.69)  
 

  

  
**Question**

Entity A, whose functional currency is the euro, enters into a contract with entity B, whose functional currency is the Norwegian krone, to purchase oil in six months for US$10 million. The host oil contract is not within IFRS 9’s scope, because it was entered into and continues to be for the purpose of delivery of a non-financial item in accordance with the entity’s expected purchase, sale or usage requirements. The oil contract includes a leveraged foreign exchange provision that states that the parties, in addition to the provision of, and payment for, oil, will exchange an amount equal to the fluctuation in the exchange rate of the US dollar and Norwegian krone applied to a notional amount of US$10 million. Should the foreign currency provision be separated?

**Answer**

The payment provision under the host oil contract of US$10 million can be viewed as a foreign currency derivative, because the US dollar is not the functional currency of either entity A or entity B. This foreign currency derivative would not be separated, however, because the second bullet point of [paragraph 41.68](62.md#s260-Para_41.68) makes it clear that a crude oil contract that requires payment in US dollars is not regarded as a host contract with a foreign currency derivative.

However, the leveraged foreign exchange provision, that states that the parties will exchange an amount equal to the fluctuation in the exchange rate of the US dollar and Norwegian krone applied to a notional amount of US$10 million, is in addition to the required payment for the oil transaction. It is unrelated to the host oil contract and, therefore, should be separated from the host oil contract, and accounted for as an embedded derivative.

[IFRS 9 para IG C8].

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.71.1"></a>FAQ 41.71.1 – Should a foreign currency feature be separated for a contract denominated in a currency commonly used in local business transactions?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para BCZ4.95  
 Reference to standing text: [41.71](62.md#s260-Para_41.71)  
 

  

  
**Question**

An entity, located in a country with a hyper-inflationary economy, contracts to purchase raw materials for use in its manufacturing process. The contract is denominated in US dollars, a stable currency, and not in the local currency, because most local transactions and external trade are undertaken in US dollars. Should a foreign currency derivative be separated?

**Answer**

The embedded foreign currency derivative (denominated in a currency other than the local currency) does not need to be separated, because the contract is denominated in a currency that is commonly used in the local economic environment.

If the contract was priced in another international stable currency (say, the euro) rather than US dollars, and the euro is not a currency that is commonly used in local business transactions, the embedded derivative (euro versus the entity’s functional currency) would have to be separated.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.81.1"></a>FAQ 41.81.1 – How should a non-option-based derivative be separated?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.3  
 Reference to standing text: [41.81](62.md#s261-Para_41.81)  
 

  

  
If embedded non-option derivatives could be separated on different terms to those stated or implied in the contract, a single hybrid instrument could be decomposed into an infinite variety of combinations of host instruments and embedded derivatives. However, an embedded derivative that is not already clearly present should not be separated – that is, a cash flow that does not exist cannot be created (with the exception of, for example, assuming a functional currency cash flow in an embedded foreign currency forward contract).

If the terms of an embedded forward contract were determined so as to result in a fair value other than zero at the inception of the hybrid instrument, that amount would essentially represent a borrowing or lending. Therefore, it is inappropriate to separate an embedded non-option derivative on terms that result in a fair value other than zero at the inception of the hybrid instrument. This means that the forward price assumed in the embedded derivative should generally be at market rates observed when the embedded derivative is separated.

[IFRS 9 para IG C.1]. [IFRS 9 para IG C.2].

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.81.2"></a>FAQ 41.81.2 – How should a non-option-based derivative be separated to produce zero fair value at inception?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.3  
 Reference to standing text: [41.81](62.md#s261-Para_41.81)  
 

  

  
Entity X advances C900 to entity Y for one year at 6% interest rate, and it concurrently enters into an equity-based derivative in which it will receive any increase or pay any decrease in the current market price (C100) of entity Z’s equity shares. The current forward price for one year for entity Z’s equity shares is C200. These two transactions (the loan and the derivative) can be bundled into a structured note that could have an almost infinite variety of terms. Three possible contractual terms for the structured note that would be purchased by entity X for C900 are shown below:

- Entity X to receive, at the end of the year, C954 plus any excess (minus any shortfall) in the current market price of entity Z’s equity shares over (or under) C200.

- Entity X to receive, at the end of the year, C1,054 plus any excess (minus any shortfall) in the current market price of entity Z’s equity shares over (or under) C300.

- Entity X to receive, at the end of the year, C755 plus any excess (minus any shortfall) in the current market price of entity Z’s equity shares over (or under) C1.

All the above terms of the structured note will provide the same cash flows, given a specified market price of entity Z’s shares. If the market price of entity Z’s shares is C200 at the end of year 1, entity A will receive C954 under each of the three options. Similarly, if the market price of entity Z’s shares increases to C306, entity A will receive C1,060 under each of the options.

As is apparent, the difference in terms under the three options is totally arbitrary, because those differences have no impact on the ultimate cash flows under the structured note. Thus, those differences are not substantive and should have no influence on how the terms of the embedded derivatives are identified. Therefore, the hybrid instrument’s separation into an embedded derivative and a host debt instrument should be the same for all of the three options described for the structured note.

That separation would generally result in the structured note being accounted for as a debt host contract, with an initial carrying amount of C900 and a fixed annual interest rate of 6%, and an embedded forward contract with C200 forward price, which results in an initial fair value of zero.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.84.1"></a>FAQ 41.84.1 – Should the different options in a callable convertible bond be accounted for separately?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.4, IFRS 9 para B4.3.5(e), IAS 32 para 31, IAS 32 para IE37.   
 Reference to standing text: [41.84](62.md#s261-Para_41.84)  
 

  

  
**Question**

An entity issues 20,000 callable convertible bonds at a total par value of C2 million. Each bond pays fixed interest and is convertible, at the holder’s option, at any time up to maturity into the entity’s ordinary shares. Each bond also contains an embedded call option that gives the bond’s issuer the right to call and redeem the bond at any time before maturity. Should the conversion and call options be accounted for separately?

**Answer**

The bond has two embedded options that are held by different parties. The bond’s holder has the option to convert the bond into a specified number of shares. The issuer has the option to call back the bond and pay an amount generally at a premium over par value.

From the issuer’s perspective, the equity conversion option is an equity instrument of the issuer and, therefore, it is outside IFRS 9’s scope. Under IAS 32, the issuer must separate the equity and the liability elements of the compound instrument. As far as the issuer’s call option (the right to call and redeem the bond) is concerned, this must be valued separately (using an option pricing model), because it is distinct in character and risk from the written equity conversion option. This value is then included in the liability component before separating the equity component under IAS 32. The call option will need to be accounted for separately, unless the option exercise price is approximately equal (on each exercise date) to the amortised cost of the host debt instrument or reimburses the lender for the present value of lost interest for the remainder of the host contract.

From the holders’ perspective, the purchased call option (to convert) and the written call (to redeem) will result in the instrument failing the SPPI test, and the entire bond is measured as at fair value through profit or loss.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.84.2"></a>FAQ 41.84.2 – Should embedded forward and option features in a bond be accounted for separately?

 

Reference to standard: IFRS 9 para B4.3.4  
 Reference to standing text: [41.84](62.md#s261-Para_41.84)  
 

  

  
**Question**

An entity issues a debt instrument at par, with a term of five years. The debt is callable three years after the issue. If the debt is called, the holder will receive an amount that is adjusted by the percentage change in the FTSE 100 index, and that amount doubles if the FTSE 100 index exceeds a certain level. The instrument contains two embedded derivatives: a forward contract that pays double if the FTSE 100 index exceeds a certain level; and an option that is linked to changes in the FTSE 100 index. Should the forward and option features be accounted for separately?

**Answer**

It is not appropriate to separate both a forward and an option on the equity index, because those derivative features relate to the same risk exposure. Instead, the forward and option elements are treated as a single, compound embedded derivative.

### <a id="s262-FAQ_41.84.3"></a>FAQ 41.84.3 – Can a put option be closely related to a convertible bond host whose conversion option is an embedded derivative?

 

Reference to standard: IAS 32 para 16; IFRS 9 para B4 3.4  
 Reference to standing text: [41.84](62.md#s261-Para_41.84)  

  

  
**Question**

An entity, with functional currency of HKD, issues a five-year euro-denominated convertible bond for HKD100. The bond is puttable at the holder's option (holder's put option) after two years for the initial amount paid by the investor (that is, the euro amount determined at inception as the equivalent of HKD100). Based on previous IFRIC conclusions, a foreign currency convertible bond such as this is accounted for as a financial liability by the issuer. The conversion option is an embedded derivative which will require separate accounting treatment. Based on an option pricing model, the conversion option has a fair value on initial recognition of HKD20. The amortised cost of the host liability would be HKD87.47 after two years.

Should the put option be viewed as closely related to the host debt instrument under IFRS 9?

**Answer**

No. In cases where the instrument is puttable by the holder and the conversion feature is treated as an embedded derivative, the put option can never be closely related to the debt host, because it involves a put of both the debt host and the derivative conversion feature. Accordingly, the put option and the conversion feature should be treated as a single compound embedded derivative in accordance with paragraph B4.3.4 of IFRS 9 regarding multiple embedded derivatives.
